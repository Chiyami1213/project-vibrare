1Kabuki and Its Music

1.1Offstage and Onstage

Kabuki, the leading theater of the Edo江戸 period with bunraku文楽, is above all drama, but music is essential to it, as are dance and stylized movement (kata型). It was handed down mostly orally and changed constantly; music probably changed more, unintentionally, than any other part Tokita 229–230.

Two Kinds of Music
Kabuki music is of two kinds. Offstage music, from a hidden ensemble, gives background and sound effects; it is fragmentary and cannot stand alone. Onstage music accompanies dance, with the musicians in view. Shamisen and voice are the core of both, and nagauta長唄 dominates both.
(1.1)

Tokita 230. The offstage musicians are, strictly, nagauta musicians Tokita 230.

Onstage, nagauta shares the dance repertory with tokiwazu常磐津, kiyomoto清元 and other genres of the Bungo lineage (bungo-kei jōruri豊後系浄瑠璃), and with gidayū義太夫. These pieces last between 15 and 50 minutes Tokita 230.

G Kabuki music grew from three roots: the nō能 instrumental ensemble (hayashi囃子), to which the shamisen三味線 was added; the popular dance songs of the period (kouta小唄); and medieval narrative (heikyoku平曲, sekkyō説経, jōruri浄瑠璃). Like nō before it, kabuki歌舞伎 was a melting pot Tokita 230.

1.2Through the Periods

Step through the history with the buttons or the arrow keys. Each period left its imprint on the music heard today Tokita 230.

Women’s Kabuki · c. 1600–1629

  • Most histories start with the shrine maiden Okuni阿国, but Tokita stresses continuity with earlier troupes of women entertainers, and today’s kabuki bears little resemblance to Okuni’s shows Tokita 230.
  • Onna kabuki女歌舞伎 centered on dance; the music was basically the nō hayashi, perhaps with bamboo flute and hand gong Tokita 230–231.
  • Malm gives the traditional date, 1596, on the dry bed of the Kamo River in Kyoto, and notes that early kabuki played on nō and kagura stages Malm 239–240.

Young Men’s Kabuki · 1629–1652

  • After women were banned in 1629 came wakashū kabuki若衆歌舞伎, with the more sedate mai舞 style; koto箏 sometimes joined the accompaniment Tokita 231.
  • Theater musicians were permanent employees of one theater and were adding the shamisen by the 1650s Tokita 231.

Men’s Kabuki · 1652–1700s

  • Young men were banned in 1652, giving yarō kabuki野郎歌舞伎. Female impersonators (onnagata女形) led to multi-act plays, with mimetic dance scenes (shosagoto所作事) as a separate stream Tokita 231.
  • In the Genroku元禄 era (1688–1703) kabuki became recognizably modern: drama inspired by bunraku, and narrative dance. Many plays opened with michiyuki道行 entrance songs on the hanamichi花道 ramp Tokita 244.
  • Malm: the term edo nagauta江戸長唄 first appears on a playbill of 1704 Malm 231.

Nagauta Dances · 1750s–1820s

  • The earliest surviving nagauta dances date from the 1750s; the best known is Musume Dōjōji (1753), over fifty minutes long Tokita 244.
  • From the 1780s, quick-change suites (henge buyō変化舞踊) had one actor dance several characters, each scene in a different genre Tokita 244–245.
  • From the 1790s to the 1820s many scenes used two or more genres on stage at once: kakeai掛合 Tokita 245.
  • By 1800 a day held two full plays, each with at least one fully musical dance scene Tokita 231.

Ōzatsuma and Matsubame-mono · 1826–Meiji明治

  • In 1826 the nagauta musician Kineya杵屋 Saburōsuke IV received the right to use the “forty-eight patterns” of the old narrative genre ōzatsuma-bushi大薩摩節 Tokita 245.
  • Adaptations of nō and kyōgen狂言 (matsubame-mono松羽目物), such as Kanjinchō勧進帳 from the nō Ataka安宅, were popular in the Meiji period Tokita 245.

Modern Kabuki · 1900s–

  • From the early 1900s full-length plays gave way to programs of popular highlights; the National Theatre revived full multi-act plays Tokita 237.
  • Audiences know less and less of the conventions, especially musical ones; earphone guides (also in English) explain them Tokita 229, 258.
beyond the main source

Malm describes Edo audiences drawn from every class, with actors more adored than modern film stars, and suggests the theater served as a safety valve in a rigid society Malm 239–240. Tokita compares an Edo theater visit to a sporting event: people ate, drank, talked and showed their approval or disapproval aloud Tokita 237.

2The Offstage Ensemble

2.1The Geza Room

Geza
The offstage ensemble plays from a screened cubicle at the audience’s left of the stage, the geza下座 or kuromisu黒御簾. Its music is heard but not seen: like a film sound track, it includes songs as well as instrumental music and effects, chosen for atmosphere and texture rather than literal meaning. The players are men with nagauta長唄 professional names, chiefly Mochizuki望月 and Tanaka.
(2.1)

Tokita 231. Other names for it: geza ongaku下座音楽, narimono鳴物, kagebayashi陰囃子 Tokita 231.

Malm explains the names: kuromisu, “black curtain”, recalls a black flat once placed in front of the room, and geza, “lower place”, its old position below the second-floor room of the gidayū義太夫 musicians. He places it “downstage right”; Malm gives directions from the actor’s point of view, so this is the same side as Tokita’s audience left Malm 241–242.

G Geza music rarely represents music the characters would actually hear. Its link with the onstage music is mostly in content, not in sound, so an offstage tune can even clash in tonality with the onstage music. One common musical link: the offstage hand gong (kane) doubles the onstage stick drum (shimedaiko締太鼓) Tokita 232.

2.2What Geza Music Signals

The repertory has 800 or more pieces (Kageyama’s estimate), with regional differences between Tokyo and Kyoto–Osaka. Mochizuki classifies it by social setting, from court nobles down to outcastes and the brothel quarter; Malm by weather and season, location, character, and situation Tokita 232, 234.

2.3Drums and Clappers

Signals
The huge barrel drum ōdaiko大太鼓 signals the start of a day’s program. Hardwood blocks, hyōshigi拍子木 (struck together) or tsukeツケ (struck on a board), mark the start and end of a play or act and point up the stylized movements of violent scenes.
(2.3)

Tokita 234.

Malm adds detail. The ōdaiko once hung in a tower over the entrance; the pattern ichibandaiko一番太鼓 (“drum number one”) still sounds an hour before curtain. Its main use is atmosphere: wind and rain are symbolic, not imitative, so the wind pattern may sound in a still night scene to suggest a robber’s cold eye. The ki (hyōshigi) is struck by a stagehand, not a geza player; the tsuke is struck at stage left for fights and running Malm 244–247. Miki notes mallets with yarn balls (yukibai) for snow on the ōdaiko, and that the tsuke also cuts off a mie見得 pose Miki 172, 185.

2.4Tsukechō

Tsukechō
The choice of tunes and patterns for a production is written in notebooks called tsukechō付帳, known from the late seventeenth century. The selection was rewritten for each production season: a written record serving an orally transmitted art.
(2.4)

Tokita 234. Malm: the cue sheets gave only the text where the geza enters and the names of the pieces; notation books were guarded by the guilds Malm 255.

G Tokita warns that the great compendia of geza music present it as fixed, whereas the rewriting of tsukechō shows it is highly flexible, responding to casting and production Tokita 258–259.

3The Shamisen and Edo Culture

3.1Sound and Aesthetic

The shamisen三味線 was tied to the theater and the licensed quarters, which later made it seem disreputable to Meiji明治 modernizers. In the Edo江戸 period it embodied the aesthetic called iki粋 or sui粋 (“cool”, “chic”); its sound is called shibui渋い, bitter-sweet Tokita 236.

Voice and Shamisen
The shamisen plays mostly single lines, not chords, and relates to the voice heterophonically: tsukazu hanarezu付かず離れず, the same melody not quite together in time. This is typical of Edo vocal music for koto箏 or shamisen, narrative or lyric.
(3.1)

Tokita 236; compare Chapter 7, fact 6.2.

GenreShamisen character (Tokita)
Jiuta地歌refined, with understated eroticism
Gidayū義太夫gruff and masculine (yabo野暮): heavy plectrum and strings, thick neck, low tunings
Nagauta長唄lightest instrument; instrumental virtuosity, fast interludes with double stops
Tsugaru-jamisen津軽三味線instrumental folk style; dense and rhythmically complex

Tokita 236.

G Sawariさわり is strongest in the narrative genres (gidayū, tokiwazu常磐津, kiyomoto清元) and in tsugaru-jamisen, weakest in the lyric genres (nagauta, jiuta, kouta小唄) Tokita 236.

3.2Three Necks

Neck Sizes in Kabuki
In kabuki歌舞伎 the vibrating string length is the same in every style, about 62 cm, but nagauta uses the narrow-necked hosozao細棹, tokiwazu and other jōruri浄瑠璃 the medium chūzao中棹, and gidayū the thick futozao太棹. Neck thickness matters less to the sound than plectrum, bridge, strings and skin.
(3.2)

Tokita 236.

3.3Tunings and Pitch

Transcriptions take the first string as b, but there is no absolute pitch. In the Meiji period a numbered pitch system was introduced, with A as ippon no chōshi一本の調子 (“pitch one”) and higher pitches numbered upward by semitones Tokita 237. On how Tokita numbers B, see Where the Sources Differ.

Tunings
The three common tunings, in relative pitch: honchōshi本調子 b–e′–b′, niagari二上り b–f♯′–b′ and sansagari三下がり b–e′–a′. Shifts of tonal center and register, related to but not dependent on tuning changes, give shamisen music variety.
(3.3)

Tokita 237, 254.

4Nagauta

4.1Character and Standing

G Yokomichi Mario横道萬里雄 once remarked that the outstanding feature of nagauta長唄 is that it has none: built by borrowing from other styles, it has no core of its own. Even so, its voice, shamisen三味線, ornaments, formulas and repertory set it clearly apart Tokita 242.

The Leading Genre
Nagauta (“long song”) is the strongest branch of shamisen music: about half of all kabuki歌舞伎 dance scenes are nagauta, the rest shared roughly equally by tokiwazu常磐津 and kiyomoto清元. It has the broadest repertory, a practical notation developed early in the modern period, the most schools and amateurs, and was the first shamisen genre taught on NHK television.
(4.1)

Tokita 242. Kineya杵屋 is the commonest stage name for its onstage singers and players Tokita 243.

Malm: if gidayū義太夫 is king of narrative shamisen music, nagauta rules the lyric side, and it belongs to Tokyo as gidayū belongs to Osaka. The older Kyoto nagauta of jiuta地歌 is now called kamigata nagauta上方長歌; the kabuki genre is edo nagauta江戸長唄 Malm 231.

4.2The Onstage Ensemble

Debayashi
The onstage ensemble (debayashi出囃子) has equal numbers of singers and shamisen, plus at least four hayashi囃子 players: flute, kotsuzumi小鼓, ōtsuzumi大鼓 and shimedaiko締太鼓, of which only the kotsuzumi may be doubled. It is the nō能 hayashi except that the flutist also plays the bamboo takebue竹笛 (shinobue篠笛).
(4.2)

Tokita 243. Malm: drums, singers and shamisen may each increase, usually by three; the two tsuzumi鼓 together are the daishō大小 Malm 231–232.

The drum patterns are of two kinds: hongyō本行, basically the same as in nō, and chirikaraチリカラ (chirikara-byōshiチリカラ拍子), unique to kabuki and named after its mnemonic syllables. The choice of nō flute or takebue depends partly on which kind is played Tokita 243. Malm hears the chirikara patterns supporting both the shamisen rhythm and the choreography Malm 234.

G In nagauta the hayashi sit in front of the singers and players; in tokiwazu and kiyomoto only the offstage musicians add hayashi, mostly in non-narrative sections and the finale Tokita 246–247.

4.3From Kouta to Long Song

Nagauta counts as lyric (utaimono謡物), pure song in principle, reflecting its roots in shamisen kumiuta組歌, suites of short songs without a story. What separates it from a kouta小唄 suite is a narrative sequence, formal sections such as oki置, kudoki口説 and chirashi散らし, and a dancer playing a role Tokita 243–244.

Narrative Influence
G Three steps brought nagauta closer to the narrative genres: henge変化 suites, in which each scene changed genre and so put nagauta on an equal footing (from the 1780s); kakeai掛合, several genres on stage together (1790s–1820s); and the adoption of the ōzatsuma大薩摩 patterns (1826), used for declamation and stage action.
(4.3)

Tokita 244–245. Rokkasen六歌仙 (1831) shows a henge sequence: ōzatsuma and gidayū, kiyomoto, nagauta, nagauta with kiyomoto, ōzatsuma Tokita 245.

Ōzatsuma also survives as a separate onstage style, singer and player standing, in stylized fights and danmariだんまり (“encounter in the dark”) scenes. Nō-based matsubame-mono松羽目物 such as Kanjinchō勧進帳 use nō resources and ōzatsuma patterns to suggest the samurai world Tokita 245. Many nagauta musicians, such as Kineya Seihō, have written new music in traditional idioms (shin-hōgaku新邦楽) Tokita 245.

beyond the main source

Malm notes that from the early nineteenth century concert nagauta (ozashiki) appeared, and that a named composer wrote only the voice and shamisen, then asked the head of the hayashi guild to add drums and flute: a communal composition. Different guilds may thus give one piece different parts Malm 231, 237.

5The Narrative Genres

5.1The Bungo Lineage

Bungo-kei Jōruri
Tokiwazu常磐津 and kiyomoto清元 are cousins in the bungo-kei jōruri豊後系浄瑠璃 (Bungo lineage). Miyakodayū Itchū brought a soft Kyoto style to Edo江戸; his pupil Miyakoji Bungo-no-jō became a popular idol in the 1730s with love-suicide scenes, until he was banned from Edo theaters. His pupils carried on under new names: Tokiwazu Mojitayū (1747), Tomimoto Buzentayū (1748) and, from that line, Kiyomoto Enjudayū (1814).
(5.1)

Tokita 245–246. The other members are itchū-bushi一中節, shinnai-bushi新内節, tomimoto-bushi富本節, miyazono-bushi宮薗節 and shigetayū-bushi繁太夫節 Tokita 246.

Bungo-no-jō’s style was blamed for a wave of love suicides; fans copied his hair and clothes Tokita 246. Malm calls kiyomoto the highest and least percussive of these genres and tokiwazu markedly less violent than gidayū義太夫 (Chapter 7, 3.2) Malm 228–229.

G While nagauta長唄 took on narrative traits, these genres took on nagauta traits: the odoriji踊り地 dance section, hayashi囃子 support, and retuning from honchōshi本調子 to niagari二上り or sansagari三下がり for dance and lyrical sections. They remain more formulaic than nagauta, especially in their “urgent” narrative and their cadences Tokita 246.

5.3Takemoto and Other Genres

Takemoto竹本
Puppet plays adapted for kabuki歌舞伎 (maruhon-mono丸本物) keep the gidayū narrator and shamisen三味線, called takemoto竹本 (or choboチョボ) in kabuki, but the actors speak all the dialogue. Examples: Kanadehon chūshingura仮名手本忠臣蔵, Yoshitsune senbon-zakura義経千本桜, Sugawara denju tenarai kagami菅原伝授手習鑑.
(5.2)

Tokita 256–257. Malm likens the chobo in kabuki to a Greek chorus, setting and commenting on the scene, whereas in bunraku文楽 they drive the drama Malm 241.

Several genres are now “old music” (kokyoku古曲): katō-bushi河東節, itchū-bushi, and the Bungo-lineage shinnai新内, tomimoto富本, shigetayū and miyazono宮薗. Katō-bushi survives in kabuki in one play, Sukeroku助六, where, uniquely, women perform it. Ogie-bushi荻江節 broke away from nagauta. Street shinnai (shinnai-nagashi新内流し) used two shamisen, one playing a high obbligato (uwajōshi上調子) Tokita 257.

Short shamisen songs of the salons, utazawa歌沢, hauta端歌, kouta小唄, dodoitsu都々逸, were absorbed into dance music; kouta are still essential to a geisha’s training Tokita 258.

6.2The Sections

Kabuki Dance Form
Kabuki dance form is a fixed order of sections shared by nagauta, tokiwazu and kiyomoto. Oki置 and chirashi散らし occur once each; there may be several or no kudoki口説 or odoriji踊り地, and the part between entrance and kudoki is the most variable. Each section ends with a cadence in voice and shamisen, an interlude and a change of sub-style, often with a change of tuning.
(6.1)

Tokita 248–249.

6.3Malm’s Six Sections

Malm’s Version
G Malm’s six standard sections, named by late nineteenth-century Japanese scholars: oki (introduces scene or character, declamatory), michiyuki (entrance, usually after a full-ensemble interlude), kudoki (soft and lyrical, voice and shamisen alone, seldom on the same beat), odoriji (lively, often a new tuning, with taiko太鼓 and bamboo flute), chirashi (“scattering”: faster and thicker), and dangire段切れ (the finale and final pose).
(6.2)

Malm 232–236. Some apply jo-ha-kyū序破急: oki and michiyuki as jo序, kudoki and odoriji as ha破, chirashi and dangire as kyū急 Malm 236.

In a chirashi Malm hears the tsuzumi鼓 in eight-beat patterns while taiko and nō flute play a ten-beat pattern, sarashi晒: an out-of-step “sliding door” effect that drives toward the end. The flute–drum unit takes over the function of harmony, adding color and tension Malm 234–236. The final cadences are so familiar that audiences applaud as they begin Malm 236.

6.4Phrases and Codas

Phrase-length formulas are most obvious at cadences and openings; following Yamada’s finding for gidayū義太夫, Tokita expects named patterns to be a small part of the music (Chapter 7, 7.3) Tokita 249–250.

The Narrative Coda
A narrative coda is a long phrase condensing a section of urgent narrative. It bridges two songs or brings back the narrator’s voice after a quotation, to words such as “That was interesting” or “Let’s have a snatch of …”.
(6.3)

Tokita 250. The term is Tokita’s.

7Two Pieces Compared

7.1Echigo-jishi

Echigo-jishi越後獅子 (1811), one scene of a seven-part henge変化 suite, portrays Kakubei, a traveling lion-dance entertainer from Echigo, festive but an outcaste. Its highlight is the cloth-bleaching dance (nunosarashi) with two long strips of white cloth. It is based on the jiuta地歌 piece Sarashi晒 Tokita 250–251.

A Lyric Piece
Echigo-jishi is about 21 minutes with no dialogue: a string of quoted songs in a loose narrative frame. It has no oki置 and no true kudoki口説, but several odoriji踊り地 and virtuosic interludes. The shamisen三味線 stays in sansagari三下がり, the song tuning, though the pitch may be raised for growing brilliance.
(7.1)

Tokita 251, 254.

The section before the folk-song-like hamauta works like a kudoki: drums drop out, tempo slows, and long melismas in a high register carry erotic content; but both are quoted folk songs and lack the usual kudoki formulas Tokita 254.

7.2Seki no to

Seki no to関の扉 (“The Barrier Gate”, 1784), set in the ninth century, brings together the poet Ōtomo Kuronushi, disguised as the barrier guard Sekibei, and the spirit of a black cherry tree disguised as a courtesan who seeks revenge. Tokiwazu常磐津 takes great pains over Sekibei’s rough, drunken masculinity and the courtesan’s laughter and tears Tokita 254.

A Narrative Piece
Seki no to (second part about 50 minutes) is in honchōshi本調子 throughout, as narrative genres typically are. It has long passages of dialogue, many over shamisen, and the dance form is laid over narrative form: the kudoki is play-acting between courtesan and customer, and the chirashi散らし is a violent showdown.
(7.2)

Tokita 254–255.

9Listening and Research Today

9.1Kabuki Music Today

Offstage music cannot live outside kabuki歌舞伎, but the onstage genres thrive in recitals: tokiwazu常磐津 and kiyomoto清元 usually with voice and shamisen三味線 only, nagauta長唄 with onstage hayashi囃子. Some nagauta groups never play for kabuki, seeing that role as subordinate; more than any jōruri浄瑠璃, nagauta is now a purely musical art Tokita 258.

G Tokita calls the earphone guide a mixed blessing, getting in the way of direct listening but perhaps needed by newcomers, and recommends kabuki DVDs with commentary that can be switched off Tokita 258.

9.2Research

Resources
Key resources: Kageyama (1992), the most comprehensive book on kabuki music; Mochizuki’s LP set Kabuki geza ongaku shūsei (1961); Kineya’s staff transcriptions (1976–80); Malm (1978) in English; and Machida’s cross-genre study of senritsukei旋律型 (1982). In the Taishō大正 period Yoshizumi Kojūrō devised a systematic nagauta notation, which inspired Asada Shōtetsu’s for kiyomoto.
(9.1)

Tokita 258–259.

Where the Sources Differ

Errors and disagreements found while cross-reading, grouped by source, with the check that exposed each one. The guide text uses the form named in each check.

Tokita and Hughes 2017

Year the ōzatsuma patterns passed to nagauta長唄. Tokita: 1826, to Kineya杵屋 Saburōsuke IV (p. 245).
Check: The same chapter: “handed” to nagauta in 1827 (p. 259). Not resolved here; the guide uses 1826.
Founding of the National Theatre. Tokita: the National Theatre was established in 1965 (p. 237, n. 4).
Check: Yamada山田: it opened in Tokyo in 1966 (Tokita 200; Chapter 7). The two dates may refer to founding and opening.
Numbering shamisen三味線 pitches. Tokita: A is pitch one, pitches then rise in semitones, “so that B is called pitch two” (p. 237).
Check: Rising by semitones from A, B would be pitch three (A, B♭, B). One of the two statements must be wrong; the guide gives only the principle.

Malm 2000

Genres in Momijigari紅葉狩. Malm: Momijigari uses nagauta, tokiwazu and gidayū on stage (p. 250).
Check: Tokita: Momijigari is a three-genre kakeai of nagauta, kiyomoto and tokiwazu (p. 245). Malm describes one 1956 production; the mix may vary.
Sections of the dance form. Malm: six sections, oki, michiyuki, kudoki, odoriji, chirashi, dangire (p. 232).
Check: Tokita: oki, hanamichi, a variable part, kudoki, monogatari, odoriji, chirashi (p. 249). Not a contradiction but a different list; the guide gives both.
Stage directions. Malm places the geza room downstage right (p. 241).
Check: Tokita places it on the audience’s left (p. 231). Malm uses the actor’s view (p. 241, n. 3), so both mean the same side.
