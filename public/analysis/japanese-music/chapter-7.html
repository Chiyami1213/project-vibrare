The Shamisen and Gidayū-bushi

The three-string lute that became the voice of the Edo period, and its greatest narrative genre: gidayū-bushi, the sung drama of the bunraku puppet theater, where one narrator and one shamisen player carry a whole play. Compiled by cross-reading Tokita and Hughes (chapter by Yamada Chieko) with Malm and Miki.

1The Shamisen

1.1Arrival

Arrival
The shamisen三味線 grew from the Ryukyu琉球 sanshin三線, itself from the Chinese sanxian三弦: three strings, snakeskin, played with a pick. Later sources say it reached the port of Sakai堺 near Osaka around 1562; the earliest document, a diary of 1574, mentions a player with a Ryukyu mission.
(1.1)

Malm 213; Miki 88. Flavin dates its arrival in Kansai to the late sixteenth century (Chapter 6, 7.1).

Japanese players, probably biwa hōshi琵琶法師, changed it: a body of four pieces of wood instead of one, cat or dog skin instead of snake (big snakes are rare in Japan), a large biwa-like plectrum, and the buzzing sawariさわり Malm 213–215 Miki 89–92. G Malm calls it a creative “Japanization”; Miki adds that the ban on Western music during national isolation left the shamisen at the center of Edo江戸 musical culture Malm 214 Miki 88.

1.2Construction and Sawari

Body, Neck and Sawari
The body (dō道) is a frame of four pieces of wood covered front and back with cat or dog skin, with a patch (bachigawa撥皮) where the plectrum strikes. The neck (sao棹) is in three pieces. The first (lowest) string does not rest on the upper bridge, so it buzzes against a shallow cavity: sawari, which also sounds in sympathy with certain higher notes.
(1.2)

Malm 214–215; Miki 89–91; Tokita 204.

G Because the plectrum strikes string and skin together, Miki stresses that the shamisen is a percussion instrument as well as a string instrument: “the body of the shamisen is a taiko太鼓 drum” Miki 92. Malm describes its tone as “sweet-sour” with a drum-like snap and a twangy hum like a jew’s-harp Malm 215. The whole instrument is about one meter long; the strings are silk, sometimes nylon or tetron Miki 91.

Narrative and Lyrical
Shamisen music divides into katarimono語り物, narrative genres (jōruri浄瑠璃: gidayū義太夫, itchū一中, katō河東, miyazono宮薗, tokiwazu, kiyomoto, shinnai新内, plus sekkyō-bushi説経節 and rōkyoku浪曲), and utamono歌物, lyrical genres (jiuta, nagauta, ogie-bushi荻江節, hauta端歌, utazawa歌沢, kouta).
(1.3)

Miki 88; Malm 217. Malm notes that jōruri is a historical term, while katarimono is a later scholars’ word coined to contrast with utamono; in practice the line is often blurred Malm 217.

2Tunings and Techniques

2.1Three Tunings

Tunings
The three basic tunings, written on b: honchōshi本調子 b–e′–b′ (a fourth and a fifth), niagari二上り b–f♯′–b′ (“raise the second”: a fifth and a fourth) and sansagari三下がり b–e′–a′ (“lower the third”: two fourths). There is no fixed pitch: the tuning is set to the singer’s voice.
(2.1)

Malm 216; Miki 93–94; Tokita 204. Writing on b is a convention: the first string is notated as b so that the common notes in first position need no accidentals Miki 93. Malm gives the real pitch of the first string as anything from G to D Malm 216.

G Malm reports a traditional mood theory, honchōshi solemn, niagari gay, sansagari melancholy or serene, but calls its application “nebulous” Malm 216. Miki lists two further traditional tunings, roku-sagari六下がり and ichi-sagari一下がり, and his own nakakari-chōshi (1964), built on an augmented fourth Miki 93. A second, higher shamisen三味線 (uwajōshi上調子) may use a capo Malm 216.

2.2Left Hand

The neck has no frets; a seam marks only a fourth or fifth, so intonation depends on the player’s ear Miki 94–95.

Sliding as Style (Miki)
G Sliding from note to note on one string with one finger is “a vital part of the aesthetics” of the shamisen: a passage played on easily reached positions on adjacent strings is not nearly as shamisen-like.
(2.2)

Miki 95.

2.3Right Hand and Kuchi-jamisen

The plectrum strikes downward; an upstroke is sukui掬い. Jiuta地歌 players strike near the wooden frame for a soft tone that suits the koto箏; gidayū義太夫 players can play tremolo, which other shamisen do badly Miki 99–100.

Syllables for Strokes
Kuchi-jamisen口三味線, the shamisen’s mnemonic syllables, encode string and stroke, not exact pitch. In Miki’s representative set, open strings are don (string 1), ton (string 2) and ten (string 3); a stopped note is tsun on strings 1–2 and chin on string 3; upstrokes and plucks use r- syllables (ron, ren, rin).
(2.3)

Miki 103–104. Syllables differ by school and genre Miki 103.

G Miki describes kokema as a right-hand rhythmic habit that delays the second of two eighth notes Miki 102; in his koto chapter he describes the same kokema as rushing the weak beats (Chapter 6, 4.3). The two descriptions do not agree; see Where the Sources Differ.

3Narrative Shamisen Genres

3.1From Jōruri to Katarimono

Origins of Jōruri
Jōruri浄瑠璃 began in the fifteenth century as unaccompanied narrative, named after Princess Jōruri, heroine of its main story. Under the influence of heike平家 it took the biwa琵琶, then the newly arrived shamisen三味線, and in the early seventeenth century joined puppet shows: ningyō jōruri人形浄瑠璃.
(3.1)

Tokita 198. Malm gives the same story, adding that the tale is of the young Yoshitsune義経 and that sekkyō説経 scripture narrators also took up the shamisen, giving sekkyō jōruri説経浄瑠璃 Malm 217.

Early seventeenth-century ningyō jōruri varied by city: a gentle style in Kyoto, a bombastic heroic style in Edo江戸. After Takemoto竹本 Gidayū義太夫 appeared in Osaka, these styles declined Tokita 198.

3.2A Family of Bushi

G Malm’s rule: a new genre formed whenever a pupil deviated enough from a teacher to split from the guild, and it usually took the founder’s name Malm 220.

Malm tells why naniwabushi was long ignored by scholars: in a 1911 copyright case a court ruled, on expert advice, that it was “not music” Malm 219–220.

3.3Lyrical Genres in Brief

The lyrical side began with songs of restaurants and pleasure quarters, kouta小唄 (“short songs”), gathered into kumiuta組歌; texts are in the Shichiku shoshinshū糸竹初心集 (1664) Malm 229. Kouta developed Kyoto–Osaka and Edo repertoires, was reformed by utazawa歌沢 in the nineteenth century, and is played with the fingertip or a small plectrum on a small shamisen for an intimate tone Malm 230. Nagauta長唄, the lyrical genre of kabuki, is treated in Chapter 8.

4Gidayū-bushi: History

4.1Through the Periods

What Gidayū-bushi Is
Gidayū-bushi義太夫節 is the sung narrative of the puppet theater, “founded” in Osaka in 1684 by Takemoto竹本 Gidayū義太夫. It is a branch of jōruri浄瑠璃, normally performed by one narrator and one shamisen三味線 player. Bunraku文楽 was the name of one puppet theater and now names the whole art.
(4.1)

Tokita 197. Malm adds that bunraku was named after one of the theater owners Malm 224.

Step through the history with the buttons or the arrow keys.

Jōruri Before Gidayū · 15th–17th centuries

  • Unaccompanied jōruri narrative took the biwa琵琶, then the shamisen, and joined puppet shows in the early seventeenth century Tokita 198.
  • Puppetry itself is far older: Ōe Masafusa described wandering puppeteers in the eleventh century Tokita 198.
  • Takemoto Gidayū (1651–1714) learned from the narrators Inoue Harima-no-jō of Osaka and Uji Kaga-no-jō of Kyoto Tokita 198.

Gidayū and Chikamatsu近松 · 1684–1703

  • Gidayū opened his own theater, the Takemoto-za, in Osaka’s Dōtonbori道頓堀 district in 1684, and brought in the playwright Chikamatsu Monzaemon (1653–1724); together they produced more than forty plays Tokita 198.
  • Sonezaki shinjū曾根崎心中 (1703) was the first contemporary play about working people Tokita 198.
  • The plays survive and are still staged, but Gidayū’s own music was not handed down Tokita 198.

Two Theaters · 1703–1740s

  • In 1703 Gidayū’s pupil Toyotake豊竹 Wakatayū (1681–1764) opened the rival Toyotake-za at the east end of Dōtonbori, with Ki no Kaion as its playwright Tokita 198.
  • The rivalry made a golden age and drove experiments with the puppets that led to three manipulators per puppet Tokita 198–199.

The Great Masterpieces · 1740s

  • The Takemoto-za premiered Sugawara denju tenarai kagami菅原伝授手習鑑 (1746), Yoshitsune senbon-zakura義経千本桜 (1747) and Kanadehon chūshingura仮名手本忠臣蔵 (1748) Tokita 199.
  • A quarrel at the first Chūshingura忠臣蔵 sent narrators from each theater to the other, blurring the two house styles (4.2) Tokita 199.

Repertory and Notation · late 18th–early 19th centuries

  • Shamisen players were at first left off the playbills, but their role grew. Tsuruzawa鶴澤 Tomojirō III (d. 1826) devised a tablature using the 48 kana, still used almost unchanged (8.1) Tokita 199.
  • Few new plays were written, especially after the playwright Chikamatsu Hanji died in 1783; performers instead enriched the old ones Tokita 199–200.
  • Fires and decline struck both great theaters Tokita 200.

Bunraku · 1790s–today

  • In the early 1790s Uemura植村 Bunrakuken of Awaji淡路 opened a small theater in Osaka; in 1872 it was named the Bunraku-za, and a rival, the Hikoroku-za, opened in 1884 Tokita 200.
  • Shōchiku松竹 (then Matsutake) took over the Bunraku-za in 1909. A labor dispute split the troupe in 1949; in 1963 it became a state-supported incorporated body and the factions reunited Tokita 200.
  • The National Theatre in Tokyo (1966), a training school (1972) and the National Bunraku Theatre in Osaka (1984) followed; UNESCO listed bunraku in 2003 Tokita 200–201.

4.2Nishi-fū and Higashi-fū

Two House Styles
G By tradition, each theater had a house style: the older Takemoto-za at the west end of Dōtonbori sang nishi-fū西風 (“western style”), plain and strongly narrative; the Toyotake-za at the east end sang higashi-fū東風 (“eastern style”), showier and more musical. After the narrators swapped theaters in 1748, attention turned to the personal style (fū風) of the narrator who first performed each piece.
(4.2)

Tokita 198–199. Yamada山田 presents the two styles as tradition (“said to be”), not as an analyzed difference.

The styles still matter. In a five-act play the third act uses the subdued nishi-fū and the fourth the lyrical higashi-fū, and major patterns have separate third-act and fourth-act forms (5.2) Tokita 202.

beyond the main source

Malm compares the cultural reach of gidayū義太夫 in Osaka to that of a major magazine or newspaper in America: some residents’ speech and thinking were shaped by its songs. He also notes that many kabuki歌舞伎 plays are “humanized” puppet plays Malm 224.

5Stage, Plays and Performers

5.1The Stage and Puppets

In Gidayū’s day one puppeteer held each puppet. Three puppeteers first appear in 1734, and by the end of the eighteenth century the present method was set: the chief puppeteer works the head and right arm, a second the left arm, a third the feet Tokita 201. Malm gives the same division Malm 224–225.

Where the Music Sits
The musicians sit on the yuka床, a dais jutting out from the audience’s right of the stage. A small revolving section in its center swaps one pair of performers for the next with almost no break. Two screened rooms above the dais are used in some scenes, and the hayashi囃子 room at the left of the stage holds offstage effects and mood music.
(5.1)

Tokita 201–202. Malm describes the narrator touching the closed book to his forehead before the dais turns Malm 225.

Puppeteers dress in black with black hoods (kurogo黒衣), black meaning invisibility, though the lead puppeteer may go unhooded in active scenes Tokita 201.

5.2Three Kinds of Play

Jidaimono時代物 (period pieces) are set before the seventeenth century, mostly among samurai; recent events were moved into earlier eras out of deference to the authorities. At least half of the roughly 150 plays in today’s repertory are jidaimono Tokita 202.

DanDramatic roleTypical content
Firstbeginning of the affairceremonial scene at court, palace, shrine or temple

After Yamada’s Table 9.1 Tokita 203.

Scenes Within an Act
Each dan段 except the fifth has several scenes. With three, they are kuchi口, naka中 and kiri切; the kiri (kiriba切場) is the climax, given to senior musicians, while kuchi and naka (together the haba端場) build up to it with shorter, simpler patterns. The first scene of the whole play is the daijo大序, “grand prelude”: shamisen三味線 sonae, a solemn Chinese-style text (jokotoba序詞), the long pattern ō-oroshi, the story, and a closing ō-sanjū大三重.
(5.2)

Tokita 202. Ō-oroshi and ō-sanjū occur only in the daijo Tokita 202.

Sewamono世話物 (contemporary plays) show townspeople (chōnin町人) in three, sometimes two, acts called maki巻 (“scrolls”). They aim at realism and a quicker pace, go straight into the story, and end each maki with sanjū三重, except the last, which ends with the dangiri段切 melody Tokita 203.

Michiyuki (“journey”) scenes follow characters traveling; in a jidaimono one usually falls between the third and fourth acts to reverse the mood, and in a sewamono it is often the lovers’ journey to suicide. Keiji景事 (keigoto景事) scenes are similar but scenic. Both use several narrators and shamisen players and frequent tuning changes, whereas ordinary jōruri浄瑠璃 stays almost always in honchōshi本調子 Tokita 203–204.

5.3Tayū and Shamisen

The Pair
The tayū太夫 narrates and sings every role from a seated position, supported by deep abdominal breathing: a cloth is wound round the waist and a heavy bag of sand and beans (otoshi落とし) is tucked into the kimono. He reads from a hand-written yukahon床本 on a stand. Good shamisen playing is not mechanical accompaniment: it “must breathe” with the tayū.
(5.3)

Tokita 204. Malm: the shamisen player in fact directs the pace of the drama with calls and signals, and teams stay together for years Malm 226.

Every tayū is named Takemoto竹本 or Toyotake豊竹; great names pass to performers by ability, not by inheritance through an iemoto家元 Tokita 204.

FeatureGidayū義太夫 shamisen
Typefutozao太棹 (thick neck); with the tsugaru-jamisen津軽三味線, the largest body and thickest neck
Strings and bridgethickest strings; a very high bridge weighted with lead; players carry up to 30 bridges for different conditions

Tokita 204. On the bridge material the sources differ; see Where the Sources Differ.

G Pitch is relative and set to the tayū’s voice, but register shows rank: the climax of the third act is the lowest, the haba is higher than the kiri, and the daijo, a training piece for the youngest narrator, is the highest Tokita 204–205.

The shamisen also has solo meriyasuめりやす, fixed melodies repeated as often as the stage needs, named after stretchy knit fabric. Other instruments appear occasionally: the kokyū胡弓 fiddle, for example, for pathos as a hero nears death Tokita 204–205.

6Delivery Types

6.1Kotoba, Ji, Iro and Fushi

TermMeaning (Yamada山田)
Kotoba詞spoken dialogue; highly realistic, more so than in nō能
Ji地broadly, anything not kotoba; narrowly, the basic melodic delivery, as opposed to fushi節
Iro色between speech and ji: no fixed pitch, but more rise and fall than kotoba; leads into speech with the shamisen三味線 motive irodome色止め

G Inobe orders these as a ladder of musicality, kotoba – iro – ji-iro – ji – fushi; Yamada doubts it can be generalized so easily, and calls gidayū-bushi義太夫節 one of the least clearly conceptualized Japanese genres Tokita 205. Malm uses three styles, kotoba, ji and the half-sung ji iro地色, and stresses that performance flows constantly between them, unlike the separate dialogue, recitative and aria of opera Malm 226.

6.2Hirano’s Three Deliveries

Hirano’s Model
R Hirano’s three vocal deliveries for narrative music: ginshō吟唱, closest to speech, stylized but without fixed pitch; rōshō朗唱, syllabic, around one focal pitch (the rōshō pitch) and following the word accent; eishō詠唱, the most melodic, with fixed pitch and melisma.
(6.1)

Tokita 214. Yamada uses the model for the phrase, the unit sung in one breath Tokita 214.

DeliveryIn gidayū-bushi (Yamada)
Ginshōkotoba-nori (speech “riding” rhythmic shamisen patterns, near the end of a piece), iro, and the flat delivery of the jokotoba序詞; kotoba itself is realistic acting, not heikyoku平曲-style narration
Intermediatephrases mixing fixed and unfixed pitch, traditionally ji-iro; marked haru (“stretch” the voice: strong and high) or u (ukasu浮かす, “float” the voice)
Rōshōsyllabic narration around one pitch; the shamisen gives one note and falls silent, as in heikyoku. These phrases make gidayū義太夫 a true katarimono語り物
Eishōuncommon; high, for grief, and usually only one phrase before the shamisen moves on

Tokita 216–218.

6.3Voice and Shamisen Together

Hirano’s model fits only where the voice is independent of the shamisen. In much of gidayū the shamisen punctuates each vocal phrase with two or three notes carrying its skeleton; the shamisen evens out the note lengths, so these phrases are more musical than rōshō Tokita 214, 219.

Neither Touching Nor Parting
In tsukazu hanarezu付かず離れず (“neither touching nor parting”), voice and shamisen share practically one melody with a lag in time and pitch: a heterophony. The shamisen defines the vocal line; once the shamisen part is known, the vocal part follows.
(6.2)

Tokita 219–221. The gloss of the term is ours.

How a Scene Builds (Malm)
G Malm’s listening sketch of a scene: a shamisen prelude, a deep hum, then a simple narrative line with short shamisen comments; tension mounts, the shamisen interrupts more often, and the voice moves among speech, song and a mixture of the two.
(6.3)

Malm 225. Malm calls gidayū by many accounts the most difficult Japanese music, demanding stamina and acting Malm 226.

Worked Example 6.4 · Map a Kiriba from Opening to Close constructed

You are listening to the climactic final scene (kiriba切場) of the third act of a jidaimono時代物. Name what you hear at each of the six moments in the table (right), and say where this scene sits in pitch compared with the haba端場 before it.

MomentWhat you hear
Anew narrator and shamisen; same scenery
Bthe narrative starts after the opening
Csyllabic narration on one pitch; shamisen silent
Dvoice and shamisen on one melody, slightly apart
Evoice falls; shamisen lands on an open string
Fthe act ends

Six moments in the third-act kiriba of a jidaimono.

  1. Assumptions. Third-act kiriba: climax, nishi-fū, senior performers, lowest pitch.
  2. Moment A. A = opening okuri送り, sung to the last syllables held over from the previous scene.
  3. Moment B. B = harufushi.
  4. Moments C and D. C = rōshō (one focal pitch, shamisen silent); D = tsukazu hanarezu heterophony.
  5. Moment E. E = a shōdan小段 cadence such as fushiochi節落ち, marked fushi in the text.
  6. Moment F and Pitch. F = dangiri段切; the kiriba sits lower than the haba.
Answer
A: opening okuri. B: harufushi. C: rōshō. D: tsukazu hanarezu. E: a shōdan cadence such as fushiochi. F: the dangiri melody. The third-act kiriba is pitched lower than the haba; only about a tenth of what you hear is stock pattern.
(E6.7)

7Senritsukei

7.1Layers and Patterns

Phrase, Shōdan, Dan
A senritsukei旋律型 (melodic type) is a melody fixed enough to be recognized wherever it appears, named or not. In Yamada’s layered structure, phrases, senritsukei and kotoba詞 form a shōdan小段 (small section), always closed by a descending pattern such as fushiochi節落ち; shōdan form the dan段, the whole piece.
(7.1)

Tokita 206; Table 9.2 after Komoda’s idea of layered structure. Kuchi口, naka中 and kiri切 can be seen as a level in between Tokita 206.

Some shōdan have a character of their own: the opening makura枕, the closing dangiri段切, and the kudoki口説 (plaint), in which the heroine pours out her anguish. Most do not, and these are the basic material of the genre Tokita 206.

7.2Cadences

Cadential Patterns
Patterns ending a shōdan: fushiochi (the commonest), yuriユリ (named by the number of vocal “shakes”, most often mitsuyuri, three), otoshi落とし, oroshi and suete. In all of them the voice descends and the shamisen ends on the open second or open first string, giving a strong close. All except suete are marked in the text simply as fushi節.

7.3Narrative and Borrowed Patterns

In the long makura, with no dialogue to break it, an unnamed semi-cadential pattern acts as punctuation: a short shamisen motive on the open strings with a five-mora vocal line Tokita 210–211.

Basic Narrative Patterns
Basic narrative patterns include harufushi, perhaps the most important pattern, which usually starts the narrative after the opening okuri or sanjū, and nagaji長地 (“long narrative”), at least one 7 + 5 line of syllabic delivery dwelling on the tone a semitone above the open third string. Short ko-okuri (three syllables) and u-okuri (four) are also basic.
(7.3)

Tokita 211–212.

G Borrowed patterns come from older genres (nō能, heikyoku平曲, sekkyō説経), other jōruri浄瑠璃 styles, folk arts and jiuta地歌. They set a mood: heroism with geki外記, edo江戸 or tosa土佐; sadness with bun’ya文弥 or sekkyō; elegance with reizei冷泉. The commonest are tataki for the pathos of separation and hyōgu for refinement Tokita 212–213.

Despite all these names, senritsukei make up only about 10 percent of the music; the rest, the free phrases of section 6, is what Yamada argues research must explain Tokita 213, 227.

8Transmission and Notation

8.1Learning by Ear

Teaching is oral: the student memorizes the text while listening to the teacher, who rarely names the patterns; a sense of the formulas comes with long experience Tokita 221–222.

Shu, Fushishō and the Books
Shamisen notation is shu朱 (“red ink”); vocal notation is fushishō. Both are hand-written beside the text. Shu is a tablature: kana of the irohaいろは show finger positions (kandokoro勘所), with kuchi-jamisen口三味線 where needed; senritsukei旋律型 are not written out. The shamisen三味線 player’s copy is the keiko-bon稽古本 (practice book), but he plays from memory; the narrator’s is the yuka-hon床本.
(8.1)

Tokita 222, 225.

G The vocal marks are vague. Cadences are all marked fushi節; haru (higher and louder) and u (float the voice) are read differently by each narrator and have become expressive rather than pitch marks, while jō上, chū中 and kin still say something about pitch. So the books alone cannot reproduce or analyze the music Tokita 225–226. Malm agrees that the “notation” cannot be sight-read: one word may mean a pitch level, a style or a stock pattern Malm 227.

8.2Research

Research began with printed texts in the Meiji明治 period. Yūda Yoshio based a text on the yukahon床本 of Takemoto竹本 Tsunatayū VIII; Inobe Kiyoshi went further and started from a recording. Machida’s compendium of senritsukei (1982), Motegi’s transcriptions, and Inobe, Gerstle and Malm’s Theater as Music are key resources; Inobe is regarded as the leading scholar of the music Tokita 226–227.

Yamada山田 names the problem: pieces are very long, the repertory is varied, transcription is laborious, and gidayū義太夫 has not always been treated as music, so the free, non-formulaic narrative remains largely unstudied Tokita 227.

9Beyond the Puppet Stage

9.1Su-jōruri and Women Narrators

Su-jōruri
Gidayū-bushi義太夫節 without puppets is su-jōruri素浄瑠璃. Bunraku is for men only, but in su-jōruri women perform: joryū gidayū女流義太夫 or musume gidayū娘義太夫, very popular in the Meiji明治 and Taishō大正 periods and little researched.
(9.1)

Tokita 197.

Malm dates the flourishing of female jōruri浄瑠璃 in small yose寄席 halls to the early nineteenth century, and reports an order from Meiji University forbidding students to spend time with the female tayū太夫; he credits late twentieth-century feminism with their return to city stages Malm 227–228. Amateur puppet theaters such as Awa and Awaji淡路 also use gidayū義太夫 Tokita 198.

beyond the main source

Malm describes a postwar bunraku文楽 Madame Butterfly (Ochō fujin) that opened with “Auld Lang Syne” on an offstage violin and “Sakura” on the shamisen三味線, and compares the spirit of jōruri to Italian opera Malm 228.

9.2The Gidayū Shamisen Today

Miki writes for composers. The futozao太棹 is tuned a perfect fourth or fifth below other shamisen, probably matched to the large voice of Takemoto竹本 Gidayū; players of smaller shamisen find it hard to get a full sound from it Miki 104–105.

G Its thick, nearly right-angled plectrum, heavy weight and high bridge make tremolo effective, unlike on other shamisen; it can play passages that subtly depart from standard tuning, best left to the player; and it has a loud percussive stroke, tataki-bachi叩き撥, in which the plectrum audibly hits the skin Miki 106.

9.3Twentieth-Century Genres

Malm lists two new twentieth-century genres. Tōmeibushi東明節 (1910), created by Hiraoka平岡 Ginshū from katōbushi河東節, was named for Tokyo and Meiji; delicate and slightly Westernized, it is rare today. Yamatogaku大和楽 (1933), by Ōkura Kishichirō大倉喜七郎, mixed older shamisen styles with Western-style singing and a new end-blown flute, the ōkurabue; it did not take root Malm 237–238.

Where the Sources Differ

Errors and disagreements found while cross-reading, grouped by source, with the check that exposed each one. The guide text uses the form named in each check.

Malm 2000

Okuri and sanjū. Malm: sanjū shows the scene is in the same place; okuri, a new place (p. 227).
Check: Yamada山田: okuri when the performers change but not the scenery; sanjū when both change (Tokita 207). This is a direct contradiction; the guide follows Yamada, a specialist in gidayū-bushi.
Bridge of the gidayū shamisen三味線. Malm: a tortoiseshell bridge with lead inserts (p. 226).
Check: Yamada: water buffalo horn with two pieces of lead (Tokita 204); Miki also says horn (p. 91). The guide follows Yamada and Miki.
Number of acts. Malm: plays have three to twelve acts (dan) (p. 226).
Check: Yamada: jidaimono have five dan, sewamono three (sometimes two) maki (Tokita 202–203). Malm may be counting scenes or exceptional plays.
When women’s gidayū flourished. Malm: female jōruri flourished in the early nineteenth century (pp. 227–228).
Check: Yamada: very popular in the Meiji明治 and Taishō大正 periods (Tokita 197). Both may be right for different stages; not resolved here.

Miki 2008

Kokema. Miki, shamisen chapter: kokema delays the second of two eighth notes (p. 102).
Check: Miki, koto箏 chapter: kokema rushes the weak beats (p. 148). The two descriptions do not agree.
Yuan dynasty dates. Miki dates the Yuan period 960–1368 (p. 113).
Check: He gives 1271–1368 elsewhere (p. 88), the usual dates; 960 is the start of the Song.

Tokita and Hughes 2017

When the Bunraku theater was built. Yamada: Bunraku was a theater built in Osaka in the early nineteenth century (p. 197).
Check: The same chapter: Uemura植村 Bunrakuken set it up in the early 1790s, and it was named Bunraku-za in 1872 (p. 200).
