1Two Names, One History

1.1Why Treat Them Together

Every 22 April, at the Osaka temple Shitennōji四天王寺, priests chant and gagaku雅楽 dancers perform together on a stone stage over a pond. This is the Shōryō-e聖霊会, a memorial for Prince Shōtoku聖徳太子 (574–622), and for more than a thousand years it was the standard shape of a large temple or shrine ceremony Tokita 35.

The rite nearly died in the 1860s–80s. The temple’s hereditary musicians had been called to Tokyo for a new imperial ensemble, and Buddhist worship was under government pressure. Local priests and merchants revived it in the 1880s Tokita 35.

One History, Two Genres (Nelson)
G Gagaku and shōmyō声明 share a common origin on the Asian mainland, much of their music theory, a common history and overlapping performance contexts. Today’s split between “Shinto court music” and “Buddhist chant” is largely a product of Meiji明治 policy, which separated Shinto from the “foreign” religion of Buddhism.
(1.1)

Malm makes the same point from the theory side: shōmyō and court music share a basically identical theory, both rooted in Chinese theory Malm 66–67.

1.2Gagaku: The Word and Its Repertoire

The word gagaku is Chinese yayue雅楽, “proper music”, the name of Confucian ritual music in China. In Japan it comes from the Bureau of Music set up in or before 701, the Gagakuryō雅楽寮 (or Utaryō雅楽寮). Older texts simply say gaku楽, “music”; gagaku as a genre name is rare before the late nineteenth century Tokita 36. Malm glosses it as “elegant, correct, or refined music” Malm 97.

Today gagaku means the music and dance of the Music Department of the Imperial Household Agency, in three categories Tokita 36:

The Three Categories of Gagaku
kuniburi no utamai国風歌舞: songs and dances of indigenous origin for imperial and Shinto rites.
kangen管絃 and bugaku舞楽: instrumental music and dance from the mainland.
saibara催馬楽 and rōei朗詠: accompanied vocal music of the Heian平安 court.
(1.2)

The mainland category is split by origin into two classes:

ClassOriginPerformed AsDance Side
Tōgaku唐楽largely Chinese (plus rin’yūgaku林邑楽, said to be Indian or Southeast Asian)kangen and bugakuLeft (samai左舞)
Komagaku高麗楽largely Korean (plus music of Pohai)almost only bugakuRight (umai右舞)

G The symmetry is partly an illusion. Each side has just under 30 dances, but most komagaku dances are smaller, and once the kangen repertoire is counted tōgaku outnumbers komagaku more than five to one Tokita 36, 40.

G Nelson warns against the label “world’s oldest orchestral music”. In kangen the ensemble does look orchestral, but its style is chamber-like. Many vocal genres died out in the wars of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries and were revived later, so today’s music is largely a late-nineteenth-century systematization and not a replica of ancient practice Tokita 36–37. Malm calls it “perhaps the oldest extant orchestral art music” and “a living museum” Malm 97; see Where the Sources Differ.

1.3Shōmyō: The Word and Its Repertoire

Shōmyō (“voice-clear”) first translated the Sanskrit śabda-vidyā, the linguistic study of Sanskrit, one of the five fields of Brahman learning. It came to mean Buddhist ritual chant only from the twelfth century; before that the term was bonbai梵唄, “Sanskrit hymn” Tokita 37.

Shōmyō covers all traditional vocal music of Buddhist ritual, from highly melismatic pieces in broad free rhythm to syllabic sutra chanting at one character per beat. Even the Zen sects, which stress chant less, have a rich sound world.
(1.3)

Three classifications cross each other Tokita 37–38:

  • By sect. Tendai天台 shōmyō is clearly different from Shingon真言, and some sects have sub-schools.
  • By function in the ritual framework, whose basic structure many sects share.
  • By language: Sanskrit, Chinese or Japanese. This shows the age and origin of a piece, and the historical layers inside one rite.

Malm gives a parallel triple: bonsan梵讃 (Sanskrit), kansan漢讃 (Chinese) and wasan和讃 (Japanese) Malm 67.

1.4Who Performs Today

  • The palace Music Department has about thirty men: two directors, three assistant directors, some twenty musicians and four administrators, plus retired consultants and students. Their duties center on private imperial and Shinto rites, visits of foreign dignitaries, and public concerts each spring and autumn. Fewer of them each year come from the ancient families Tokita 37.
  • Groups outside the palace. Tokyo Gakuso東京楽所 and Jūnion-kai (formed in the late 1970s) mix palace musicians with top amateurs. New religions such as Tenri-kyō天理教 keep active ensembles, and new semi-professional groups have many women Tokita 37.
  • Shōmyō on stage. The National Theatre in Tokyo, after commissioning new gagaku works from the 1970s, turned to shōmyō concerts in the early 1980s Tokita 37.

Malm notes that only men perform imperial bugaku, although women may study and perform elsewhere Malm 103 n. 3.

2History

2.1Through the Periods

Step through the periods with the buttons or the arrow keys. Period dates follow Nelson’s chapter.

Before and During Nara奈良 · to 794

  • Buddhist chant came with Buddhism in the mid-sixth century via the Korean kingdoms. Early rites read sutras to protect the state or performed group repentance; the Shunie修二会 (Omizutoriお水取り) at Tōdaiji東大寺 has run every year since 752 Tokita 38.
  • Gigaku伎楽, a masked pantomime with flute, drums and gong, arrived from Korea in the early seventh century, flourished, then died out, leaving masks Tokita 39. Malm dates it to 612, brought by Mimashi味摩之 of Kudara百済 Malm 98.
  • Missions to the Sui and Tang courts brought musicians and priests. The Bureau of Music existed by 701; Nara temples and Shitennōji四天王寺 had their own music departments Tokita 39.
  • 752: the “eye-opening” of the Great Buddha at Tōdaiji. Over ten thousand priests chanted, including the four standard pieces Bai唄, Sange散華, Bonnon梵音 and Shakujō錫杖, followed by native and foreign dances, “a microcosm of the performing arts of eighth-century Asia” Tokita 39 Malm 97.
  • The Shōsōin正倉院 storehouse keeps masks, costumes and instruments, and the Tenpyō Biwa-fu天平琵琶譜, six lines of biwa琵琶 notation on the back of a receipt dated 747 Tokita 39.

Heian平安 · 794–1192

  • Japanization. Official contact with China ended in the ninth century; gagaku雅楽 and shōmyō声明 grew together as the court’s contemporary music, peaking from about the twelfth century Tokita 39.
  • Esoteric Buddhism. Kūkai空海 (774–835) brought Shingon真言 and its core chant, Sanskrit bongo no san梵語讃 and Chinese kango no san漢語讃. For the Tendai天台 school, Saichō’s disciple Ennin円仁 (794–864) brought many pieces and was the first to compose shōmyō in Japanese Tokita 40.
  • Left and Right. Tōgaku唐楽 and komagaku高麗楽 were reorganized; Japanese musicians composed new pieces for both. A Folk Music Office (Ōuta-dokoro大歌所) kept indigenous songs; saibara催馬楽 appeared Tokita 40–41.
  • Notation and court life. The first official notation collection, the flute score Shinsen Ōjō-fu新撰横笛譜, was finished in 920. Shiika-kangen詩歌管絃, poetry plus pipes and strings, was the basic skill of anyone in office Tokita 42.
  • Pure Land. Genshin源信 (942–1017) and Yōkan永観 (1033–1111) made the narrative kōshiki講式 popular, built from fixed formulas, kyokusetsu曲節 Tokita 43–44.
  • The Insei century. Hereditary music houses (gakke楽家) formed; Ryōnin良忍 founded the Tendai Ōhara大原 center in 1109. Emperor Go-Shirakawa後白河 and Fujiwara no Moronaga藤原師長, the period’s two great musicians, both died in 1192 Tokita 44.

Kamakura鎌倉 and Nanboku-chō南北朝 · 1192–1392

  • A period of consolidation for gagaku: nobles kept the string and vocal lineages, the gakke the winds, percussion and dance. Koma no Chikazane狛近真 finished the first major treatise, Kyōkunshō教訓抄, in 1233 Tokita 45.
  • Shōmyō at its richest. Jōkei貞慶 and Kōben高弁 (Myōe明恵) widened the topics of kōshiki; Kakui覚意 developed a notation that fixed pitch even in long melismas Tokita 45–46.
  • By the end of the fourteenth century the repertoire of the older schools was largely complete, and it shaped heike-biwa平家琵琶, sōga早歌 and the vocal music of nō能 Tokita 46.

Muromachi室町 · 1392–1568

  • The Ōnin応仁 war (1467–77) nearly destroyed court culture. Saibara died out; rōei朗詠 shrank to a handful of pieces. Gagaku survived through religious centers outside the capital Tokita 46.
  • Toyohara no Muneaki豊原統秋 wrote the Taigenshō體源鈔 in 1512 Tokita 46 Malm 101.
  • At Mt Koya高野山 a Shōmyō-shū声明集 was printed in 1472, perhaps the world’s oldest surviving printed music, one year older than the Constance Gradual (1473) Tokita 46.

Momoyama桃山 and Edo江戸 · 1568–1868

  • Peace and the Tokugawa shogunate made regular rites possible. Sects were reorganized and printed chant books spread Tokita 47.
  • Musicians from Osaka and Nara reinforced Kyoto’s court players (the sanbō-gakunin三方楽人 system); the shogunate set up ensembles in Edo and Nikkō日光 Tokita 47.
  • Abe no Suehisa安倍季尚 finished the encyclopedia Gakkaroku楽家録 in 1690. Lost genres such as azuma-asobi東遊 (1813) and kume-mai久米舞 (1818) were revived; most of the kuniburi no utamai国風歌舞 are revivals of this time Tokita 47.
  • Unlike shōmyō, gagaku never had enough practitioners for printed notation Tokita 47–48.

Meiji明治 to the Present · 1868–

  • Laws separating Buddhism and Shinto led to the destruction of many temples, and shōmyō was heard less Tokita 48.
  • The palace Music Department began in Tokyo in 1870; the standard part scores Meiji Sentei-fu明治撰定譜 (1876, 1888) presented a “sanitized and sanctified” tradition as unchanged since antiquity Tokita 48.
  • Gagaku served state propaganda in the colonies; several families ended when their young men died in the war. New pieces were seen as an affront until the 1960s Tokita 48.
  • Today the National Theatre, new groups and younger players are widening both arts Tokita 48. Malm notes world tours and groups outside Japan Malm 102.

2.3Assimilation and the “Reforms” Question

In the ninth century the many imported styles were folded into two classes, and both ensembles shrank. The tōgaku ensemble became smaller than its Chinese model; the Korean ensembles lost their harps and zithers, keeping only the transverse flute Tokita 40–41.

Two Accounts of the Ninth Century
G Nelson: modern research often credits a “reform of the music system” (gakusei kaikaku楽制改革) over fifty years from Emperor Ninmyō仁明天皇 (r. 833–50), but no contemporary record mentions such a policy. It may be wiser to assume that awkward instruments simply died out.
G Malm: the retired emperor Saga嵯峨天皇 and a group of nobles spent much of 833–50 bringing order to the repertoire, creating the standard orchestra and the Left/Right classes, much as the Mannheim school standardized the Western orchestra.
(2.1)

Sources: Tokita 41 Malm 99–100. Nelson also explains the two-class split by court custom: contests between two teams (archery, wrestling, horse racing) and Buddhist rites that paired two performing groups Tokita 40–41. Malm points to Confucian ideals of symmetry and notes that pieces were moved and new ones written to balance the sides, which cost komagaku much of its Korean flavor Malm 100.

3Instruments of Gagaku

3.1Winds (Fukimono)

The instruments fall into three classes: fukimono吹物, hikimono弾物 and uchimono打物 Tokita 49.

InstrumentWhat It IsUse
kagurabue神楽笛transverse flute, 6 holesShinto songs and dances
ryūteki龍笛transverse flute, 7 holes, the largesttōgaku唐楽 (Left)
komabue高麗笛transverse flute, 6 holes, the smallestkomagaku高麗楽 (Right)

Holes and use from Tokita 49 Malm 109–110. Malm adds that the brocade on the closed end is red for the ryūteki and kagurabue and green for the komabue, the colors of their dances Malm 110.

Hichiriki. It carries the core melody and leads the ensemble through the heterophony Miki 59. Its tone is nasal like an oboe but broader, thanks to its large reed and loose embouchure, which allows the microtonal inflections called enbai塩梅 Malm 108–109 Miki 56–57. Its range is only a little over an octave Miki 57. Sei Shōnagon清少納言 hated it (“like the noisy crickets of autumn”); other tales credit it with moving a robber to return his loot Malm 107–108.

Ryūteki. Modeled on a Tang flute; its usable range starts at kan甲 (E5 in Miki’s chart) and its top register is piercing. In classical gagaku it plays without vibrato and, as Miki puts it, “performers always play at mezzo forte” Miki 16, 18–19.

The Shō
The shō plays aitake合竹, chords of up to six pitches. It sounds on both exhaling and inhaling (kigae気替え), changes chords by moving fingers one by one (te-utsuri手移り), and each one-breath chord swells from soft to loud. Its reeds must be kept dry over a brazier or heater.
(3.1)

Sources: Tokita 49 Malm 110–112 Miki 64–68. The chord names come from their lowest pipes, and students sing the hichiriki melody to those names Malm 111. Miki adds that the left index and middle fingers always add shichi and gyō (B and A in his chart) in classical chords, and that the instrument was reshaped in Japan by the mid-Heian period Miki 67–68.

3.2Strings (Hikimono)

  • Wagon和琴 (yamato-goto大和琴): a six-string long zither in zigzag pentatonic tunings. Often called native, it is probably based on an ancient Korean model Tokita 49.
  • Biwa琵琶 (gakubiwa楽琵琶): a large four-string, four-fret lute played with a plectrum. It marks time with arpeggios that may end in a two- or three-note fragment; strings are pressed only on the frets Tokita 49 Malm 106.
  • Koto (sō箏, gakusō楽箏): a 13-string zither tuned pentatonically, played with picks on the thumb and two fingers. Unlike the later popular koto箏, its strings are never pressed behind the bridges. It plays a few set patterns, mainly shizugaki静掻 and hayagaki早掻, which mark off sections more than they make melody Tokita 49 Malm 105.

The Shōsōin正倉院 keeps treasures that left no descendants: a five-string biwa inlaid with a camel and palms, parts of a harp (kugo箜篌), and a Chinese qin琴 Malm 106.

3.5Notation and Shōga

Tablature and Shōga
Gagaku part notations are tablatures: they show fingerings, strings or frets, not pitches. Flute and hichiriki parts carry shōga唱歌, a sung version of the melody in which consonants show phrasing and technique and vowels show pitch relations. Players sing the shōga, often for a long time, before touching the instrument.
(3.3)
  • Large Heian平安 notation collections for flute, biwa and koto survive, but the professional families behind today’s palace practice stress oral transmission and treat notation as a memory aid Tokita 52.
  • Shō notation gives one sign per chord, now read as the aitake name; biwa notation names open strings and fret positions; koto notation names strings. The scores read right to left Tokita 52.
  • Malm adds that the habit of singing the parts gave rise to a vocal genre: court imayō今様 set new poems to melodies such as Etenraku越天楽 Malm 100–101, 109.

4Mode and Melody in Gagaku

4.1The Modes in Practice

Chapter 1 introduced the twelve tone names and the six gagaku雅楽 modes. Nelson shows what a mode means in practice using Goshōraku no Kyū五常楽急, in hyōjō平調 Tokita 55:

One Mode, Two Versions
In hyōjō the strings and shō笙 keep largely to dorian on E, but the flute and especially the hichiriki篳篥 often lower F♯, G, C♯ and D by a semitone or more. Only the first, fourth and fifth degrees (E, A, B) stay stable and uninflected, so dissonances fall on the other degrees.
(4.1)

G Similar modal complexity runs through all tōgaku唐楽 modes, most of all ichikotsuchō壱越調, which shows several layers of historical change Tokita 55.

4.2Watashimono: Crossing Over

Watashimono
When a gagaku piece is moved to another mode it becomes a watashimono渡物: the melody itself changes, so it is a new composition, a paraphrase of its parent. Pieces move only within their class (ryo呂 to ryo, ritsu律 to ritsu).
(4.2)

Sources: Malm 115 Tokita 55. Etenraku越天楽 exists in all three ritsu modes. The shō part is simply transposed, but the hichiriki and ryūteki龍笛 melodies have each grown the traits of their mode Tokita 55. Malm explains the limits by the instruments: they cannot easily play the extra chromatic tones; pieces originally in taishikichō太食調 are never moved Malm 115.

Five-tone cores of the three ritsu modes, labeled with the degree names (see 6.2). These show the frames only; the real melodies differ in each mode.

4.3Where Is the Melody?

Three answers compete Tokita 59–62:

  • Many Japanese performers and scholars: the melody is the detailed line of the flute and hichiriki. Malm’s account agrees: the winds carry the melody, the shō gives a harmonic matrix, and the strings and drums mark time Malm 106, 110–112.
  • Garfias (1975): an abstract melody that follows the overall contour but leaves out each instrument’s style.
  • Picken and his Cambridge students (Wolpert, Marett, Markham):
Ancient Melody in Slow Motion
R The Cambridge view (Picken): the ancient Tang melodies survive in the modern shō and biwa琵琶 parts, slowed by a factor of four or eight, like a cantus firmus. Today’s texture is layers of accretion: unwritten notes added to the shō chords and biwa arpeggios, and old ornaments in the flute and hichiriki that have become the melodic surface, with modal changes to suit later tastes.
(4.3)

For Goshōraku no Kyū, the shō line read at a quarter of its note values, the modern biwa line read the same way, and the biwa line in the twelfth-century Sango Yōroku三五要録 are almost identical, and all fit dorian on E Tokita 60. It follows that the original tempo was faster Tokita 60.

G Nelson’s assessment. Japanese critics focus on philological errors and may miss the broader argument; Markham’s saibara催馬楽 work hardly mentions the genre’s extinction and revival and should be tested against vocal notations from the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries. Still, “change as a result of gradual accretions” is far more persuasive than the idea that tōgaku never changed, and explains the parts better than Garfias’s abstraction Tokita 61–62.

5Rhythm and Form in Gagaku

5.1Hyōshi: The Rhythmic Cycle

Fixed-rhythm tōgaku唐楽 is built from repeated rhythmic cycles Tokita 55, 58:

The Metrical Formula
R A piece’s meter is named by the formula X-Y-hyōshi拍子 Z: X = the number and length of beats in a bar, Y = bars per cycle, Z = cycles in the piece. The closing hyōshi is the strong taiko太鼓 beat, once per cycle, written at the cycle’s end. The last few cycles usually use a busier pattern, kuwae-byōshi加拍子.
(5.1)

Goshōraku no Kyū五常楽急 is haya-yahyōshi hyōshi-hachi, “quick eight beats, [taiko] beats eight”: eight bars of 4/4 per cycle, eight cycles, with kuwae-byōshi in the last two Tokita 58. Because the strong beat falls on the first beat of the fifth bar of each written cycle, the strong beats land on the first beats of odd-numbered bars of the melody and reinforce its two-bar phrasing Tokita 58.

Malm gives the simpler classes from Chapter 1: nobebyōshi延拍子 (eight-beat units, slow), hayabyōshi早拍子 (four-beat), tadabyōshi只拍子 (two and four mixed) and yatarabyōshi夜多羅拍子 (two and three, for fast dance) Malm 115. Nelson confirms the last two: tadabyōshi pieces alternate 2/4 and 4/4, and when danced as Right pieces they drop the last beat of the 4/4 bar, giving yatara-byōshi夜多羅拍子 Tokita 58–59 n. 3.

5.2Jo-Ha-Kyū and Piece Size

Goshōraku五常楽 (“Music of the Five Virtues”) is said to be from China in the 627–49 era and was first played in Japan in 702 Tokita 52. Its full four-movement form is the “ideal” gagaku雅楽 structure:

MovementMeaningCharacter
Jo序introductionlong, slow, free rhythm
Ei詠songonce sung to a Chinese text, now lost
Ha破breaking awaylong, slightly faster, fixed rhythm
Kyū急rushingshorter and faster

Tokita 52. Malm agrees that some large pieces, such as Goshōraku, insert an ei between ha and kyū Malm 116. Today only the last movement is usually played Tokita 52.

Tōgaku pieces come in three sizes: taikyoku大曲 (large; long suites, rarely played whole), chūkyoku中曲 (middle) and shōkyoku小曲 (small). The movements of Goshōraku are chūkyoku except the last, a shōkyoku.
(5.2)

Tokita 55. Malm adds that large and medium pieces usually follow the three-part jo-ha-kyū序破急 form while small pieces tend to have only ha and kyū; today several pieces are often combined to make a jo-ha-kyū set Malm 116.

5.3A Kangen Performance

  1. Netori音取. A short prelude that sets the mode: shō笙, then hichiriki篳篥, flute and kakko羯鼓, then short phrases on biwa琵琶 and koto; Malm calls it a refined version of tuning up Malm 116.
  2. Staggered entries. Solo ryūteki龍笛; percussion from bar 3; other winds at the first strong taiko beat (bar 5); strings two bars apart: lead biwa bar 7, lead koto bar 9, second biwa bar 11, second koto bar 13 Tokita 58.
  3. Tutti with no change of instrumental color, but a gradual accelerando to a peak somewhat before the end, near the switch to kuwae-byōshi. Final beats of even bars stretch while the shō changes fingers and the biwa arpeggiates Tokita 58 Malm 117.
  4. Tomede止手. Only the leaders play a free-rhythm cadence; the shō chord thins, and the strings end with single notes Tokita 58 Malm 117.
As bugaku舞楽, the same piece is faster and steadier for the dancers: a stricter 2/2 in place of a free 4/4, shō chord changes made all at once half a beat earlier, phrases played in one breath, and the whole AABB form repeated five times; the accelerando remains.
(5.3)

Tokita 58–59. In one 1999 bugaku performance the tempo rose from 20 to 56 in twelve minutes Tokita 59 n. 4.

6Shōmyō

6.1Shōmyō in Its Ritual

Ceremony and Rite
A hōe法会 is a ceremony (its purpose); a hōyō法要 is a rite inside it (its content). A rite is led by the master, the dōshi導師, at the altar; the priests (shikishū職衆) sing following his signals on ritual instruments (narashimono鳴物).
(6.1)

Rites are classed several ways Tokita 62–63:

  • Esoteric or exoteric, depending on whether the dōshi performs an esoteric ritual at the altar; esoteric rites focus on the Diamond or the Womb Realm mandala.
  • By the four standard pieces Bai唄, Sange散華, Bonnon梵音 and Shakujō錫杖: ikka hōyō一箇法要 (Bai only), nika hōyō二箇法要 (Bai and Sange, mostly esoteric) and shika hōyō四箇法要 (all four, exoteric only).
  • By the central element: a sutra reading, a kōshiki講式, or mantras.

Shingon真言 uses more than twenty narashimono; six in one rite are the hanshō半鐘 bell, the small inkin bell, cymbals and gong (hachi鈸, nyō鐃), the struck plate kei磬, and the gokorei五鈷鈴 handbell Tokita 64. Rites are built symmetrically or cyclically around their center: in Nelson’s example the opening and closing invocations and hymns mirror each other Tokita 64–65.

beyond the main source

Malm at a Tendai天台 morning service (Asakusa浅草, 1956). A large bell called the priests; a hidden drum accompanied the procession. A solo cantor began very slowly; then the group joined, each priest at his own pitch. In the gyōdō行道 procession the cantor sang a Sanskrit bai while the others chanted in Chinese, like a cantus firmus. The service ended with wasan和讃 sung in steady eight-beat phrases over a drum Malm 70–72.

6.2Pitch, Mode and Range

Shōmyō声明 theory, largely shared with gagaku雅楽, knew that the twelve tones could combine with the modal types (ryo呂, chūkyoku中曲, ritsu律), but each sect used far fewer modes Tokita 64, 66.

The Shōmyō Modes
R Shingon shōmyō uses five modes: ichikotsuchō壱越調 (ryo on D), hyōjō平調 (ritsu on E), sōjō双調 (ryo on G), ōshikichō黄鐘調 (chūkyoku on A) and banshikichō盤渉調 (ritsu on B). Tendai adds shimomu下無 (F) and, rarely, kamimu上無 (C).
(6.2)

Malm states the same rule from the Buddhist theory books: ryo scales start only on D or G, ritsu on E or B, and the mixed scale only on A Malm 69. Each scale has five main degrees, kyū宮, shō商, kaku角, chi徴 and u羽, plus two changing tones marked ei詠 (raised) and hen変 (lowered) Malm 67–68.

  • Modulation. Tendai theory allows three kinds, with the final moving a minor third (ryo ichikotsuchō to ritsu banshikichō), a major second (to ritsu hyōjō) or a perfect fourth (to ritsu ōshikichō). Shingon uses only the first, which Nelson likens to moving between a major key and its relative minor Tokita 66.
  • Range. An octave is a jū重 (“layer”). Three layers, shojū初重, nijū二重 and sanjū三重, give a theoretical three octaves, but most pieces stay within two around the middle nijū Tokita 66.
  • Ornament by degree. Some figures belong to particular degrees: only the final and the fifth take the common yuriユリ, a move away from a tone and back Tokita 66. Malm calls yuri (“swinging”) the most common ornament Malm 69.
  • Theory and practice. Centuries of oral transmission opened a large gap. Tendai cares about pitch; most Shingon pieces are sung with little regard to the stipulated pitches, yet Shingon Buzan豊山 kōshiki stays closer to the medieval notation than Tendai’s Tokita 66.

6.3Language and Musical Style

Four Kinds of Text
Text language shapes style.
Sanskrit (via Chinese sounds): meaning matters little, so melodies tend to be melismatic.
Chinese: the largest class, from melismatic free-rhythm hymns to syllabic sutra chanting, one character per beat.
“Hard” Japanese (Chinese texts read in Japanese order): explanatory and narrative solos, syllabic with light ornament at phrase ends.
“Soft” Japanese (vernacular, often 7-5 syllable lines): songs of praise, often melismatic in part.
(6.3)

Sources: Tokita 67–68. Genres in “hard” Japanese include hyōbyaku表白, jinbun神分, saimon祭文, rongi論義 and kōshiki; in “soft” Japanese, sandan讃歎, wasan and kyōke教化 Tokita 41–42, 67–68.

Two contrasting examples. The Sanskrit hymn Shichi no Bongo四智梵語 is melismatic and free; in both the Tendai and Shingon versions many short melodic fragments (senritsukei旋律型) carry names, apparently a fairly recent development Tokita 68–71. The narrative Nehan Kōshiki涅槃講式 (Kōben高弁, 1215) is syllabic and built from formulas Tokita 71:

Kyokusetsu
R In kōshiki, narration runs through kyokusetsu曲節. Each formula recites on two central tones a whole tone or a fourth apart, reflecting Japanese pitch accent, and ends with a falling cadence that leads to the next. In Shingon Buzan practice they cycle: shojū → kō-nijū甲二重 → (via chūon中音) down to shojū or up to sanjū, and from sanjū only back to kō-nijū.
(6.4)

G This formula structure was taken up and elaborated in later narrative genres such as heike-biwa平家琵琶 (Chapter 3) Tokita 44.

6.4Notation: Hakase

Shōmyō is written in neume-like lines called hakase博士. Names vary by sect, so Nelson proposes three neutral classes by how pitch is shown Tokita 72–74:

ClassHow Pitch Is ShownTraditional Names
Approximatelines suggest up and down movement; no system. Earliest from the eleventh century; most gagaku vocal notation is this typetada-bakase只博士, ko-hakase古博士, kari-bakase仮博士, meyasu-bakase目安博士
Graphthe starting pitch of each character is fixed by where and at what angle the line leaves it; from the late twelfth centuryzu-hakase図博士
Five-tonethe five tone names are tied to line directions 45 degrees apart, so even long melismas read exactly; Kakui覚意, late thirteenth centurygoin-bakase五音博士

Both early types often added helper signs: tone names or flute and koto tablature Tokita 74. Malm notes that shōmyō notation looks indefinite yet is quite specific, as the unison chorus shows Malm 70.

6.5Buddhist Song Outside the Temple

beyond the main source

Malm traces Buddhist music into popular culture Malm 75–77:

  • Preaching songs. Wandering nuns sang the life of the Buddha with picture scrolls (sekkyō説経, utazekkyō歌説経), one root of later secular narrative music.
  • Wasan simplified for townspeople fed into imayō今様; rural hymns entered folk song, some still sung with melismas like chant.
  • Saimon turned into satirical songs by wandering yamabushi山伏 and, with the shamisen三味線, into utazaimon歌祭文, a forerunner of jōruri浄瑠璃.
  • Goeika御詠歌 pilgrim hymns helped create the ondo音頭 type of folk song, call and response, used for bon odori盆踊り dances at the summer festival of the dead.
  • Nenbutsu odori念仏踊り, dances from the invocation of the Buddha’s name; a popular form is said to be the first dance of kabuki’s founder.

Percussion: the wooden fish-mouthed mokugyo木魚 keeps an ostinato for chanting, the Nichiren日蓮 sect’s fan drum uchiwa-daiko団扇太鼓 drives its prayers, and the great temple bell bonshō梵鐘 marks the hours and the New Year Malm 72–74.

7.1Change Through Accretion

How Gagaku and Shōmyō Changed (Nelson)
G Both traditions changed in the same direction over the centuries: more ornamentation, with short named melodic patterns; slower tempos; and modal change, with tones other than the final, fourth and fifth flattened.
(7.1)

Tokita 62, 75. This matches the hyōjō平調 evidence in 4.1: stable E, A and B; lowered other degrees in the winds.

7.2Gaps in the Field

  • Gagaku雅楽: little research on komagaku高麗楽, rōei朗詠 and the kuniburi no utamai国風歌舞, especially mikagura御神楽. Many historical sources lack facsimiles, typographical editions, translations or even good Japanese notes; the language skills needed take years Tokita 62.
  • Shōmyō声明: the biggest obstacle is sectarian boundaries and their clashing terms. Some rites (Tōdaiji’s Shunie修二会) are studied exhaustively, others hardly at all; musical analysis of current practice is rudimentary Tokita 75.
  • Access: few notation facsimiles are published, often too small to read, and outsiders may find doors closed in a conservative part of society Tokita 76.

G Malm adds a hopeful note: through tours and groups abroad, gagaku “will survive and change”, and by the principle of marginal survival it might one day be heard best far from home Malm 102.

Where the Sources Differ

Errors and disagreements found while cross-reading, grouped by source, with the check that exposed each one. The guide text uses the form named in each check.

Tokita & Hughes 2017 (Nelson)

Two endings for Heian平安. Nelson dates Heian 794–1192 and Kamakura鎌倉 from 1192 (pp. 39, 45); the book’s own Table 1.1 gives 794–1185/92.
Check: Compared with Chapter 1’s table. Both dates are in use; the guide keeps Nelson’s within this chapter.
Muromachi室町 dates. Nelson: Muromachi 1392–1568 and Momoyama桃山/Edo江戸 from 1568 (pp. 46–47); Table 1.1: Muromachi 1333–1573.
Check: Nelson counts Nanboku-chō南北朝 (1333–92) separately and ends Muromachi at Nobunaga’s entry into Kyoto. The guide notes both.

Malm 2000

Which scale is “major-type”. Malm: the kaku degree is a half step lower in ryo than in ritsu, so “one might call the ritsu a major-type scale and the ryo a minor-type” (p. 69).
Check: With kaku lower in ryo (D E F♯ A B against D E G A B), ryo has the major third. Nelson likens Shingon’s ryo-to-ritsu modulation to major and relative minor (Tokita 66). The guide treats ryo as the major-like type.
The ninth-century reforms. Malm presents reforms by the retired emperor Saga嵯峨天皇 in 833–50 as fact (pp. 99–100).
Check: Nelson notes no contemporary record of such a policy and dates the supposed reforms from Emperor Ninmyō’s reign (Tokita 41). Shown as two accounts in 2.3.
Shōsōin正倉院 instruments. Malm: forty-five instruments survive in the Shōsōin (p. 102).
Check: Nelson: about 75 examples of 18 types (Tokita 39). The counts may differ in what they include; not resolved here.
Where the melody lies. Malm: the winds carry the main melody; the shō is a harmonic matrix and the strings mark time (pp. 106, 110–112).
Check: Nelson reports the Cambridge evidence that the ancient melody sits in the shō and biwa琵琶 (Tokita 60). The guide shows both views in 4.3.
String entries. Malm: first biwa arpeggio on the last beat of bar 8, gakusō at bar 13; winds after the taiko accent at bar 7 (p. 117).
Check: Nelson, for Goshōraku no Kyū: winds at bar 5, biwa at bar 7, koto箏 at bar 9, second biwa 11, second koto 13 (Tokita 58). Malm describes a general case and Nelson one piece; the lab follows Nelson.

Miki 2008

Dynamics in gagaku雅楽. Miki: “Gagaku is devoid of any dynamic variation” and players “always play at mezzo forte” (p. 19).
Check: Miki himself says the shō swells from p within each breath (p. 66), and Malm describes each shō chord rising to a crescendo (p. 111). The statement applies to the flute part.
