1Ryukyu: Land and History

1.1Four Island Groups

The Islands
At its height the kingdom of Ryukyu琉球 covered four island groups: Amami奄美, Okinawa沖縄, Miyako宮古 and Yaeyama八重山. Each has its own musical identity, yet they share enough language and culture to be treated as one sub-sphere of Japanese culture. Amami has belonged to Kagoshima鹿児島 since early Meiji明治; the other three form Okinawa Prefecture.
(1.1)

Tokita 303.

G Linguists think a “proto-Japanese” split around the third or fourth century into Ryukyuan and mainland dialects, and Okinawan religion is still ancestor worship and animism led by women, as on the mainland before Buddhism. Scholars from Arai Hakuseki新井白石 to Yanagita Kunio柳田國男 stressed sameness with Japan for political reasons; today the stress has swung to Okinawa’s distinctiveness Tokita 304.

Ryukyuan music divides into folk and classical. Unlike elsewhere in Japan, the folk tradition is still creative, and Ryukyu has its own court classical music, developed by the aristocracy of the capital Shuri首里 Tokita 304.

1.2A Short History

Step through the history with the buttons or the arrow keys.

Chūzan中山 and Ming China · late 14th–15th c.

  • The principality of Chūzan, in central Okinawa Island, opened diplomatic relations with the new Ming dynasty in the late fourteenth century; Ryukyu then kept a semi-autonomous status for five centuries Tokita 303.
  • Fujianese immigrants settled in Naha’s Kumemura久米村 district in the early fifteenth century and probably brought the sanxian三弦, ancestor of the sanshin三線 Tokita 311–312.

Shō Shin’s Kingdom · 1478–1609

  • King Shō Shin尚真 (r. 1478–1526) united the islands from Amami to Yaeyama, forbade private arms and is credited with bringing Chinese processional music (rojigaku路次楽) Tokita 309, 312.
  • Ryukyu prospered as a tributary of Ming China and an entrepôt between China, Japan and Southeast Asia Tokita 309.

Satsuma薩摩 Rule · 1609–1879

  • In 1609 an army from Satsuma (southern Kyushu九州) invaded; Amami was annexed outright. The kingdom kept a sham sovereignty toward China, and its nobility built a self-consciously Ryukyuan court culture Tokita 303, 312.
  • Highlights: Tamagusuku Chōkun’s kumiodori組踊 (1719) and Nomura Anchō’s scores for King Shō Tai尚泰 (1869) Tokita 312–313.

Okinawa Prefecture · 1879–1945

  • The Meiji government dissolved the kingdom in 1879. Displaced aristocrats settled in the provinces and spread court arts to former commoners Tokita 303–305.
  • Poverty sent many to the mills of Osaka or abroad; the Second World War brought devastation Tokita 307.

Occupation and Reversion · 1945–today

  • Twenty-seven years of US military occupation ended with reversion to Japan in 1972 Tokita 307–308.
  • Okinawan music now enjoys a popularity unmatched by traditional music elsewhere in Japan Tokita 321.

G Thompson stresses that Meiji was not mainly a time of Westernization for Okinawa: assimilation was imposed from outside, while the scattering of the aristocracy caused an artistic flowering from within Tokita 304–305.

2Ryukyuan Folk Music

2.1Kamiuta

Sacred Songs
The bedrock of all Ryukyuan music and dance is kamiuta上歌, sacred songs sung by priestesses (noroノロ or tsukasa司) in religious rites, even today. Under the kingdom they formed a female hierarchy headed by the high priestess Kikoe-ōgimi聞得大君, usually a sister or relative of the king.
(2.1)

Tokita 305. The earliest strata of Ryukyuan folk music survive in the Miyako宮古 islands, long closed to outside influence Tokita 305.

The songs call on ancestral spirits in Nirai-Kanaiニライカナイ, the other world across the sea, or on gods in the heavenly Obotsu-Kagura of the Chinese-influenced court cosmology. They ask for good harvests, safe voyages, cures and rain, or carry oracles, and are sung in open-air shrines (utaki御嶽) where the gods possess the priestess Tokita 305–306.

Musically, kamiuta range from heightened speech to short strophic melodies of narrow range that mirror the parallel couplets of the text; they are usually unaccompanied, sometimes with a stick drum. The priestesses’ gestures include basic moves of classical dance Tokita 306.

2.2Work Songs and Festivals

The largest surviving body of farm work songs is in Yaeyama八重山: yuntaユンタ and jirabaジラバ. They are short strophic tunes in a variant of the Okinawa沖縄 scale: frames of a perfect fourth or fifth with a central note a major third above the lower frame. In Koinā yunta, a 12-beat phrase ending on the upper frame is answered by a 12-beat phrase returning to the lower one Tokita 306.

The main festivals are Bon (lunar seventh month) and the harvest festival (full moon of the eighth month). The best-known Bon dance is the eisāエイサー of central Okinawa: after the spirits have left, young men and women go from house to house singing and dancing to sanshin三線 and drums Tokita 306. Harvest festivals mix classical items with local ones Tokita 307.

beyond the main source

Malm notes that Okinawan drummers lift the sticks quickly off the head after each stroke and fill the rhythm with lively vocal calls, far from the kakegoe掛け声 of nō能, and that the outer islands keep a folk style close to the mainland’s Malm 266–267.

2.3Recreational Songs

G Popular songs grew partly from recreational songs, which, like Japanese utagaki歌垣, began as improvised courting exchanges; the custom survives in Amami奄美. Most use a skeletal melody that carries many texts of the right meter, so text and music are not tied; the classical repertory shares this trait Tokita 307.

Amami developed its own tradition after 1609: a wide vocal range with much falsetto, and plucking with a thin sliver of bamboo instead of a large horn or ebony plectrum. Both traditions meet on Tokunoshima徳之島 and Okinoerabu沖永良部島 Tokita 307.

2.4New Folk Songs and Okinawan Pop

Shin-min’yō
Shin-min’yō新民謡, “new folk songs” rooted in tradition, began in the late 1920s with Fukuhara Chōki普久原朝喜 (1903–81), whose Osaka label Marufuku Records was the first to issue Okinawan music. Unlike mainland shin-min’yō, his songs remain central to the repertory.
(2.2)

Tokita 307. Record companies have been based in Okinawa only since the 1950s Tokita 307.

Songs of great pathos came from poverty, emigration, war and occupation, such as Yaka-bushi屋嘉節, set in a postwar internment camp. Singers such as Kadekaru Rinshō嘉手苅林昌, Noborikawa Seijin登川誠仁, Kina Shōei喜納昌永 and China Sadao知名定男 shaped the image of Okinawan music Tokita 307–308.

After reversion in 1972 a fusion of traditional music and the rock of the US bases was pioneered by Kina Shōkichi喜納昌吉 (Champloose), Teruya Rinken照屋林賢 (Rinken Band) and China Sadao. It stresses high female vocals, the Okinawa scale, the sanshin and the dance rhythms of eisā and kachāshīカチャーシー Tokita 308.

3Court and Classical Music

3.1Chinese and Japanese Genres

Imported Genres
The Ryukyuan court used two Chinese genres: ozagaku御座楽, Ming and Qing vocal and instrumental music for state occasions, and rojigaku路次楽, processional music for double-reed pipe, trumpets, drums, clappers and cymbals. From Japan it took nō能 and the koto箏, the only important instrument imported from Japan.
(3.1)

Tokita 309. Ozagaku is pronounced uzagaku御座楽 in Okinawan Tokita 305.

Ryukyuan embassies to Edo江戸 marched to rojigaku, whose volume overwhelmed the townspeople. Both Chinese genres lapsed with the kingdom; since the restoration of Shuri首里 Castle in 1990 they are being recreated with help from Taiwan and Fujian Tokita 309. The koto now mostly accompanies the sanshin三線 but keeps a small solo repertory of danmono段物 in Yatsuhashi八橋 style and three songs Tokita 309.

3.2Omoro and the Birth of Uta-sanshin

The indigenous classical music is uta-sanshin歌三線, songs (fushi節) accompanied by sanshin, sung in the literary language of Shuri Tokita 309–310.

Ryūka
Uta-sanshin texts are mostly ryūka琉歌: three lines of eight syllables and one of six (8-8-8-6), unlike the 5-7 meters of Japanese verse.
(3.2)

Tokita 309, 311.

3.3Schools and Kumiodori

After 1609 the nobility forged a distinctly Ryukyuan culture, above all for the Chinese investiture missions; court arts were called ukwanshin udui御冠船踊, “crown ship dances” Tokita 312.

4The Sanshin

4.1The Instrument

The sanshin三線, a three-string plucked lute with a snakeskin body, holds pride of place among Ryukyuan instruments; many Okinawan adults can sing basic classical items while accompanying themselves Tokita 305.

4.2Tunings

Three Tunings
With a movable do set to the singer’s range, the standard sanshin tuning is honchōshi本調子, do–fa–do′, as on the shamisen. Niagi二揚, do–sol–do′, serves mainly lyrical solo songs. Sansagi三下げ lowers the top string a whole tone from honchōshi.
(4.1)

Tokita 318, 320. Thompson prints the sansagi example as “C-F-B”; a whole tone below c′ is b♭. Miki: for instrumental music the first string usually lies between f and c′, the second a fourth above Miki 115.

4.3The Classical Ensemble

Over time uta-sanshin歌三線 gained other instruments: a koto箏 in concert and, for dance, also the kūchō胡弓 (a three-string spike fiddle with a small snakeskin body), a flute and a stick drum. Only the sanshin players sing Tokita 313.

G Playing was once restricted to men, and women are still not generally accepted as classical singers, yet the koto is now almost entirely played by women Tokita 313. Sanshin parts move simply on the beat or in eighth notes; Miki advises keeping to such patterns Tokita 318; Miki 115.

5Nufa-bushi and Kunkunshī

5.1Kunkunshī Notation

Kunkunshī
Kunkunshī工工四 is the Ryukyuan sanshin三線 tablature, adapted from the Chinese gongche工尺 system and named after its first three symbols. Each square is one beat, twelve to a line. A header gives the number of beats and the tempo, measured against a heartbeat of 72 per minute.
(5.1)

Tokita 314–315, 318.

Nufa-bushi伊野波節, in the second volume of the Nomura anthology, is a “semi-mukashi-bushi昔節”: the second volume holds the weighty, slow mukashi-bushi, and the others mostly shorter ha-bushi端節. It also accompanies one of the seven classical women’s dances Tokita 314. In solo performance do may lie anywhere from about G to D; C is the usual ensemble pitch Tokita 315.

5.2Form and Voice

Form
Each song opens (and closes) with a short sanshin prelude, the utamuchi歌持. The form of Nufa-bushi follows the four lines of the ryūka琉歌: A-A // B-C. Section A ends with stylized syllables (hayashi-kotoba囃子詞, “anu nzo yo”), and the lower two lines begin by shifting the tonal center to the fourth degree.
(5.2)

Tokita 318.

The voice spans two octaves, the widest range in the repertory, with long notes at both ends. It gives a melismatic version of the slow sanshin skeleton, syncopating against it, and breathes unobtrusively within long phrases. Several vocal inflections resemble nō能 technique, since early performers sang both Tokita 318.

Other forms: through-composed ha-bushi (Chin-bushi), A-A with hayashi-kotoba filling the short last line, A-A′ (Chunjun-bushi), and nakafū仲風 songs in utamuchi-A-A′-A″-B Tokita 318, 320.

6Ryukyuan Scales

6.1Classical Scales

Classical Scales
Nufa-bushi伊野波節 uses a diatonic major scale without the sixth, with the tonal center moving between the first and fourth degrees. The seventh is unstable, usually about a quarter tone flat. Traditional scales tend toward such shifting centers and unstable fourths or sevenths.
(6.1)

Tokita 320.

G Other honchōshi本調子 songs center on the open middle string with a slightly high fourth (Akatsichi-bushi); a niagi二揚 scale with one clear center and diatonic intonation (Fishi-bushi干瀬節) became the norm of shin-min’yō新民謡 Tokita 320. Miki notes that the classical sanshin三線 pitches deviate from equal temperament Miki 115.

6.2The Okinawa Scale and Ritsu

Okinawa沖縄 Scale
Modern folk music favors a clear pentatonic scale, do–mi–fa–sol–ti, in major-scale intonation (re often replaces mi descending): Koizumi’s “Okinawa scale”. As tetrachords, its infix lies a major third above the lower frame. The oldest Miyako宮古 songs use ritsu律 instead, with the infix a major second above.
(6.2)

Tokita 306, 320.

G Thompson argues ritsu, found in the earliest folk layers all over Japan, probably predates the Okinawan scales, which have no mainland counterpart and seem tied to uta-sanshin歌三線 Tokita 320. Miki instead derives the Ryūkyū scale from yō陽 with some pitches raised Miki 2. Malm hears a “Chinese sound” in the pentatonic scale but a distinctly Okinawan lilt in the rhythm Malm 267.

7Ainu: People and Concepts

7.1The Ainu Today

The Ainuアイヌ
The Ainu (“human being”) once lived across northern Honshu, Hokkaido北海道, Sakhalin樺太 and the Kuriles. Hokkaido’s survey counts about 24,000, though other estimates are several times higher; few elders still command the language.
(7.1)

Tokita 323–324. Ainu words in this chapter are written in italics without Japanese script.

G Chiba warns that the data are thin, so information from different times and places must be merged: most genre data date from the 1930s to the 1970s, while knowledge of voice production and transmission comes from recent interviews Tokita 324. Malm likens the Ainu’s fate to that of Native Americans and traces Siberian roots in their jew’s harp and shamans’ drum Malm 267.

7.2Sa, Haw and Hum

Ainu Concepts
No Ainu word matches “music”. A key term is sa, roughly “melody”: what separates song from speech, including ornament, voice changes and rhythmic feel. The Saru dialect also separates haw (“voice”: humans, birds, the tonkori, the violin) from hum (“sound”: drums, bells, motorcycles).
(7.2)

Tokita 324.

G Music for the Ainu is less an art for its own sake than a tool: festival song and dance, stories in song, prayer, formal greetings and disputes, even magic spells Tokita 324.

8Ainu Musical Features and Genres

8.1Melody Beyond Pitch

Three Ways to Make a Melody
Ainuアイヌ melody can be structured by pitch, by timbre or by voice production: falsetto against natural voice, voiced against voiceless, loud against soft, exhaled against inhaled. Each can shape a tune alone or combined. Pitched tunes use pentatonic sets, mostly anhemitonic, of two to seven tones.
(8.1)

Tokita 325. The fewer the tones, the more timbre dominates; six or seven tones suggest recent Japanese influence Tokita 325.

Rekte (“to make ring out”) names alternations of vocal technique, from quick flicks into falsetto to glottal stops that cut the voice, almost like vibrato; repeated, they become structure, and for dance they create the pulse Tokita 325–326. Nuclear tones need not be a fourth or fifth apart, unlike Koizumi’s Japanese modes Tokita 325. Songs repeat short melodies of two to four beats; longer falling melodies show Japanese influence Tokita 332.

8.2Rhythm and Group Formats

Fixed tempo and meter of two to four beats prevail, at a medium tempo. Most songs are accompanied only by hand-clapping, on every beat or every other, on- or off-beat, without coordination, unless the clapping is part of a dance or a seated circle Tokita 332–333.

8.4Instruments

Instruments
Chiba’s three main instruments: the tonkori (ka), a five-string zither of Sakhalin and northern Hokkaido北海道 played on open strings only; the mukkuri, a bamboo jew’s harp; and the kaco, a Sakhalin frame drum for shamanic rites, now obsolete. Most songs, though, are accompanied by a struck vessel lid (sintoko), a stick on the hearth, or clapping.
(8.2)

Tokita 331–332, 338–341. Another tuning is a–g–f–d–c Tokita 331.

The tonkori, nearly obsolete by the 1970s, has since revived with more precise, more harmonic playing under Western influence Tokita 331, 338. Most mukkuri pieces imitate birds, animals, rain or woodcutting through vocal-tract effects as much as melody Tokita 332. Other sound makers include the pehkutu reed pipe and mouth-resonated strings Tokita 341.

9Today and Research

9.1Ainu Transmission and Revival

Ainuアイヌ song was learned by imitation: a child sat by a skilled singer at a banquet, a bride learned from her mother-in-law. Singers practiced alternating registers while counting on their fingers, and mukkuri tunes were taught with mnemonics whose vowels shape the mouth Tokita 341–342.

Revival
In 1984 and 1994 the government designated 17 Ainu groups as holders of an Important Intangible Cultural Property. New music on traditional lines includes the tonkori albums of Oki (Kano Oki).
(9.1)

Tokita 342.

G Chiba lists today’s challenges: an education system, teaching materials and places to learn, since the school curriculum is Western-based and teachers are few Tokita 342.

Where the Sources Differ

Errors and disagreements found while cross-reading, grouped by source, with the check that exposed each one. The guide text uses the form named in each check.

Malm 2000

When Okinawa沖縄 became a prefecture. Malm: Okinawa became a Japanese colony in the seventeenth century and was absorbed as a prefecture in the last half of the twentieth century (p. 266).
Check: Thompson: the Meiji明治 government dissolved the kingdom in 1879 and created the prefecture; 1972 was the reversion from US occupation (Tokita 303, 308). Malm seems to conflate reversion with annexation.
Which finger holds the sanshin三線 pick. Malm: a horn pick attached to the middle finger (p. 267).
Check: Miki: a long pick of ebony or horn on the first finger (p. 114). Thompson only says a large plectrum of horn or ebony (Tokita 307). The guide gives both.
How many strings the tonkori has. Malm: a two- to five-stringed zither (p. 267).
Check: Chiba: five strings; a few three-, four- and six-string examples are reported (Tokita 331, 338). Five is the norm.
How many Ainuアイヌ instruments matter. Malm: only two instruments of importance, tonkori and mukkuri, traditionally played by women (p. 267).
Check: Chiba: three main instruments, adding the kaco frame drum of Sakhalin樺太 shamanism (Tokita 337–341); he does not say they were women’s instruments.
Who performs rekuhkara. Malm: a duet by two women (p. 268).
Check: Chiba: two people face one another; it is usually for adults but also among children (Tokita 334, 337).

Miki 2008

The Yuan dynasty dates. Miki: the sanxian of the Yuan period (960–1368) (p. 113).
Check: Miki himself gives the usual 1271–1368 elsewhere (p. 88); 960 is the start of the Song. Also noted in Chapter 7.
Where the Okinawa scale comes from. Miki: the Ryūkyū scale appears to be derived from the yō scale with some pitches raised (p. 2).
Check: Thompson: the distinctive Okinawan scales have no mainland counterparts and seem tied to uta-sanshin; the older ritsu layer is shared with Japan (Tokita 320). An open question of interpretation.

Tokita and Hughes 2017

Lute or zither. Chiba once calls the tonkori a “lute” (p. 324).
Check: Elsewhere Chiba calls it a five-stringed zither (pp. 331, 338), as does Malm (p. 267). The guide says zither.
Sansagi example. Thompson prints the sansagi tuning as “C-F-B” (p. 320).
Check: Lowering the top string c′ by a whole tone gives b♭, as the guide shows; probably the flat sign was lost.
