1The Biwa and Its Narratives

1.1A Family of Lutes

The biwa琵琶 is a short-necked, pear-shaped lute with a peg box bent back at a right angle, two crescent sound holes, and four or five frets Miki 71–73. Its ancestor is the Chinese pipa琵琶, itself shaped by the Persian ud Miki 71 Malm 150. In Japan it split into several traditions, each with its own instrument, plectrum and repertoire:

TraditionAroseWho Played ItWhat It Sang
Gakubiwa楽琵琶arrived about 700court musiciansnone: part of the gagaku雅楽 ensemble (Chapter 2)
Biwa hōshi琵琶法師 and heike平家13th centuryblind professional recitersThe Tale of the Heike
Mōsō盲僧 and zatō座頭 biwadocumented from the Edo江戸 periodblind priests and blind entertainers in Kyushu九州sutras, exorcisms, war tales, short songs
Satsuma-biwa薩摩琵琶16th century (samurai), Meiji明治 (concert)samurai, then Tokyo amateursdidactic poems and war tales
Chikuzen-biwa筑前琵琶1890ssighted amateurs, mostly womenlyrical tales in a shamisen-influenced style

Tokita 77, 89, 105–106, 115–120. Satsuma-biwa and chikuzen-biwa are grouped by scholars as kindai biwagaku近代琵琶楽, “modern biwa music” Tokita 115.

Tall Frets, Pressed Strings
G The core sound of every narrative biwa comes from tall frets. The string is pressed between the frets, not onto the neck, so one fret can give several pitches by pressure, and the string buzzes against the fret, the effect called sawariさわり. The gakubiwa, with low frets, stops strings on top of the frets and gives one pitch per fret.
(1.1)

Malm 156 Miki 74. Frets grew taller as the instrument evolved: the gakubiwa barely higher than a Chinese pipa, the heike biwa a little higher, and satsuma and chikuzen frets higher still Malm 156 n. 4.

1.2Narrative Music and the Blind

Japanese narrative music is called katarimono語り物, “told things” Tokita 77. For six centuries its chief bearers were blind men. In the fourteenth century the professions open to the blind, medical (massage, acupuncture, moxa) and musical, came under a guild, the Tōdō-za当道座, which licensed performers Malm 153. Tokita’s authors date the national organization later: heike guilds “eventually merged” by the start of the Edo period Tokita 80.

The Blind Guild
The Tōdō当道 gave its members ranks, from kengyō検校 at the top through kōtō勾当 and bettō別当 to zatō. In the Edo period it moved into the new koto箏 and shamisen三味線 music; the Meiji government abolished it in 1871.
(1.2)

Tokita 77–78, 80. The Tōdō also fought the Kyushu mōsō for control of secular performance; see 6.3.

G Two lines of descent run through this chapter. The court-and-capital line runs from the gakubiwa through heike. The Kyushu line runs from blind ritual players to the modern concert schools. Tokita’s authors stress that the two lines kept influencing each other Tokita 78, 105.

2Heike: History

2.1Through the Periods

Step through the history of heike平家 with the buttons or the arrow keys. Komoda薦田 uses the term heike for the performance tradition; heikyoku平曲 is an Edo-period amateurs’ word now common among literary scholars Tokita 77.

Formative Period · 13th century

  • Heike narrative arose in the thirteenth century. It may first have been a requiem for the Taira (Heike) clan, destroyed by the Minamoto (Genji源氏) in the wars of 1180–85 Tokita 77.
  • The best-known origin story is in Tsurezuregusa徒然草 §226 (Yoshida Kenkō吉田兼好, 1330–31): the recluse Yukinaga行長 wrote the Tale under the Tendai天台 abbot Jien慈円 and taught it to Shōbutsu生仏, a blind biwa hōshi琵琶法師 from eastern Japan Tokita 78.
  • No official record names Yukinaga or Shōbutsu, but the three figures stand neatly for the three sources of heike: kōshiki講式 chant (Jien), gagaku雅楽 (Yukinaga) and biwa hōshi music (Shōbutsu) Tokita 78. Malm tells the same story and draws the same three streams Malm 152–153.

Flourishing · 14th–16th centuries

  • Two or three generations after Shōbutsu, reciters split into the Ichikata一方 and Yasakagata八坂方 Tokita 78.
  • Kakuichi覚一 (?1300–71) wrote down the Kakuichi-bon覚一本 text although blind, added new techniques, and founded the Ichikata guild Tokita 78–80.
  • The century after Kakuichi was the peak: temple services, paid public (kanjin勧進) shows, and private gatherings of nobles and lords Tokita 80.
  • After the Ōnin応仁 war (1467–77) many reciters moved to jōruri浄瑠璃 with the new shamisen三味線, but heike kept a place at poetry and tea gatherings. Tokugawa Ieyasu徳川家康 became an ardent patron Tokita 77, 80.

Edo江戸 Period · 1600–1868

  • The shogunate gave the Tōdō当道 privileges and made heike an official ceremonial music Tokita 80.
  • New schools: the Maeda-ryū前田流 (Maeda Kyūichi前田九一) and the Hatano-ryū波多野流 (Hatano Kōichi波多野孝一) Tokita 80.
  • Sighted amateurs developed notation; in 1776 Ogino Chiichi荻野知一, a kengyō検校 in Nagoya名古屋, edited the Heike Mabushi平家正節, which spread across the country Tokita 80.
  • G As notation grew, improvisation “declined severely”, and the style grew more solemn and refined Tokita 80.

Decline and Preservation · 1868–

  • The Tōdō was abolished in 1871. Blind players turned to koto箏, shamisen and medical work; the Hatano-ryū barely lasted in Kyoto until the mid-twentieth century Tokita 78, 80–81.
  • Nagoya lineage: blind players of the Maeda-ryū. Ten pieces were passed down to Imai Tsutomu今井勉 (b. 1958), two of them incomplete, so “eight pieces” is the usual count Tokita 81 and n. 1.
  • Tsugaru津軽 lineage: sighted amateurs. Kusumi Taiso楠美太素 (1815–82) learned Edo Maeda-ryū heike; his son Tateyama Zennoshin館山漸之進 (1845–1916) and grandson Tateyama Kōgo館山甲午 (1894–1989) kept it Tokita 81–82.
  • The government named Nagoya heike (1955) and Tsugaru heike (1959) for recording and preservation; the named performers have since died Tokita 82.

2.2Two Lineages Today

Nagoya and Tsugaru
Both surviving lines belong to the Maeda-ryū and use the Heike Mabushi. The Nagoya lineage was carried by professional blind musicians and relies almost wholly on oral transmission; the Tsugaru lineage was carried by sighted amateurs. They differ because of distance and because each changed after 1871.
(2.1)

Tokita 81–82. Mayeda Akio showed that the Nagoya–Tsugaru difference lies in how each reads the hakase博士 Tokita 102.

Two concrete differences recur in Komoda’s chapter:

  • Scale of kudoki口説. Nagoya sings it in the miyako-bushi都節 scale, Tsugaru in the min’yō民謡 scale; Nagoya probably once used min’yō too Tokita 82 n. 2.
  • Sawariさわり. The Nagoya biwa琵琶 has a buzzing fret; Tsugaru players did without it until some of Tateyama館山 Kōgo’s students copied Nagoya around the 1980s Tokita 96.

Malm’s account, from the 1950s on, says that by mid-century only a few old men in Nagoya could recite the Tale, and that Imai Tsutomu took up the traditional style Malm 153.

2.3Malm’s Picture of the Origins

Malm opens with blind priests from Kyushu九州. Eight were called to Mt Hiei比叡山 to drive snakes from the grounds of the new Enryakuji延暦寺; four stayed in Kyoto. One who returned, Jōjuin成就院, became the founder of the Chikuzen筑前 blind school, and one who stayed, Manshōin, the founder of the Satsuma薩摩 school Malm 151.

G He adds color that Tokita’s authors do not: the Shimazu島津 clan used the blind priests’ nationwide network as a spy ring, and Satsuma later set them to “improve” clan members with edifying music Malm 152, 154. Treat these as Malm’s narrative; Tokita’s chapter 5 gives the sect documents’ versions, which it calls unverifiable (see 6.1).

3Heike: The Sung Narrative

3.1Scale and Tonal Centers

Each heike平家 melody centers on a dominant tone and a tone a fourth above it. Over a range of about two and a half octaves there are five basic tonal centers, written in relative pitch as B–e–a–b–e′; a piece climbs through them from low B to high e′ Tokita 82.

Two Scale Families (Komoda薦田)
R Heike builds its scale from fourths (Koizumi’s tetrachords). Kudoki口説 and hiroi拾 use conjunct min’yō民謡 tetrachords (middle tone a minor third above the lower). Shojū初重, chūon中音 and sanjū三重 use disjunct miyako-bushi都節 tetrachords (middle tone a minor second above the lower). Komoda reads the first group as inherited from biwa hōshi琵琶法師 narrative, the second from kōshiki講式 chant.
(3.1)

Tokita 82; Koizumi’s tetrachords are explained in Chapter 1.

Our illustration, built from the opening scales in Tokita’s Table 4.1 (pp. 90–91); Tokita’s own Figure 4.4 uses Shibata Minao’s “skeleton” diagram.

Malm describes a typical vocal line in the same terms: long narration between notes a fourth or fifth apart, with a few seconds below a pitch center, “reminiscent of” the Buddhist and nō能 tone system Malm 159.

3.2Rhythm and Delivery

Syllabic passages have no meter but a fairly steady pulse of one beat per syllable; melismatic passages are in free rhythm Tokita 82. Hirano Kenji平野健次 combined pitch and rhythm into three delivery styles; Komoda adds a fourth between the last two Tokita 82–84:

Four Styles of Delivery
ginshō吟唱 declamation: syllabic, without musical pitch (e.g. shirakoe白声).
rōshō朗唱 intoned recitation: syllabic, precise pitches (e.g. kudoki, hiroi).
arioso: the many intermediate stages (e.g. orikoe折声, sage).
eishō詠唱 aria: melismatic, precise pitches (e.g. chūon, sanjū).
(3.2)

G The same three Hirano平野健次 styles reappear in Kyushu九州 blind narrative (6.4) and in other vocal arts Tokita 113.

Three Views of a Formula
G Formulas are linked by text (hiroi for battle and armor, orikoe for laments and prayers, chūon for elegant or noble scenes, kamiuta only for waka), by music (basic tone, opening scale, a fixed biwa琵琶 prelude) and by structure (fixed sequences, completeness, function). Some formulas, such as kudoki, shirakoe and sashikoe, have no consistent link to content.
(3.3)

Tokita 84. Kudoki rarely stands alone: it is followed by sage or kowarisage, which are “incomplete” formulas attached to complete ones Tokita 86–87.

3.4The Dan

How a Dan Is Built (Komoda)
R A dan段 is a sequence of formulas, the smallest unit where musical and literary structure coincide. A typical dan moves introductory → (connecting) → central → concluding. The central formula carries the musical and literary weight and sits higher; the concluding formula sinks to low B.
(3.4)

Tokita 87–89. A dan ends where the range drops and melisma increases.

  • Introductory: usually kudoki; sometimes shirakoe, shojū, sashikoe or geon.
  • Central: sanjū, chūon, hiroi, orikoe, kamiuta; the central formula sets the character of the whole dan.
  • Concluding: kudari and shojū.

Tokita 89. Not every dan is complete: some have only introductory formulas, and others add a conclusion, like Suzuki’s fourth dan: shirakoe, hazumi, kudoki, shojū Tokita 89.

Suzuki has 19 formulas in four dan. Its first dan is the model case worked through in Example 3.5 Tokita 84, 87. Malm describes the same principle more loosely: the Tale’s two hundred episodes are divided into phrases, each with a stereotyped singing manner Malm 158.

G This way of composing by combining formulas is shared with nō and kōshiki chant Tokita 84, and Malm notes the shared terms ageuta上ゲ歌, sageuta下ゲ歌 and kudoki across Buddhist chant, heike and nō Malm 159.

4The Heike Biwa

4.1Origin and Structure

The heike平家 biwa琵琶 has four strings and five tall, bridge-like frets; the peg box bends back from a short neck, and the body is shallow like a pipa琵琶. There is no standard size, but most are about 80 cm long Tokita 89.

From Court Lute to Heike Biwa (Komoda薦田)
G The heike biwa descends from the gakubiwa楽琵琶, which came to Japan around 700. Twelfth- and thirteenth-century pictures show biwa hōshi琵琶法師 playing instruments of the same form (the gakubiwa is about 20 cm longer), and nobles rewarded reciters with biwa琵琶 strings and plectra. The fifth fret had been added by 1299, perhaps to reach the high basic tone e′.
(4.1)

Tokita 89, 92. Komoda states that the older theory of a separate blind-priest lute brought by its own route in the Nara奈良 period “has been disproved” Tokita 89; Malm and Miki both repeat that theory. See Where the Sources Differ.

The gakubiwa plectrum has rounded tips; the heike plectrum has pointed ones Tokita 92. Malm describes it as larger and wider Malm 157, and Miki as slightly larger than the court plectrum Miki 75.

4.2Tuning and Frets

The Heike Biwa Tuning
R The tuning derives from the gagaku雅楽 ōshiki-chō黄鐘調 tuning (A, c, e, a), but in relative pitch. Strings I, III and IV are tuned like the shamisen’s niagari二上り: A, e, a. String II is tuned so that it gives e when pressed at the third fret; it rests on the sawariさわり fret and is never played open.
(4.2)

Tokita 92–93.

The Five Frets
Numbered from the peg box, the third fret gives a major third above the open string, the fourth a fourth, the fifth a fifth; these three are fixed. The first fret is movable and set for the best sawari. The second is rarely used, kept, Komoda says, to show the instrument’s noble origin.
(4.3)

Tokita 93–94. Malm notes that fret spacing varies between instruments, so the scales vary too, and that pressure behind the fret can partly compensate Malm 157.

4.3Playing Techniques

In Nagoya名古屋 practice the player sits with legs folded to one side. The left hand presses close to the left side of the frets, and the right strikes with the bachi撥 Tokita 94:

TechniqueWhat Happens
bachi from abovethe basic stroke
sukui掬い (“scoop”)an upstroke from below
hajiki弾き (“flick”)left-hand pizzicato
hishigiヒシギdamping the after-sound

Tokita 94. Early in the fourteenth century a noble wrote that “the biwa was played like the koto箏”, a sign that single-string melodic plucking from the gagaku koto had entered heike Tokita 94.

4.4The Biwa Part

Four Kinds of Biwa Music
R With rare exceptions the biwa never plays while the voice sings. Its music falls into four kinds: preludes named after the formula they introduce (kudoki bachi, hiroi bachi; a shortened ryaku-bachi when the formula returns), interludes tied to a vocal segment, ai no te合の手 (often one stroke giving the next phrase’s basic tone) and the postlude kōsō.
(4.4)

Tokita 94. Preludes before central formulas are long: the hiroi bachi repeats double stops rapidly for a martial mood Tokita 94.

Malm describes the same alternation: the biwa gives the starting pitch, the voice sings the phrase, and the biwa returns with the next pitch or a mood pattern Malm 159. He reports a theory, credited to George Gish, that every biwa convention of a performance is found in a long instrumental secret piece played before the narration Malm 158 and n. 7.

4.5Sawari

The Nagoya biwa has a fret nearest the peg box with a mountain-shaped ridge. Strings I and III vibrate against its slope and buzz; this is sawari. The fret is moved before each performance for the best effect, and string II sits on its top to keep it from toppling, which is why string II is never played open Tokita 95.

The shamisen’s sawari appeared in the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century, and the heike biwa probably developed its own around the same time; the first written mention is in the Jūkyō, before the mid-eighteenth century.
(4.5)

Tokita 96 and n. 4. Miki explains the acoustics on the modern biwa: a main pitch between the pressing point and the bridge, plus a higher secondary pitch from the string’s contact with the thick fret Miki 74. Malm notes that the early shamisen三味線 was modified to imitate the biwa buzz Malm 156 n. 5.

5.1Scores

There are seven or eight kinds of heike平家 notation. Blind reciters needed none; scores arose in the seventeenth century as aids for sighted amateurs Tokita 96.

Reading a Heike Score
R As in kōshiki講式 chant and nō能, a heike score writes formula names and hakase博士 between the vertical lines of text. Syllables without hakase are sung on the formula’s basic tone. The same sign can be realized differently depending on the formula and its basic tone, and some techniques are taught only orally (kuden口伝).
(5.1)

Tokita 96, 98. Kindaichi Haruhiko金田一春彦 transcribed over a hundred kinds of hakase from Tateyama館山 Kōgo’s performance Tokita 100.

5.2Biwa Notation

The Mabushi prints the biwa琵琶 melodies in a separate index. Where certain formula names and hakase appear, the matching biwa melody is played. The notation uses kuchi-shōga口唱歌 (sung syllables), with string numbers (low to high) and left-hand pressing points. Interludes and the kōsō postlude are not written; they are taught orally Tokita 98.

Kudoki Bachi in Syllables
The kudoki口説 prelude in kuchi-shōga: Kuwararin (press III-4, mute II, sweep four strings) · Ren (III and IV open, left-hand pizzicato) · Ton (I open) · Tsun (II-3, the grace note e) · Ton (III open) · Ton (I open) · Jan (press II-3, strike I and II).
(5.2)

6Kyushu: The Blind Traditions

6.1Mōsō and Zatō

Two Kinds of Blind Player
mōsō盲僧 were blind Buddhist priests, mostly under the Tendai天台 sect, who performed agrarian and household rites with the biwa琵琶. Zatō座頭 (or biwa-hiki) were blind men without priestly certification who did similar rites and lived mainly by entertainment. Since the Edo江戸 period both carried liturgical texts and secular narratives.
(6.1)

Tokita 105–106, 108. The term zatō-biwa座頭琵琶 has been used since 1990 as a partner to mōsō-biwa盲僧琵琶; scholarly labels also include jijin-biwa地神琵琶, kōjin-biwa荒神琵琶, kuzure-biwa and higo-biwa肥後琵琶 Tokita 106. There is no record of blind women (goze瞽女) playing biwa or performing rites in Kyushu九州 Tokita 105 n. 3.

The sects’ own histories trace the Jijinkyō地神経 (sutras to the earth deity Jijin) from the Buddha to a blind follower and through China and Korea to Kyushu in the reign of Emperor Kinmei欽明天皇 (539–71). De Ferranti stresses that the earliest reliable documents are all mid-Edo, so these accounts cannot be checked Tokita 109 and n. 8.

6.2Rituals

The main rites today are group ceremonies honoring each sect’s founder (hōraku hōyō) and seasonal rites (kaidan hōyō) built around harai祓い, exorcism of the earth spirit Jijin and of Kōjin, the spirit of the hearth. Mōsō and zatō were also hired to purify wells and new houses and to pray for rain Tokita 110–111.

Biwa in the Rite
In ritual chant the biwa plays almost continuously, with a clear pulse and most moras in a binary relation to it. Pitch relations are often vague: the biwa can act like the mokugyo木魚 woodblock of ordinary sutra chanting. Texts are standard sutras, invocations of Jijin and Kōjin, and shakumon narratives.
(6.2)

Tokita 111. Contrast heike平家, where voice and biwa alternate (fact 4.4).

Malm names the music kōjinbiwa荒神琵琶 after the kitchen god and hears, on his CD track 8, a Kannon観音 chant over “a jaunty pentatonic tune” in the yō陽 scale with a brief turn to in陰 Malm 151, 160.

6.3Secular Repertory and the Tōdō

On their seasonal rounds mōsō stayed in parishioners’ homes and performed war tales (kuzure崩れ) and short songs (hauta端歌) in the evenings. This made them rivals of the Tōdō当道, which from the seventeenth century asked the shogunate to ban secular biwa by anyone but its own heike zatō. The Satsuma mōsō were protected by the Shimazu島津; others sought the patronage of the Kyoto temple Shōren-in青蓮院 (1783). The Tōdō won its dispute with the mōsō sects in 1674 Tokita 111–112.

What the Blind Players Sang
The secular repertory has three kinds: danmono段物 (also kuzure or biwa gundan), long narratives in sections of 30 minutes or more on Kyushu history, Heike episodes and heroes; hauta, short pieces; and charimono, comic pieces.
(6.3)

Tokita 112. The Meiji明治 government outlawed mōsō ritual in 1871; the sects re-formed as Tendai hōryū in 1907 and then banned secular performance Tokita 110–112. The state designated higo-biwa an Intangible Cultural Property in 1973, though Higo肥後 players said they first heard the term from scholars Tokita 112 and n. 10.

Each shōdan opens with a biwa motive (te) and closes with a full or half cadence in the voice.
(6.4)

Tokita 113. The segments are fushi節 to players and senritsukei旋律型 (“melodic patterns”) to scholars; each has formulaic text and music, which makes “composition in performance” possible Tokita 113.

6.5Instruments of the Blind Players

Blind players’ biwa were not standardized, but all had four strings, tall frets allowing pitch bends of up to a fourth at some frets, and strong sawariさわり. They had to come apart for travel: the slender “bamboo-leaf” sasa biwa笹琵琶 and a shorter rounder type. A large sasa biwa used in Higo is often called the higo-biwa Tokita 114. Malm adds that the blind player was led by his staff, or by an assistant holding its other end Malm 159.

Tunings separate sacred and secular: rokuchōshi六調子 (do-fa-sol-sol) for sutras and honchōshi本調子 (do-fa-do′-do′, like the shamisen三味線 tuning of that name) for narratives. From the early twentieth century many players switched to the standard four-string chikuzen biwa.
(6.5)

Tokita 114.

7Satsuma-Biwa

7.1Satsuma Origins

The conventional story, written by Meiji明治 and Taishō大正 practitioners and repeated by scholars, runs as follows Tokita 116–117:

  • In the mid-sixteenth century Nisshinsai日新斎 (Shimazu Tadayoshi島津忠良, 1492–1568) wrote the Iroha Utaいろは歌, moral poems for samurai retainers, said to have been chanted first with biwa琵琶 by senior mōsō盲僧 of the Jōrakuin常楽院. He also wrote three longer poems, the oldest texts of the hauta端歌 category.
  • With the mōsō patriarch Fuchiwaki Juchōin he remodeled the biwa for a forceful style fit for warriors: larger body and plectrum, a convex soundboard (said to imitate European instruments brought by the Jesuits), and more and taller frets.
A Founding Story to Question
G De Ferranti treats this account with caution. It first appears in an early-twentieth-century source; no satsuma biwa survives from before the late eighteenth century; and the earliest account of Satsuma薩摩 war tales (kuzure崩れ, in the 1783 travel diary Tōzai Yūki) describes a blind hōshi, not a samurai. Early kuzure were probably regional versions of war tales known to blind players across Kyushu九州.
(7.1)

Tokita 117. Shimazu島津 Tadashi’s research also questions Nisshinsai as founder and raises possible Korean sources for the left-hand technique and the kuzure style Tokita 125.

Mid- and late-Edo sources name three styles (fū風) by class: shifū士風 of samurai and senior mōsō, the zatō座頭 style of blind professionals, and machifū町風 of merchant amateurs from the early nineteenth century. Whether these were musical styles or only social labels is unclear; late in the Edo江戸 period the zatō Myōju blurred them by his influence Tokita 117.

Malm describes the Shimazu banning “vulgar” popular music among clan members and recommending three edifying arts: music for a small vertical flute (tenpuku天吹), a special warrior dance (samurai odori侍踊り), and narrative satsumabiwa薩摩琵琶. He calls the samurai style stately and the townspeople’s (machifū) style flashier, and the latter prevails today Malm 154, 161.

7.3The Satsuma Instrument

Satsuma Biwa at a Glance
The satsuma biwa has a large thin plectrum (up to 30 cm wide) struck against the soundboard, tall frets, and a slightly convex soundboard. The four-string, four-fret Seiha instrument is tuned g-D-g-a; the five-string nishiki錦 instrument (Kinshin, Nishiki and Tsuruta players) g-D-g-d-d. The unison of strings I and III drives the kuzure battle interludes.
(7.3)

Tokita 119. The plectrum’s size is traditionally explained by its occasional use as a weapon Tokita 119.

Miki gives the frets of the modern satsuma biwa above any open string: fret 1 a slightly wide minor second (some schools move it to a major second), fret 2 a minor third, fret 3 a fourth, fret 4 a fifth, fret 5 a minor seventh. On the chikuzen biwa fret 1 is a major second and fret 2 a little more than a minor third Miki 79.

G No equal-tempered half step above an open string is possible on either type, Miki warns; pitches between frets are made by pressing the string down, raising it a second or, with two or three fingers, up to a fourth Miki 81. Tokita’s de Ferranti says a Seiha player can regulate pitch by as much as a major ninth by pulling the top string over the fret edge, and that elaborate ornaments in the decaying sound are called yuriユリ Tokita 119–120.

beyond the main source

A composer’s view (Miki). Miki, writing for composers, lists the Satsuma techniques a score can call for Miki 84–86:

  • Striking the body with the plectrum (marked X), alone or with strings; not on the soft paulownia face of the chikuzen biwa, which it would damage.
  • Tremolo on the paired strings IV and V, which never disappears in an ensemble because of the sawariさわり.
  • Suri摺り, scraping a string lengthwise with the plectrum’s edge.
  • Kuzure: fast triplets, a figure-eight plectrum motion and tremolo in mixed meter.

He adds that a single note can never be forte; loudness comes from striking more strings Miki 85. Malm compares the kuzure to a violin tremolo or a sudden diminished chord in an old movie thriller Malm 162.

7.4Fushizuke: Setting a Text

A satsuma or chikuzen piece is defined first by its text, then by the patterns that deliver it. Scholars call the patterns senritsukei旋律型; players say fushi節. Setting a text to them is fushizuke節付け Tokita 122, 124.

The Seiha Maeuta Scheme
R The opening section (maeuta前歌) of most Seiha satsuma-biwa薩摩琵琶 pieces runs: biwa utaidashi → voice ji no chū (on si) → one or more of ji no ge (sol), ji no jō (do) or chūkan nagashi (fa) → ji no jō → chūkiri (fa) → biwa chūkiri no te.
(7.4)

Tokita 122–123. Fumon Yoshinori’s Hana-momiji follows it line by line: ji no chū, ji no jō, ji no ge, ji no jō, chūkiri Tokita 123.

Variation happens at four levels Tokita 124:

  1. Placement of patterns, e.g. one or more kuzure in the long middle section (nakauta中歌).
  2. Inclusion or omission of sub-sections, such as the Seiha gin-gawari.
  3. Choice of version: variant forms differ in length and in “affect”.
  4. Improvisation: a skilled Seiha player may vary a pattern’s inner form, often by joining phrases from different versions. This was not part of chikuzen-biwa筑前琵琶, whose founder treated each piece as through-composed.

8.2The Chikuzen Instrument

Chikuzen Biwa at a Glance
The four-string chikuzen biwa is tuned like the shamisen’s honchōshi本調子 (g-c′-g′, relative), with strong sawariさわり from bamboo strips on its five frets and a flat soundboard. The five-string type (from the 1920s) tunes its lower four strings like the Satsuma instrument, g-d-g-a, and adds d′ above. Both use a small, thick wooden plectrum, sometimes ivory-tipped.
(8.2)

Tokita 122. The four-string type was cheap and popular, c. 1900–20, and common until the 1940s Tokita 120 n. 16, 122.

The five frets carry the names of the five elements, from the top: wood, fire, earth, metal and water, a Daoist symbolism tied to mōsō ritual Tokita 122. Malm also mentions the element names and adds that the chikuzen biwa is held sideways like a shamisen, with a thick blunt plectrum like that of gidayū義太夫 Malm 163. Miki adds that the chikuzen soundboard is soft paulownia, the Satsuma one hard mulberry Miki 73.

8.3Nagashi and Named Patterns

The Asahi-kai opening follows a scheme as fixed as the Seiha正派 maeuta前歌: a biwa prelude hiraki no. 1, 2 or 3, a run of numbered vocal patterns (san, ni, san, yon …) and a closing chū中 pattern Tokita 123.

Nagashi
G The hallmark of chikuzen-biwa is nagashi流し (“flow”): an ornate biwa part and a melismatic vocal line overlap, unlike the alternation of heike. Its placement highlights moments of poetic reflection.
(8.3)

Tokita 124 Malm 163. Malm notes that modern schools often sing and play together, so some performances split singer and player Malm 161.

Malm reports picturesque pattern names (seasons, birds, animals) and numbered sets: about fourteen ban, fifteen go and twenty-five chō Malm 163. Silvain Guignard, a student of Yamazaki Kyokusui, has studied the Tachibana-kai’s creative processes since 1985 Tokita 125–126.

9Research and Open Questions

9.1Heike Research

  • Edo江戸 writers treated heike平家 as performance theory and aesthetics, e.g. Jigū’s Saikai yoteki shū西海余滴集 (17th c.) Tokita 100, 102.
  • Tateyama Zennoshin館山漸之進’s Heike Ongaku-shi平家音楽史 (1910) is the first major study; he also transcribed melodies into staff notation. Kanetsune Kiyosuke兼常清佐 (1913) studied the Hatano-ryū波多野流 Tokita 100.
  • Kindaichi Haruhiko金田一春彦 worked for forty years on the Tsugaru津軽 tradition (Heikyoku-kō平曲考, 1997) Tokita 100.
  • Orality: Kenneth Butler (1966) brought the concept in; Japanese scholars followed after the Parry–Lord work of the 1980s, with Hyōdō兵藤 Hiromi’s Kyushu九州 fieldwork especially influential Tokita 102.
G Komoda’s priorities: an accurate record of current transmission, urgent study of the single remaining Nagoya名古屋 practitioner, and questions of literacy and orality, text and music, formula and improvisation. Lost Nagoya pieces have been reconstructed for Imai Tsutomu今井勉, such as Gion shōja (2000).
(9.1)

Tokita 103.

9.2Kyushu Biwa Research

  • Research on mōsō盲僧 and zatō座頭 has grown since the late 1980s. Since no zatō survive, further work must rely on 1960s–70s recordings and on listeners’ memories Tokita 114–115.
  • For satsuma-biwa薩摩琵琶, Tanabe Hisao’s histories dominated until Koshiyama (1984) and Shimazu島津 Tadashi (from 1993) reexamined pre-Meiji sources Tokita 125.
  • Hyōdō links Kyushu practice to his model of how Edo heikyoku平曲 became fixed, a view contested by Yamashita (2002) Tokita 126.

G Across both chapters, the open question is the same one this guide keeps meeting: how an oral, formula-based art becomes tied to fixed written models, and what is lost or gained Tokita 80, 126.

Where the Sources Differ

Errors and disagreements found while cross-reading, grouped by source, with the check that exposed each one. The guide text uses the form named in each check.

Malm 2000

Heike平家 biwa琵琶 tuning. Malm: the heike biwa is “usually tuned G, B, D, and G′” (p. 157).
Check: Komoda薦田: strings I, III, IV = A, e, a (niagari), with string II tuned so that fret 3 gives e and never played open (Tokita 92–93). Both are relative pitch, but Malm’s is a major-triad tuning and Komoda’s is not. The guide follows Komoda, whose examples come from Nagoya名古屋 practice (4.2).
A separate blind-priest lute. Malm: the blind priests of Kyushu九州 used a smaller biwa “more like the Indian models” (p. 150); the chikuzenbiwa is “more directly related to the Indian form” (p. 162).
Check: Komoda: the theory that the mōsō biwa reached Japan by its own route “has been disproved” (Tokita 89); early biwa hōshi used gakubiwa-type instruments. Miki repeats the older theory (p. 71). See 4.1.
Date of Yukinaga行長. Malm dates Fujiwara no Yukinaga “ca. 1189” (p. 152).
Check: Tsurezuregusa, as Komoda reads it, places Yukinaga’s writing in the early thirteenth century, and no official record of him exists (Tokita 78).
Founder of the Satsuma薩摩 blind school. Malm: founded by Manshōin, a priest who stayed in Kyoto (p. 151).
Check: The Satsuma sect names Hōzan Kengyō宝山検校, who left Kyoto in 1196 with Shimazu Tadahisa島津忠久 (Tokita 110, 116). Both are sect traditions; de Ferranti calls such accounts unverifiable (Tokita 109 n. 8).
How many formulas. Malm: thirteen patterns in the thirteenth century, later as many as twenty-three (p. 158).
Check: Komoda: the Heike Mabushi has 42 kyokusetsu (Tokita 84). Malm may be counting biwa patterns or a different class of formula; not resolved here.
When the blind privileges ended. Malm: abolished as “one of the acts of the Meiji明治 Restoration of 1868” (p. 154).
Check: Tokita: the Tōdō当道 was abolished, and mōsō ritual outlawed, in 1871 (pp. 78, 110). The Restoration began in 1868; the law came three years later.
When the blind guild formed. Malm: all blind professions organized into the Tōdōza in the fourteenth century (p. 153).
Check: Komoda: Kakuichi覚一 founded the Ichikata一方 guild in the fourteenth century, and the guilds merged nationally only by the start of the Edo江戸 period (Tokita 80). De Ferranti dates the Tōdō’s founding to the late fourteenth century (Tokita 106 n. 4). The difference is how “the Tōdō” is defined.

Miki 2008

Who played which biwa. Miki: “Traditionally, women performed Satsuma-biwa and men played Chikuzen-biwa” (p. 75).
Check: De Ferranti: satsuma-biwa was mostly men’s until Suitō Kinjō水藤錦穣, while chikuzen-biwa soon became “chiefly practised by women” (Tokita 115). The guide follows de Ferranti (fact 8.1); Miki may mean today’s performers or vocal ranges.
Age of heike. Miki: the Heike-biwa and Heikyoku arose in the twelfth century (p. 71).
Check: Komoda: heike narrative came into being in the thirteenth century (Tokita 77); the wars it tells of were 1180–85.

Tokita & Hughes 2017

Kudoki in two scales. Komoda groups kudoki with the min’yō-tetrachord formulas (p. 82).
Check: Her own footnote adds that Nagoya now sings kudoki in miyako-bushi, Tsugaru津軽 in min’yō (p. 82 n. 2), and Table 4.1’s kudoki scale d-e-f-a is the Nagoya form. Fact 3.1 states the historical grouping.
