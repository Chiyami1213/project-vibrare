1Meiji: New Words, New Music

1.1“Japanese” “Music”

Two Modern Concepts
Two ideas are modern. Ongaku音楽, once used for Chinese (foreign) orchestral sound, widened in the 1880s and 1890s to mean “music” in the Western sense; before that no Japanese word covered all organized sound. And “Japanese music” (hōgaku邦楽) became a category only when set against “the West”.
(1.1)

Tokita 346.

G Edo-period foreign music, such as Chinese instrumental music among merchants or Dutch music heard at Nagasaki長崎, was too local to define a contrast, and people identified with their domain and rank more than with a nation. The idea of “Japanese music” was set out by educators, policy-makers and intellectuals, who debated whether Japan should choose Western music, Japanese music or a blend Tokita 346.

Western music arrived with the military: Perry’s Black Ships (1853) brought brass bands, and with Christian hymns these became tools of Western power that the shogunate and the Meiji明治 government chose to emulate Tokita 345.

1.2Music Education

Founding Music Education
In 1879 Izawa Shūji伊沢修二 proposed a national school music curriculum, a harmonious blend of the best of East and West. In 1880 Luther Whiting Mason of Boston came by invitation to found the Tokyo Music School東京音楽学校, which enrolled its first 22 students that October.
(1.2)

Tokita 364.

Problems abounded: instruments and lessons were too costly for all but the elite, teachers were few, and there was no composition teaching, although students had to write songs for textbooks. Method and imitation were valued over creativity Tokita 364.

G Herd stresses that Meiji intellectuals saw Western music as a way to understand the West, not as a replacement for native styles, continuing the old habit of borrowing from China and Korea Tokita 364.

2School Songs and New Scales

2.1Shōka

School Songs
From the 1880s shōka唱歌, school songs, taught the nation through lyrics, through sound and through the body, by unison singing. They spread Western meter, especially the steady accented duple of marches, and new pentatonic scales that still shape popular tunes a century later.
(2.1)

Tokita 346–347.

The first government collection, Shōgaku shōka-shū小学唱歌集 (1881–85, three volumes), shows Meiji明治 ambivalence: gagaku雅楽 melodies with Western harmony, Japanese words to Auld Lang Syne and Stephen Foster, and jiuta地歌 tunes in the in陰 mode with piano accompaniment. Tamura Torazō’s ten volumes (1902) set the standard: simple lyrics, pentatonic tunes and European diatonic harmony Tokita 365.

2.2Pentatonic Major and Minor

Two New Scales
The school-song scale do–re–mi–sol–la came partly from Scottish tunes in American textbooks and partly from adapting native pentatonic modes to Western tonality by shifting the tonic. Its minor version, do–re–mi♭–sol–la♭, was invented in the early twentieth century under the influence of the in (miyako-bushi都節) mode. Scholars call them the pentatonic major and pentatonic minor.
(2.2)

Tokita 346.

G Miki calls the major form the yonanukiヨナ抜き scale (“fourth and seventh removed”), a Meiji “recreation” of the old ro呂 scale of gagaku, now the representative Japanese scale through enka演歌 Miki 2. See Where the Sources Differ.

Worked Example 2.3 · Find the Old Mode in a New Scale constructed

The phrase on the right ends on c, which a Meiji teacher would hear as do. Name its scale, then find which traditional mode uses the same pitches and what the shift of tonic changes.

Source: A constructed phrase; the scales follow Yano and Hosokawa in Tokita & Hughes 2017, p. 346, and the linked-fourth method of Chapter 9.

  1. Assumptions. Pitches: c d e♭ g a♭.
  2. Missing Degrees. No fourth, no seventh: the yonanuki pattern.
  3. Scale. Scale: pentatonic minor on c.
  4. Old Mode. Same pitches as in on g.
  5. What Changed. Same pitches, new tonic, Western harmony.
Answer
The phrase uses c d e♭ g a♭: no fourth or seventh, with flat third and sixth, so it is the pentatonic minor. The same pitches form in (miyako-bushi) on g, with frames g–c and d–g. Hearing c as do moves the tonic and lets the tune carry Western minor harmony: the adaptation Yano and Hosokawa describe.
(E2.6)

3Song Before the Mass Media

3.1Sōshi Enka and Shosei-bushi

Sōshi Enka
The first shōka唱歌-derived popular song was sōshi enka壮士演歌 of the 1890s: mixed melodies (school, folk and military songs), political lyrics backing the civil rights movement or hailing victory over China (1894–95), and a style between shouting and singing, inherited from the yomiuri読売 newspaper hawkers.
(3.1)

Tokita 347.

After 1900 the genre became shosei-bushi書生節 (student songs), humorous and sentimental; violins joined around 1910, and from the mid-1920s some singers used less folk vibrato and ornament. Like Victorian broadside ballads, it led to mass-produced song after the late 1920s Tokita 347.

Nakayama Shinpei中山晋平 (1887–1952), from the Tokyo Music School東京音楽学校, was the first major composer for street singers; his Kachūsha no utaカチューシャの唄 (1914), for a play based on Tolstoy, spread through singers, sheet music and records. He was also central to shin-min’yō新民謡 and to dōyō童謡, artistic children’s songs meant as an alternative to the ministry’s school songs Tokita 347–348.

3.2Rōkyoku

Much of the public still loved shamisen三味線 song. The pleasure quarters remained centers of fads, and with conscription drawing soldiers from everywhere, urban entertainers absorbed far-flung rural songs Tokita 348.

Rōkyoku
Rōkyoku浪曲 (naniwa-bushi浪花節), shamisen-accompanied speech and song, seized new heroic stories and the new record industry. Around 1910 three stars sold over 100,000 records, almost 40 percent of all sales; in 1932, 58 percent of Japanese enjoyed rōkyoku, far more than any other genre.
(3.2)

Tokita 348.

G Despite bourgeois scorn it lasted in working-class culture into the 1950s, and its stories and vocal style passed into enka演歌 Tokita 348.

4Jazz Age and War

4.1After the Earthquake

The Great Kantō Earthquake of 1923 divided modern Japanese history much as the First World War did in Europe Tokita 348–349.

Tokita 349.

The Assembly Line
The record companies ran an assembly line: composer, lyricist, arranger, orchestra and singer each kept to a fixed role, and each song belonged to one singer (the one-song-one-singer system); covers were rare unless the song was foreign. It declined only with the singer-songwriters of the 1970s.
(4.1)

Tokita 349.

4.2Jazz, Blues and Koga Melody

“Jazz” meant modernity and decadence and covered almost any American or European popular music, played in dance halls and revue theaters by musicians from cinemas, ex-military bands and department-store boys’ bands Tokita 349. In the mid-1930s Hattori Ryōichi服部良一 (1907–93) turned “blues” (burūsuブルース) into a torch song with saxophone and clarinet Tokita 350.

Koga Melody
Koga Masao古賀政男 (1904–78) revived the shosei-bushi tradition, mixing Edo江戸 vocal style with guitar solo or mandolin ensemble. This “Koga melody” filled mainstream song and much of it was later classed as enka演歌. An early example: Sake wa namida ka tameiki ka酒は涙か溜息か.
(4.2)

Tokita 350.

The crooner Fujiyama Ichirō藤山一郎 (1911–93), trained at the Tokyo Music School東京音楽学校, left bel canto behind and made the microphone standard from the 1930s; geisha singers of new folk songs learned to sing in halls and studios for a general audience Tokita 350.

4.3Music at War

From 1934 the Department of Domestic Security censored records, removing proletarian songs, sensual vocals and anti-military themes. Newspapers held contests for propaganda songs, some with over 100,000 entries. Songs praised soldiers and comrades or longed for mothers, wives and home, but never sweethearts Tokita 350–351.

Enemy Music
G In 1940 dance halls closed and American, British and French music was banned as “enemy music”; in 1941 saxophones, muted trumpets, banjos, steel guitars and ukuleles were prohibited. Some arrangers hid jazz under folk melodies and patriotic lyrics.
(4.3)

Tokita 351. Herd: from July 1937 an almost complete ban on foreign music, with exceptions for German classics Tokita 371.

5Occupation to Rock

5.1The Boogie-Woogie Years

Under the Occupation American music was welcomed and militaristic music banned; jazz came directly through bases and radio. It split into commercial big bands with a female singer and “artistic” bebop Tokita 351–352.

Two Streams
Hattori’s Tokyo boogie woogie東京ブギウギ (1949) became the soundtrack of the Occupation, while Koga Masao’s melancholy returned. The Hattori–Koga polarity then dominated postwar song; the Koga line absorbed rōkyoku浪曲, shamisen三味線 song and folk song into what would be called enka演歌.
(5.1)

Tokita 352. Malm dates the song 1946 Malm 272; see Where the Sources Differ.

Country and western (wesutanウエスタン) appealed to well-off Tokyo youth; jazz pianist Nakamura Hachidai中村八大 brought an American pop sound to hits such as Ue o muite arukō上を向いて歩こう (“Sukiyaki”) Tokita 352.

G Early rockers thought Japanese too parochial for rebellion and sang in English; folk singers wrote Japanese lyrics full of satire. Happī Endo bridged the gap, and since the 1970s the usual answer has been Japanese lyrics with faux-English hooks.

6Enka, Idols and Okinawan Pop

6.1Enka

Enka
As youth “pops” spread, older styles in Koga’s line were lumped together in the late 1960s as enka演歌, probably after the enkashi演歌師 who strolled bars with guitar, violin or accordion. Enka became “the heart of the Japanese”, marked by tears, longing and nostalgia, pentatonic-centered scales, and traditional vocal techniques: yuriユリ, a wide swinging vibrato, and kobushi小節, fine ornament.
(6.1)

Tokita 354–355.

G Its heroines are bar hostesses, as geisha were for Edo江戸 song; some songs quote regional min’yō民謡, others take the blues saxophone. As a genre it is loose. In 1978, 31 percent of Japanese named enka their favorite, against 16 percent for Western-style pop and 6 percent for rock; by 1999 pops and rock had edged ahead Tokita 355. Such songs are also called natsu-mero懐メロ, “nostalgic melodies” Tokita 355.

6.2Idols and J-pop

In 1967 sales of Japanese artists passed foreign ones for the first time since the war, and covers of Western songs fell away. Japan’s record industry has been the world’s second largest since the 1980s Tokita 355–356, 359.

Idols
From the mid-1970s the center of sales has been aidoruアイドル, “idol” singers in their early or mid teens chosen first for looks: televisual images tied to media campaigns and to kawaii可愛い (cute) culture. Since the 1990s J-pop covers all pop, rock and idol music made in Japan, with enka as its opposite.
(6.2)

Tokita 356. Onyanko Club (1980s) and Morning Musume (1990s–2000s) were the most successful idol groups Tokita 356.

6.3Okinawan Pop

Okinawan new songs sold almost only in Okinawa沖縄 until Kina Shōkichi喜納昌吉 and Champloose recorded Haisai ojisanハイサイおじさん in the mid-1970s, with the Okinawan scale, voice and sanshin三線 over a rock band Tokita 356–357. See Chapter 10.

G Two kinds of Okinawan performer reached the mainland: idols such as Amuro Namie安室奈美恵, whose music owes nothing to Okinawa, and overtly Okinawan groups such as the Nenes, the Rinken Band and Begin. Okinawan music became “Japan’s world music”, a “domestic Other”; the mainland band The Boom’s Shimauta島唄 is often taken for an Okinawan song Tokita 357.

7Western Art Music: The Pioneers

7.1First Steps

Herd’s subject is yōgaku洋楽, Western-style music by Japanese or Western composers, and the rise of the sakkyoku-ka作曲家, the composer of “art music” (geijutsu ongaku芸術音楽) Tokita 363.

The first serious works, Kōda Nobuko’s Violin Sonata (1897) and Taki Rentarō’s two piano pieces (1900), were “courageous, but pedantic, exercises”. Records were imported from 1897 and made in Japan from 1909; Yamaha (founded 1897) mass-produced affordable instruments Tokita 365.

7.2Yamada and Fujii

Two Pioneers
Yamada Kōsaku山田耕筰 (1886–1965), trained in Berlin (1910–13), moved from German style to Scriabin and impressionism, then, after studying Japanese music in 1918–19, to quartal harmonies, quasi-pentatonic melody and sparse textures. Fujii Kiyomi藤井清水 (1899–1944) stayed home and wrote hundreds of songs in min’yō民謡 style for Western voice and instruments.
(7.1)

Tokita 365–367.

G Herd traces today’s two factions to them: Yamada’s experiments with form and harmony freed Western-style composition, while Fujii’s ethnological approach helped gendai hōgaku現代邦楽, new music for Japanese instruments, emerge Tokita 367. Miyagi Michio宮城道雄 and Nakao Tozan中尾都山 also drew on Western music (Chapters 5 and 6).

7.3The Prewar Nationalists

In 1930 composers formed the Shinkō Sakkyokuka Renmei新興作曲家連盟 (Federation of Newly Rising Composers) to build national styles. In 1935 the conductor Konoe Hidemaro近衛秀麿 said orchestras must reflect common tastes; orchestras played popular songs, tangos and gagaku雅楽, and began to live on ticket sales Tokita 368.

8Postwar Composers

8.1The Neo-nationalists

After the war most composers rejected the prewar nationalists as collaborators, yet the 1950s brought a “neo-nationalist” search for independence from foreign models, drawing on folk song, theater, social issues and religion Tokita 371–372.

Dodecaphonic Gagaku
G Matsudaira built harmony from the ten standard shō clusters, mostly quartal with seconds, and heard the friction of ryo呂 and ritsu律 modes in gagaku as a model for bimodality. Herd presents his “dodecaphonic gagaku” as proof that traditional elements suit avant-garde techniques.
(8.1)

Tokita 374.

8.2Takemitsu and the Experimental Workshop

The Experimental Workshop (Jikken Kōbō実験工房), founded in 1949 by fourteen musicians, artists, poets and engineers, mostly without formal music training, combined tape and live music with painting, film and poetry Tokita 375.

Takemitsu武満徹
Takemitsu Tōru武満徹 (1930–96) set sound in opposition to silence, not just in balance with it (Piano Distance, 1961). November Steps (1967) for biwa琵琶, shakuhachi尺八 and orchestra sets abrupt bursts from the traditional soloists against rich orchestral harmony: sound and silence, and abstractly Japan and the West.
(8.2)

Tokita 376. Malm credits November Steps with stirring international interest in the biwa Malm 155.

Yuasa Jōji湯浅譲二 drew on nō能 time and Bashō芭蕉; Ichiyanagi Toshi’s Piano Media (1972) helped found a Japanese minimalism Tokita 376–377.

8.3New Music for Japanese Instruments

Gendai Hōgaku
In 1964 Miki Minoru三木稔 (b. 1930), “the godfather of gendai hōgaku現代邦楽”, founded the Nihon Ongaku Shūdan日本音楽集団 (Pro Musica Nipponia) with 13 others, and wrote for Japanese instruments from solos to concertos for 20-string koto箏 and operas. Takemitsu’s gagaku work Shūteiga秋庭歌 (1979) and others followed.
(8.3)

Tokita 374, 377.

beyond the main source

Miki is also the author of this guide’s instrument cross-check source. He writes that Pro Musica Nipponia was formed because, outside gagaku, Japan had no mixed ensemble of its own instruments, and that its players can now perform with Western orchestras without losing their Japanese identity Miki 1. He developed the 20-string koto with Nosaka Keiko野坂惠子 in 1969, extended to 21 strings in 1971 Miki 126, and argues that Japanese instruments should keep their pentatonic character rather than follow Western functional harmony Miki 2.

Where the Sources Differ

Errors and disagreements found while cross-reading, grouped by source, with the check that exposed each one. The guide text uses the form named in each check.

Malm 2000

When Tokyo boogie woogie appeared. Malm: “Tokyo Boogie Woogie,” 1946 (p. 272).
Check: Yano and Hosokawa: 1949 (Tokita 352). Neither gives a source; the guide follows Tokita.
How old enka is. Malm: enka, the most long-lasting music-hall genre, is still going strong, citing songs of 1936 and 1946 (p. 272).
Check: Yano and Hosokawa: Meiji明治 sōshi enka faded after 1900; the modern term enka was applied in the late 1960s to older styles in Koga’s line (Tokita 347, 355). The name links them, but the genres differ.

Miki 2008

Where the pentatonic major came from. Miki: the ro scale of gagaku雅楽, no longer used there, was “recreated” in Meiji as the yonanuki scale (p. 2).
Check: Yano and Hosokawa: partly Scottish tunes in American textbooks, partly native modes adapted to Western tonality by shifting the tonic (Tokita 346). Different explanations, not exclusive.

Tokita and Hughes 2017

When visual kei was born. Yano and Hosokawa place the birth of visual kei in the late 1990s, after the success of X (later X Japan) (p. 354).
Check: Their own wording implies X succeeded before it was renamed, which happened in the early 1990s, so the late 1980s seems likelier. Treat the date with caution.
A decade before Cage. Herd: almost a decade before Cage became popular in Japan in the early 1960s, Matsudaira was already using open form (p. 374).
Check: Her example, Somaksah, is dated 1961, the same period. The claim may rest on earlier works she does not name.
