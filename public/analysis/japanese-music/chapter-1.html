1What Is “Japanese Music”?

1.1Musics in the Plural

Tokita and Hughes open by questioning the label itself. Japan has never been one culture or one people, so its music cannot be one thing either; they prefer to speak of “Japanese musics” Tokita 1–2.

  • Imported, then naturalized. Virtually every Japanese instrument came from China in historical times, and court music (gagaku雅楽) began as a foreign genre. When it started to count as “Japanese” is itself a question Tokita 1.
  • Minorities inside Japan. Okinawans, the Ainuアイヌ and Koreans in Japan have their own musics, which a single national label hides Tokita 2.
  • Hybrid forms. Hybrid forms make “traditional”, “pop” and “Western” hard to separate: enka演歌, J-pop and new music for Japanese instruments all mix sources Tokita 1, 4.

Malm’s 1959 survey (revised 2000) frames the same field as Japan “supporting two musical cultures at one time”, Western music and traditional music. He also notes a third “limbo”: modern works that use traditional materials in a new way Malm 29–30.

1.2Hōgaku, Yōgaku and Minzoku Ongaku

In the twentieth century, Japanese music came to be defined against Western music Tokita 1–2:

The Modern Categories
hōgaku邦楽 (national music) is set against yōgaku洋楽 (Western music). Every other music of the world goes into minzoku ongaku民族音楽.
(1.1)
  • Folk song was left out. Scholars’ use of hōgaku long excluded folk music, which placed it outside “serious” musicology. Ethnologists did study folk performing arts (minzoku geinō民俗芸能) Tokita 2–3.
  • A record-shop sense. Shops now use hōgaku for Western-style pop by Japanese artists and yōgaku for foreign recordings. This is separate from the scholarly sense Tokita 3.
  • A wider view. Postwar ethnomusicologists, led by Koizumi Fumio小泉文夫, began to compare Japanese music with the musics of other cultures, not only the West Tokita 3.

1.3Ga and Zoku

An older pair of categories sorts music by social register Tokita 11:

Ga and Zoku
ga雅 (elegant, of the court) versus zoku俗 (of the common people). Gagaku is literally “ga music”. The everyday koto箏 is zokusō俗箏, as opposed to the court koto, gakusō楽箏. Shamisen songs of the licensed quarters are zokkyoku俗曲.
(1.2)

G Edo-period Confucian scholars tended to treat anything Japanese as zoku and Chinese music as ga. The split is rough: nobles and commoners shared many tastes, and today zoku has lost its sense of “vulgar” and means something like “popular” Tokita 11.

2.2Music Through the Periods

Step through the periods with the buttons or the arrow keys. Each card brings together what both books say about that period.

Jōmon and Yayoi · c. 12,000 BCE–300 CE

  • Early influences came from northern Asia (shamanic ritual, long narratives) and from Southeast Asia (scales and textures shared with Island East Asia) Tokita 4.
  • Wet-field rice farming arrived from China in the Yayoi period, along with large movements of people Tokita 4.
  • Instruments known: stone and clay flutes, drums, bronze bells and zithers Tokita 4. The bronze bells (dōtaku銅鐸) have a unique shape and an unknown function. Clay tomb figures (haniwa埴輪) show zithers, drums and singers Malm 32.

Kofun · c. 300–710

  • Peoples and culture came in through the Korean peninsula. Chinese culture arrived first via Korea, then directly from China from the 600s Tokita 4–5.
  • A sixth-century chronicle lists musicians among scholars sent from Paekche, in southwest Korea Malm 31.
  • The first Japanese chronicles, the Kojiki古事記 (712) and Nihon shoki日本書紀 (720), hold some two hundred poems, many clearly sung. They also tell the myth of Ama no Uzume天宇受売命, whose dance lured the sun goddess out of her cave Malm 31–32.

Nara · 710–94

  • Tang China (618–907) shaped Japan deeply. Chinese writing arrived, which made musical notation possible, and so did Buddhism Tokita 5.
  • The Shōsōin正倉院 repository in Nara still holds more than one hundred eighth-century instruments, most of continental origin Tokita 28.
  • Malm calls this the first international period. Court musicians all came from China or Korea and played in the original style, and hundreds of musicians and dancers performed at the dedication of Tōdaiji東大寺 Malm 33.

Heian · 794–1185/92

  • Imported music was gradually naturalized. Gagaku雅楽 held tōgaku唐楽 from the Tang court and komagaku高麗楽 from Korea, along with Japanese songs such as saibara催馬楽 Tokita 4.
  • Aristocrats played biwa琵琶, koto箏 and ryūteki龍笛 flute as amateurs Tokita 12. In The Tale of Genji源氏物語, every refined person plays one or two instruments Malm 34–35.
  • By now the musicians were more often Japanese, and the music took on Japanese traits. Malm calls the Heian period the heyday of court music Malm 35.

Kamakura · 1185/92–1333

  • Military rule began. The Tale of the Heike平家物語, written for recitation to the biwa, became the model of warrior virtue Malm 36.
  • The military aristocracy favored heikyoku平曲 (Heike平家 narrative) Tokita 11.
  • Court music declined. Comic dengaku田楽 dances and Buddhist chant grew in favor, and Malm sees a new emphasis on vocal and dramatic music Malm 37.

Muromachi · 1333–1573

  • Nō能 took shape under Kan’ami観阿弥 and his son Zeami世阿弥, with the shogun Yoshimitsu足利義満 as patron Tokita 5, 11 Malm 38.
  • The blind formed the Tōdō-za当道座 guild in the fourteenth century, with government support Tokita 15.
  • Short songs (kouta小唄) and street storytellers appeared, the forerunners of jōruri浄瑠璃. Wandering priests began playing a simple bamboo shakuhachi尺八 Malm 38.

Momoyama · 1573–1600

  • The sanshin三線 came from the Ryukyu琉球 Islands and was reshaped into the shamisen三味線 in the late sixteenth century Tokita 5–6 Malm 39.
  • Drum-making reached a level at which a Momoyama drum is prized as a Stradivarius is in the West Malm 39.
  • The “Christian Century” (1543–1636) brought European contact before Japan closed itself off Tokita 5.

Edo (Tokugawa) · 1600–1868

  • Japan was almost totally isolated. Small elite circles still played Ming and Qing Chinese music (minshingaku明清楽) Tokita 5.
  • Shamisen music, koto and shakuhachi flourished, and the puppet theater (bunraku文楽) and kabuki歌舞伎 dominated the age. The Genroku元禄 era (1688–1703) was the high point of the pleasure quarters Malm 40–41 Tokita 8.
  • Women were banned from public performance in 1629 Tokita 13. Amateur demand for lessons gave rise to the iemoto家元 system Tokita 15.

Meiji and After · 1868–present

  • The first Western music of the Meiji era was military band music. School music soon favored Western music, and Malm blames tonic and dominant harmony for deadening the old melodic style Malm 42.
  • In 1871 the government abolished the guild monopolies of the blind (Tōdō-za) and of the Fuke普化 shakuhachi sect Tokita 15. The shamisen was kept out of the school curriculum Tokita 10–11.
  • After the war came national theaters, the Cultural Properties Protection Law (1950) and Living National Treasures. In 2002 Japanese instruments became required in middle school Tokita 16, 29.

2.3Two Long Trends

Malm closes his survey with two general trends Malm 44:

Two Long Trends (Malm)
G Vocal music dominates. Every instrument developed under the aegis of the voice, and the first instrumental solos were interludes between verses. Theatrical music grows steadily, from ancient ceremonial roles to its peak in the Edo period.
(2.1)

Tokita and Hughes agree from another angle. There was very little purely instrumental music, and music was rarely an independent art until very recently, which is largely why it fared badly under modernization Tokita 25. Miki, writing as a composer, makes the same point about instruments: each one grew out of its role in vocal music, which explains their many voice-like techniques Miki 3.

G Tokita and Hughes add a third pattern. Japan has long told the native apart from the imported: first China, and from the Meiji era on, “the West”. The slogan datsua nyūō脱亜入欧 (“leave Asia, enter Europe”) sums up the second shift Tokita 3.

3Social Contexts and Transmission

3.1Patrons and Audiences

Japanese music is highly dependent on context. In nō能, kabuki歌舞伎, bunraku文楽 and classical dance, the text and the staging come first, and the music supports the narrated meaning Tokita 10. The patrons changed with the ruling class Tokita 11–12:

  • Court nobility (Heian平安): gagaku雅楽 and shōmyō声明 (Buddhist chant).
  • Military aristocracy (13th–19th c.): heikyoku平曲 and nō.
  • Edo江戸 townspeople: koto箏 and shamisen三味線 music, puppet and kabuki theaters.
  • Modern media: records and radio carried nationalist biwa琵琶 music and naniwa-bushi浪花節.

G Nobles also enjoyed the arts of commoners. The shogun Yoshimitsu足利義満 patronized the nō of Kan’ami観阿弥 and Zeami世阿弥, and Emperor Go-Shirakawa後白河 loved the imayō今様 songs of female shirabyōshi白拍子 performers Tokita 11. Music begins in ritual and then becomes secular, commercial and a commodity. Subscription nō (kanjin nō勧進能), documented from the fourteenth century, already paid the actors Tokita 12.

3.2Professionals, the Blind and Gender

  • Court musicians. They were a servant class, but a privileged one, and hereditary. Some families keep centuries-long lines Tokita 13.
  • Outcaste performers. Male storytellers and actors were by definition of outcaste status, like leatherworkers (including drum-makers) Tokita 13.
  • Blind musicians. Blindness was stigmatized, though it was partly offset by a reputed ability to reach the spirit world. The guild of the blind, the Tōdō-za当道座 (14th c.), controlled heikyoku; in the Edo period its members moved to koto and shamisen. Its monopoly ended in 1871 Tokita 13, 15.
  • Gender. Women were banned from public performance in 1629. Nō, kabuki and bunraku remain almost all male professionally, and women singers are expected to sing near the male register in every genre except folk song Tokita 13–14.

3.3The Iemoto System

Iemoto Hierarchy
iemoto家元 (household head) → natori名取 (licensed name-takers) → amateur students. Students pay fees for lessons, for each step up and for the professional name, and part of every fee flows up the pyramid Tokita 15–16.
(3.1)
  • Origins. The system took shape in the Edo period, when townspeople (chōnin町人) took lessons as a cultivated pastime. It is the main structure in shamisen music, Japanese dance, tea ceremony and flower arranging Tokita 15.
  • Spread. In the Meiji明治 period satsuma-薩摩 and chikuzen-biwa筑前琵琶 took it up, and after the war even folk song (min’yō民謡) did Tokita 15.
  • G The debate. Critics call it autocratic, exploitative and conservative, yet it clearly helped many genres survive. Malm, from the 1950s, stresses the loyalty owed to one’s sensei先生: leaving a teacher is a grave breach Tokita 16 Malm 44.
  • Newer routes. NHK runs a program that trains and certifies performers, the national theaters run training schools, and culture centers offer lessons to amateurs Tokita 16.

3.4Oral Transmission and Notation

How Music Is Passed On
Transmission (denshō伝承) is mostly oral (kuden口伝). Instruments are first learned through sung mnemonic syllables, shōga唱歌, such as ohyarai houhouhi on the nō flute or to ra ro ruro on the ryūteki龍笛.
(3.2)

Why was notation not the main tool Tokita 17–18?

  • Many musicians were blind.
  • Teachers wanted to limit learning to paying students.
  • Formulaic genres are easy to learn by ear. Drum parts, for example, are learned as strings of named patterns or recited strokes (chirikara chiripopo…).

Notation now exists for every genre, and for gagaku it dates back to the eighth century. Most notations still keep an oral layer, and no two Japanese instruments use quite the same system, not even within the tōgaku唐楽 ensemble Tokita 17–18. Miki gives a composer’s summary Miki 3:

  • Shinobue篠笛 and koto mnemonics show the melodic line, with no fingerings or string numbers.
  • Shakuhachi尺八 mnemonics show fingerings.
  • Shamisen mnemonics show both the string and the technique.

Secret teachings (hiden秘伝) kept Zeami’s treatises unknown outside his school until the late nineteenth century. Their counterpart is “stealing the art” (gei o nusumu芸を盗む), learning by watching and listening, which is not meant pejoratively Tokita 18.

4Tone Systems

4.1The Chinese Inheritance: Ryo and Ritsu

R Court music and Buddhist chant brought Chinese pitch, scale and mode theory, close to Pythagorean pitch calculation. Japan reduced the Tang modes to two classes Tokita 19:

Ryo and Ritsu
ryo呂: the third degree lies a major third above the final. ritsu律: the third degree lies a minor third above the final.
(4.1)

Malm counts what remained once the theory had been “Japanized”: twelve basic tones (named from D upward), two scale structures and six modes, three ryo and three ritsu Malm 113–114. The names of the twelve tones are still used in koto箏 music:

  • Transposition changes the piece. In gagaku雅楽, a melody moved to another mode becomes a new composition, a watashimono渡物 (“crossed over”). Pieces move only within the ryo modes or within the ritsu modes, never between them Malm 115.
  • G Theory and practice diverged. Because the Chinese name kyū急 came to mean the final, some string parts had to shift notes by a semitone. In today’s gagaku different instruments may in effect be playing in different keys Tokita 19.

4.2Koizumi’s Tetrachords

R Most scholars now follow Koizumi Fumio小泉文夫 (1958, 1977), who built on Uehara Rokushirō上原六四郎 (1895) and Robert Lachmann (1929) Tokita 19:

The Tetrachord
A tetrachord is three notes: two nuclear tones (kakuon核音) a perfect fourth apart, framing one variable infix.
(4.2)
Koizumi’s Four Tetrachords
TetrachordOn CSteps (semitones)
min’yō民謡C E♭ F+3, +2
miyako-bushi都節C D♭ F+1, +4
ritsuC D F+2, +3
ryūkyū琉球C E F+4, +1
(4.3)

The pitches come from Tokita and Hughes and the semitone steps from Kawase, who encodes each type as a pair of intervals Tokita 20 Kawase. Miki’s figure brackets the same four shapes Miki 2.

  • No single tonic. Either note of the fourth can be a nuclear tone, a goal of melodic movement. There is no single tonic as in the West Tokita 20.
  • Absolute pitch hardly matters. The pitches on C only illustrate the model; outside gagaku, absolute pitch means little Tokita 20.
  • G Distinctive, not unique. The shared tetrachord structure is characteristic of Japanese music and sets it apart from the most typical Chinese pentatonic mode, though Japan is not the only place it occurs Tokita 20.

4.3Five Scales Built from Tetrachords

Two tetrachords linked a whole step apart (disjunct) make a pentatonic octave. Miki gives five scales as the ones Japanese instruments were made to play Miki 2:

Five Japanese Scales (Miki Fig. I.1)
ScaleOn CBuilt From
yō陽C E♭ F | G B♭ Ctwo min’yō tetrachords
ro呂C D E G A C(no tetrachord brackets in Miki)
ritsuC D F | G A Ctwo ritsu tetrachords
in陰 (miyako-bushi)C D♭ F | G A♭ Ctwo miyako-bushi tetrachords

(4.4)

What each scale is used for, in Miki’s account Miki 2:

  • Yō: the oldest Japanese scale, and still the main scale of folk song.
  • Ro: came with gagaku but is no longer used there. It was “recreated” in the Meiji明治 period as the yonanukiヨナ抜き scale and now stands for Japan through enka演歌.
  • Ritsu: the basis of present-day gagaku.
  • In (miyako-bushi): formed in the Edo江戸 period by lowering some pitches; the most “Japanese” scale.
  • Ryūkyū: the Okinawan scale, which seems to come from the yō scale with some pitches raised.

G In and yō. These are Uehara’s names. Many scholars use in for Koizumi’s miyako-bushi and yō for his min’yō; Tokita and Hughes avoid the pair for consistency Tokita 20 n. 5. 4.4Infix Substitution and Analysis with Few Notes

R In a miyako-bushi scale the infixes tend to act as downward leading tones. In rising passages they may be swapped for the notes a whole step below the upper nuclear tone, turning miyako-bushi tetrachords into min’yō ones. Similar swaps occur in the other types, as in the Western melodic minor. This is why careful scholars call these modes, not scales Tokita 20.

Infix Substitution (Tokita)
Falling: C D♭ F (miyako-bushi). Rising: C E♭ F (min’yō). The nuclear tones stay; only the infix moves.
(4.5)

Malm shows the same idea working as an analysis tool. Japanese music does not need a full scale or harmony to set up a tonality. It moves between tone centers, usually a fourth or fifth apart, each supported by a neighbor a whole or half step away Malm 264. So one three-note unit is enough to tell the scale:

4.5Modern Yonanuki Scales

Twentieth-century hybrids such as enka and “new folk songs” keep a Japanese identity through pentatonic melody but treat one nuclear tone as a Western-style tonic, so that chords can be added Tokita 20:

Yonanuki Scales
Yonanuki chō-onkaiヨナ抜き長音階 (“pentatonic major”): C D E G A. Yonanuki tan’onkaiヨナ抜き短音階 (“pentatonic minor”): C D E♭ G A♭. Yonanuki means the fourth (yo四) and seventh (na七) degrees removed (nuki抜き) Miki 243 n. 3.
(4.6)

G These differ from the traditional modes only in tonal function, with C as tonic, not in their interval structure Tokita 20. Compare the yonanuki minor (C D E♭ G A♭) with the in scale on G (G A♭ C D E♭): the same five pitch classes, read from a different tone.

G Before Western influence there was no common system for naming absolute pitches or scale degrees. The gagaku tone names were sometimes borrowed by more theoretically minded musicians Tokita 20.

4.6Limits of the Model

  • One theorist’s construct. The composer and musicologist Shibata Minao柴田南雄 proposed a quite different theory (1978) Tokita 20.
  • Musicians think in tunings. Players do not talk in Koizumi’s terms (unless they have read him). String players speak of tunings, not scales Tokita 20.
  • Intonation varies. The pitch of the infix can shift even within one performance. Staff notation is only an approximation Tokita 20. Miki adds that shakuhachi尺八 meri notes and koto notes that need a moved bridge call for special care Miki 2.
beyond the main source

Evidence from folk song data. Kawase digitized 2,383 songs (474,191 tones) from the Nihon Min’yō Taikan日本民謡大観 across 25 old provinces of Kyushu九州, Chugoku中国地方 and Shikoku四国. He counted every two-step pitch move that forms one of Koizumi’s tetrachords and ran a principal components analysis Kawase.

  • Pitch moves stay mostly within ±5 semitones, the span of a perfect fourth, and rise and fall about equally.
  • The second component separates min’yō and ritsu (whole-step neighbors) from miyako-bushi and ryūkyū (half-step neighbors).
  • Clustering grouped the provinces by geography and cultural background, which suggests that tetrachord habits spread along land and sea routes.

Citing Koizumi (1977), Kawase also says that min’yō and ritsu are the most frequent folk tetrachords, while miyako-bushi and ryūkyū grew out of them later.

A folk-style melody falls through the notes E5 C5 B4 | A4 F4 E4, and a later rising phrase uses E4 G4 A4 | B4 D5 E5. Name the tetrachords, the mode of the falling phrase, and explain the rising phrase.

Source: Method from Tokita & Hughes 2017, p. 19–20 (tetrachords, infix substitution) and Malm 2000, p. 264, Fig. 36 (three-note units). The fragment is constructed.

  1. Assumptions. Assumptions: nuclear tones a perfect fourth apart; one infix names the tetrachord; absolute pitch is irrelevant.
  2. Nuclear Tones. Nuclear tones: E4–A4 and B4–E5 (each a perfect fourth). The two tetrachords are linked by the whole step A–B (disjunct).
  3. Infix Interval. Infix steps: E→F = +1, then F→A = +4 (and B→C = +1, C→E = +4): the pattern (+1, +4).
  4. Tetrachord Type. Both tetrachords are miyako-bushi (+1, +4).
  5. Mode. Falling phrase: in (miyako-bushi) mode on E, E F A B C E.
  6. Rising Phrase. Rising phrase: E G A | B D E (min’yō infixes); same nuclear tones, same mode.

5Texture, Meter and Form

5.1Heterophony and Ornament

Heterophony
Japanese music is overwhelmingly monophonic. Ensembles play in heterophony, each part a variation of one melody suited to its instrument. Voice and accompaniment keep the relation called tsukazu hanarezu付かず離れず (“not too close, not too distant”).
(5.1)
  • Wind instruments play smooth lines and plucked strings choppier ones, as in the sankyoku三曲 trio of koto箏, shamisen三味線 and shakuhachi尺八 Tokita 24.
  • True polyphony, when it occurs, is usually a countermelody (for example a second koto part) that lines up with the main melody only at the cadence Tokita 24.
  • Because the melody unfolds horizontally, ornament matters, and it is diagnostic of each genre. Unlike bel canto vibrato, it is real ornament rather than a constant backdrop Tokita 24.
  • Malm describes Heian平安 gagaku雅楽 as “a block of sound” that does not move but lets other things move through it Malm 35.

5.2Meter and Rhythm

Meter (Tokita)
Metric Japanese music is overwhelmingly duple (6/8 included); there is virtually no triple meter. Free rhythm is also common, and a few gagaku dance pieces are in quintuple meter Tokita 24.
(5.2)

Malm gives the rhythmic units of gagaku theory Malm 115:

  • Nobebyōshi延拍子: units of eight beats, for slow pieces.
  • Hayabyōshi早拍子: units of four beats, for medium tempo.
  • Tadabyōshi只拍子: units of two and four mixed.
  • Yatarabyōshi夜多羅拍子: alternate bars of two and three beats, used when a fast tadabyōshi piece is danced.

G Malm also warns that folk songs written in two or four often have an inner beat “somewhere between duple and triple”. Western bar lines in a transcription distort the phrase lengths Malm 265.

5.3Patterns, Variation and Improvisation

  • Patterns. Continuity comes from formulaic material, named patterns like the kata型 of dance. Kyokusetsu曲節 usually means longer, section-length passages and senritsukei旋律型 shorter phrases Tokita 24.
  • Narrative delivery. Hirano’s three styles are widely used in research: ginshō吟唱 (close to speech), rōshō朗唱 (chant) and eishō詠唱 (aria). Tokumaru calls quotation from other genres intertextuality Tokita 24.
  • Improvisation. It is nearly absent. The exceptions are folk tsugaru-jamisen津軽三味線 and the Tokyo-area matsuri-bayashi祭囃子 festival music; elsewhere flexibility amounts to variation or interpretation Tokita 24.

5.4Folk Song Form

  • Strophic. Malm’s examples are binary (A–B) and strophic, the same music for every verse. Ternary (A–B–A) or three-phrase (A–B–C) forms also occur Malm 265.
  • Text. Lines often follow the syllable pattern 7, 7, 7, 5. Chorus interjections (hayashi kotoba囃子詞), such as a son de se, are not part of the poem Malm 265.
  • Openings. A common opening leaps to a high, often ornamented pitch before the melody goes on Malm 265.
  • Function. The heart of min’yō民謡 is work songs, above all rice-planting and fishing songs. The term min’yō itself was coined in the late nineteenth century to translate the German Volkslied Malm 262.

6.1Aesthetics of Each Age

Each age and social group had its own dominant aesthetic Tokita 25–26:

Aesthetic Ideals
miyabi雅court, gagaku雅楽
  • gracefulness, elegance
yūgen幽玄nō能, military aristocracy
  • much written about in poetry and on stage

G Performers sit formally and face the front, and there is little overt expressiveness, gidayū-bushi義太夫節 and sometimes min’yō民謡 excepted. Tokita and Hughes link this to:

  • shibumi (koto箏 and shamisen music) and reigi礼儀, decorum (gagaku and nō);
  • auspicious occasions (medetaiめでたい);
  • Edo censorship;
  • the view of art as spiritual training (shugyō修行, dō道).

Humor survived in kyōgen狂言, comic jōruri浄瑠璃, folk song and some comic sub-genres Tokita 25.

6.2Timbre

G Japanese music shows a highly developed sensitivity to sound quality and timbre Tokita 26:

  • Sawari. A buzzing drone on the lowest shamisen string. Some genres (kiyomoto清元, tokiwazu常磐津, tsugaru-jamisen津軽三味線) strengthen it with a metal screwplate, the azuma-zawari東さわり.
  • Drum timbres. In the nō hayashi囃子 ensemble, timbre becomes structural: it combines with rhythm and vocal calls into patterned units.
  • Unpitched sounds. The koto plectrum’s swish along the strings (shū) and the shakuhachi尺八’s breath sounds bring non-pitched sound into the music.
  • Imitating nature. Koto and shamisen passages imitate insects and other natural sounds.

Miki says the same from the workshop. Japanese makers never trade timbre for volume, the materials (bamboo, paulownia, mulberry, skin) decide the sound, and imitating natural sounds is “a fundamental precept” of composing and playing Miki 3.

6.3Ma

Ma
ma間 literally means a space or interval between two points. In music it means rhythm, beat or, above all, timing: the best length of a pause between sounds for the greatest effect.
(6.1)
  • In nagauta長唄: uki-ma浮き間 slightly lengthens the first beat of a pair, and tsume-ma詰め間 does the reverse Tokita 26.
  • Beat: omote-ma表間 is the downbeat and ura-ma裏間 the upbeat Tokita 26.
  • For performers: ma is crucial for dancers, actors and percussionists. One nō performer called it akuma no ma悪魔の間, “the devil’s ma” Tokita 26.
  • For composers: Miki and Ishii brought concepts such as ma into a Western frame, and Takemitsu武満徹 and Miki used such concepts in their works Tokita 8, 31.

6.4Jo-Ha-Kyū

Jo-Ha-Kyū
R jo-ha-kyū序破急: jo = prelude, beginning; ha = “breaking apart”, growing in complexity; kyū = fast, a finale that speeds up before a final slow cadence.
(6.2)
  • Origins. The terms first appear in bugaku舞楽 (gagaku dance). Zeami世阿弥 later applied them to the dramatic structure of nō Tokita 26–27.
  • Scale. Malm says the idea governs whole compositions and single phrases alike, much as question and answer does in Western music Malm 115–116.
  • In gagaku. Large and medium pieces usually follow jo-ha-kyū, sometimes with a fourth section, ei詠, between ha破 and kyū急 (as in Goshōraku五常楽). Small pieces often have only ha and kyū, and today separate pieces are often combined to make a jo-ha-kyū set Malm 116.
  • Netori. In concert gagaku, the jo序 is often a netori音取, a short prelude that sets the mode: the shō笙 enters first, then the hichiriki篳篥, the flute and the kakko羯鼓, and finally the biwa琵琶 and koto Malm 116.

6.5Against the Mystique

G Tokita and Hughes push back against claims of Japanese uniqueness Tokita 26–27:

  • The claim that Japanese music blends musical and natural sound looks unique only next to Western music since the eighteenth century, not next to most other cultures.
  • Ma as timing has been mystified as something outsiders cannot grasp.
  • Jo-ha-kyū is “a simple concept” found elsewhere too, and it hardly applies to some Japanese genres.
  • Tsunoda’s theory that only the Japanese hear natural sounds as music with the left brain was taken up by Koizumi, but Tokita and Hughes treat it as an extreme of this mystification.

7Japanese Music Today

7.1What Japanese Listeners Enjoy

Tokita and Hughes reproduce three national surveys (multiple answers allowed). The bars show the national total, the share of respondents who enjoy each kind of music Tokita 29–31.

  • 1932: the top three genres were all narrative traditions (naniwa-bushi浪花節, biwa琵琶, gidayū義太夫), popular while radio and film were young Tokita 28–29.
  • By 1999: traditional folk and classical music had fallen far behind, while the hybrid enka演歌 held its own Tokita 29.

7.2Fusion and New Composition

  • From the tradition outward. Nakao Tozan中尾都山 (1876–1956) founded the Tozan都山 shakuhachi尺八 school, mixing Japanese and Western elements into a new harmonic language. The koto箏 master Miyagi Michio宮城道雄 (1894–1956) did something similar, for example in Haru no umi春の海 (1929) Tokita 8–9.
  • From Western training inward. Composers trained in Western music, such as Miki Minoru三木稔 and Ishii Maki石井眞木, brought Japanese instruments and ideas such as ma間 into a Western frame from the 1960s Tokita 8.
  • Miki’s own account. In 1964 Miki founded the Pro Musica Nipponia ensemble to give Japanese instruments a shared contemporary practice. He writes that this let traditional players perform with Western orchestras without becoming “more westernized” Miki 1.
  • New instruments. Since the 1920s the 13-string koto has been joined by models with 17, 21 to 25, 30 and even 80 strings. Tokita and Hughes ask: “When is a koto not a koto?” Tokita 32
  • Pop fusions. Yellow Magic Orchestra chose to “give the West what they expect”, and jazz musicians looked for a Japanese style in folk song Tokita 9.

7.3Will Tradition Survive?

G Young people found tradition old-fashioned (furukusai古臭い), yet as it grows foreign to them it may become exotic and interesting again Tokita 30. The traditional genres most popular with the young are wadaiko和太鼓, tsugaru-jamisen津軽三味線 and Okinawan music Tokita 32. A cynic would say this is because:

  • wadaiko is the ultimate rock drumming;
  • tsugaru-jamisen is heavy-metal guitar;
  • the Okinawan scale is close to the Western major and easy to harmonize.

Yet none of these genres is in fact un-Japanese Tokita 32.

G Their conclusion is that the new does not necessarily displace the old in Japan; it finds a place beside it, even if it pushes the old to the margins Tokita 33. Malm reached a similar conclusion forty years earlier: both styles were growing, and how far each would bend was the open question Malm 43–44.
