1Hayari-uta

1.1Popular, Folk and Courtly

Hayari-uta
Hayari-uta流行歌 are songs that “go with the flow”: popular song. From about the seventeenth century, when the term came into use, Japanese set them apart from two kinds of more lasting song: anonymous rural songs, today called min’yō民謡 (folk song), and aristocratic genres rooted in ancient Indian or Chinese tradition.
(1.1)

Tokita 261. Malm adds that the Edo江戸 word hayari-uta became ryūkōka流行歌 in the modern period Malm 271.

G Popular culture grew with the cities. Each province was allowed one castle, so warriors and commoners gathered in castle towns; “alternate attendance” (sankin kōtai参勤交代) made Edo the trend-setter for the nation. Hideyoshi’s edicts separating warriors from commoners split elite and popular arts, and rising farm output and merchant wealth gave commoners leisure and money for music Tokita 261–262.

1.2Before the Edo Period

Step through the early history with the buttons or the arrow keys.

Nara奈良 · 7th–8th centuries

  • In 675 Emperor Tenmu summoned commoners “who can sing well”, along with dwarfs and jugglers, to court: an early hint of high and low culture Tokita 262.
  • Chinese sangaku散楽 (juggling, sword swallowing, probably music) had a court office until 782; its performers likely took to the streets Tokita 262.
  • The gazetteers (fudoki風土記) ordered in 713 describe utagaki歌垣, spring and autumn songfests on mountains where men and women drank, sang, danced and courted; utagaki were staged at court in 734 and 770 Tokita 262–263.

Heian平安 · 794–1185

  • Aristocrats were drawn to folk and popular music, including fuzoku風俗; court saibara催馬楽 seem to use folk melodies Tokita 263.
  • Imayō今様 (“modern style” songs), from the tenth century, were made and sung by itinerants, courtesans, shamanesses and the blind, usually unaccompanied or with fan beats, kotsuzumi小鼓 or small cymbals. The retired emperor Go-Shirakawa後白河 compiled the anthology Ryōjin hishō梁塵秘抄 (about 1157–79) Tokita 263.

Kamakura鎌倉 and Muromachi室町 · 1185–1573

  • Under warrior rule imayō faded and short songs called kouta小唄 took over by the mid-fourteenth century, sung mostly by women performers (kugutsu傀儡, yūjo遊女, shirabyōshi白拍子) at drinking parties Tokita 263.
  • Kyōgen actors drew on the popular songs of the day, even rice-transplanting songs; the Kanginshū閑吟集 (1518) collects several hundred lyrics Tokita 264.

Late Sixteenth Century · 1570s–1600

  • Kouta were strung together for furyū風流 dancing; street preachers (sekkyōja説経者) and juggling singers (hōka放下) roamed town and country Tokita 264.
  • Ryūtatsu-bushi隆達節, after the Sakai堺 priest Takasabu Ryūtatsu (1527–1611), was sung with hitoyogiri一節切, kotsuzumi or fan beats, and later shamisen三味線. Some of its songs use the 7-7-7-5 meter that would dominate later popular song Tokita 264.

G For ryūtatsu-bushi and later genres the tunes are often lost. Each name probably stood for a melody, a “tune family”, that varied as it traveled orally Tokita 264–265.

2Edo Popular Song

2.1Shamisen and the Pleasure Quarters

The Tokugawa regime gave classes their own music: nō能 and gagaku雅楽 for warriors and court, shakuhachi尺八 for Fuke普化 monks, koto箏 and biwa琵琶 for the blind guild. Hayari-uta流行歌 fell mostly outside official control Tokita 265.

Instrument and Venue
The shamisen三味線, portable and suited to almost any melody, became the favorite accompaniment of popular song. Licensed pleasure quarters arose at the same time: Osaka (1585), Kyoto (1589) and Edo’s Yoshiwara吉原 (1617), and about two dozen more in provincial towns, often with theater districts nearby.
(2.1)

Tokita 265.

In the seventeenth century Kansai led. As courtesans’ clients shifted from warriors to merchants, top courtesans (tayū太夫) dropped nō chant for catchy shamisen songs: Hiraku-bushi, then katabachi片撥 (perhaps from a technique without upstrokes) and rōsai弄斎, then nage-bushi投節 (“fling song”, phrase ends “flung away”). With tsugi-bushi継節 (dote-bushi土手節) of Edo江戸 and magaki-bushi籬節 of Osaka, nage-bushi was one of the “big three” Tokita 265–266.

2.2Tune Families: Itako-bushi

Kaeuta
From the mid-eighteenth century Edo songs pushed out Kansai ones. With no common notation for vocal lines, most new hayari-uta began as kaeuta替え歌, new lyrics to familiar tunes, revised in Yoshiwara or the yose寄席 variety halls. When people renamed a memorable variant, it became in effect a new tune.
(2.2)

Tokita 266. Edo had about a million people by then, perhaps the largest city in the world Tokita 266.

2.3Street Performers

Street musicians played on boulevards and temple grounds; troupes built makeshift halls of rush mats. From the early nineteenth century yose halls in every major city offered comic narrative with songs, exotic instruments, koto by blind men, and gidayū義太夫 by young women, which the shogunate eventually banned as immoral Tokita 268–269.

Door-to-door performers came especially around New Year: manzai万歳, with blessing dialogue, song and dance, sometimes licensed by aristocrats; yomiuri読売, who sang excerpts to sell song sheets Tokita 269. Malm describes the same “before-the-doorway arts” and the modern chindonyaちんどん屋 street-advertising bands Malm 271.

A Fool’s Sutra
G Ahōdara-kyō阿呆陀羅経, a “fool’s sutra”, was a spoof of Buddhist chant invented in the early nineteenth century by a sham priest, then spread by itinerant “petitioned monks” (gannin bōzu願人坊主). In a recorded example the opening is sung on clear pitches over fast shamisen ostinatos, then rhythm loosens and pitch blurs toward speech.
(2.3)

Tokita 269–270.

Street narrative styles such as saimon祭文, kudoki口説, chongareちょんがれ and chobokureちょぼくれ have mostly vanished, but chongare and chobokure gave rise to naniwa-bushi浪花節, a favorite into the twentieth century Tokita 270–271.

2.4Song Publishing

From the 1660s booklets of pleasure-quarter songs were printed and sold. The Shichiku shoshinshū糸竹初心集 (Kyoto, 1664) is special: it gives simple notation for hitoyogiri一節切, koto or shamisen. The Kyoto anthology Matsu no ha松の葉 (1703) mixes short songs (hauta端歌), nagauta, kumiuta組歌 and Edo jōruri浄瑠璃. By the mid-eighteenth century cheap broadsheets (kawaraban瓦版) dominated, again usually without melodies Tokita 271–273.

G Even the 1664 notation records only outlines of tunes everyone knew, and its scale is disputed: the shamisen part seems to have half steps, but some scholars read the koto and hitoyogiri parts as having none Tokita 273.

3Hauta, Utazawa and Kouta

3.1Hauta and the Utazawa School

By the mid-nineteenth century a “man of taste” was expected to sing the latest styles; connoisseurs met at studios, bathhouses, barbershops and teahouses, often taught by geisha. New genres emerged between classical and popular Tokita 274.

From Hauta to Utazawa
Hauta端歌 (Edo江戸 hauta; ha破, “something short”) exploded in popularity in the 1850s. A circle around the ex-geisha teacher Sawa (“Uta no Sawa”) became the utazawa歌沢 school, with the retired samurai Utazawa Sasamaru (1797–1857) later named its first head; rivalry then split it into the Tora and Shiba schools.
(3.1)

Tokita 274.

The split was about status and politics, not music, though the Shiba school was said to stress the shamisen三味線 and the Tora school the voice Tokita 274. Malm calls utazawa a reform that added dignity and grace and toned down eroticism Malm 230.

3.3Kouta

Kouta
Modern kouta小唄 (“small songs”) grew from livelier hauta. Despite a popular story that the first kouta was composed in 1855, a genre called (Edo) kouta only emerged at the start of the twentieth century, nurtured mainly by retired geisha teachers.
(3.2)

Tokita 275. Before then, “kouta” meant many kinds of short song Tokita 275. See Where the Sources Differ.

Kotama of Shinbashi opened a studio in 1916 and soon became the first kouta iemoto家元; by 1972 some 180 people headed schools Tokita 275, 278. Kouta is sung to shamisen plucked with the fingernails rather than a plectrum, without the tension and volume of utazawa; its themes are love and parting Tokita 278. Malm: fingertip or small plectrum, on a smaller shamisen Malm 230.

beyond the main source

Malm describes late twentieth-century kouta recitals of businessmen amateurs, for whom one party kouta mattered as much as golf, with two rotating platforms swapping performers on stage Malm 230.

4What Is Min’yō?

4.1A Borrowed Concept

A Modern Word
Min’yō民謡 (“folk song”) is a modern word: Herder’s Volkslied (1775) reached Japan around 1890 as a Sino-Japanese compound, carrying Romantic ideas with it. Villagers themselves simply said uta歌, “song”, with a prefix as needed (taue uta田植歌, rice-planting song). Its partner term minzoku geinō民俗芸能, folk performing arts, dates from about 1958.
(4.1)

Tokita 281–282. Malm: scholars coined min’yō in the late nineteenth century to translate Volkslied Malm 262.

The idea caught on slowly. A 1920 “min’yō concert” in Tokyo drew some who expected nō能 chant (the character for yō陽 is also used for nō singing) and police who expected a left-wing rally Tokita 281. As late as the 1980s, older villagers told Hughes that min’yō meant other people’s songs; their own were just uta Tokita 285.

4.2Defining Folk Song

Asano’s Definition
R Asano Kenji’s definition: min’yō are songs born naturally within local communities which, transmitted aurally, reflect daily feeling naively. A song that loses its local color, he wrote, has fallen into the lowest class of popular song.
(4.2)

Tokita 282. Hughes points out that artless simplicity is a Romantic notion: many folk songs were carefully crafted Tokita 282.

G The stress on the local reflects the Japanese ideal of the furusato故郷, the “old village” or native place: “Folk song is the heart’s home town.” Songs carry place names, and a migrating song may be renamed by its new home: Oiwake bushi追分節, from a post town in central Japan, became Esashi oiwake江差追分 in the far north Tokita 282–283.

5Folk Song: Village to Nation

5.1The Traditional Village

Villagers sang work songs to coordinate effort or pass the time, dance songs for the Bon festival, and songs for evenings, weddings and special days Tokita 284.

Songs on the Move
Village repertories were always moving. Travelers, migrant workers and itinerant professionals carried songs back and forth; lyrics were rarely written and melodies never, so songs varied constantly. Geisha added shamisen三味線 and polish to village songs, a contribution purists undervalue.
(5.1)

Tokita 284. The Haiya bushiハイヤ節 family has been traced all over Japan (Machida 1965) Tokita 284.

A good voice was not needed for work songs, the village singer Itō Moyo told Hughes; what mattered was having enough verses to last till sunset. Someone who played too much was a dōrakumono道楽者, a wastrel, and one lord forbade all but the richest to dance during planting and harvest Tokita 284.

5.2Modernization

Between the 1890s and the 1950s Japan went from 90 percent rural to 80 percent urban, and village singing gave way to a “folk song world” (min’yō-kai民謡界) and then a national industry Tokita 285.

What Modernization Changed
Removed from their contexts, min’yō民謡 were treated more like classical music. Key changes: songs heard more in cities than villages; folk song bars and theaters; a new profession of folk song teacher, some heading iemoto家元-style schools; standardization and some notation; professional “folk singers” (min’yō kashu民謡歌手); recordings and, from 1926, radio; virtuoso accompaniment; respectable dress and lyrics; contests; and the loss of dialect and local color.
(5.2)

Tokita 285–286. In the country, machines made most work songs redundant Tokita 286.

Malm agrees that mass media helped as well as harmed: radio singers from the 1920s became stars, and local business and government promoted old and new “folk” songs for tourism Malm 262.

Tokita 286–288.

Singers are backed by kakegoe掛け声, rhythmic shouts, and hayashi-kotoba囃子詞, melodic refrains Tokita 288. Voice quality varies with function and mood, and fans still prize a voice “reeking of the earth” (tsuchikusai土臭い) and intricate ornaments (kobushi小節), richest in free-rhythm and northern songs Tokita 289.

6.2Meter, Texture and Text

Basic Traits
Meter: 2/4 dominates; a long-short “6/8” appears in dance songs; triple meter is virtually absent; free rhythm is common for songs not tied to movement. Texture: heterophony, all parts close to the voice. Text: most often four lines of 7, 7, 7 and 5 syllables, with verses that move freely between songs.
(6.1)

Tokita 288–289. Free-rhythm songs are now called takemono竹物, “bamboo pieces”, after their shakuhachi尺八 Tokita 288–289.

Three exceptions to heterophony: drone-like shamisen三味線 chords; a shamisen countermelody (as in Yasugi bushi安来節); and a flute phrase repeated regardless of the voice, common in Bon songs. Sado okesa佐渡おけさ combines voice, a repeated shamisen motive and an independent flute line Tokita 289.

Malm adds that many folk songs are binary (A–B) and strophic, often open with a high, ornamented vocal phrase, and have an inner beat between duple and triple that bar lines distort; chorus interjections such as “a son de se” are hayashi kotoba囃子詞, not part of the poem Malm 265. Traditionally songs were solo, unison, or leader and chorus; stage songs today have one soloist Tokita 289.

6.3Modes

Folk Modes
Pentatonic modes dominate. All share a structure of linked fourths: two fixed pitches a fourth apart frame one auxiliary note whose position varies. The infix a minor third above gives the min’yō scale (sometimes called yō陽), common in folk song and rare elsewhere, with the intervals of a piano’s black keys; a whole step above gives ritsu律; a half step above gives miyako-bushi都節 (sometimes called in陰), less common and darker.
(6.2)

Tokita 289–290. Hughes notes that min’yō has no single tonic, so no single mode, and that ritsu has the same intervals with clearer nuclear tones Tokita 289.

G Intonation of the infix is flexible, so one song may drift between ritsu, miyako-bushi and min’yō, even within one performance; today’s professionals follow Western intervals but still flatten the downward leading tones of miyako-bushi Tokita 290. Malm teaches the same three-note units: two framing tones a fourth apart, with the inner tone a whole step from a frame for yō and a half step for in; folk songs may switch quickly between them Malm 264.

7Folk Song Today

7.1Booms and Preservation

The last big “folk song boom” began in 1978 with the NHK program Min’yō o anata ni, whose young stars Kanazawa Akiko and Harada Naoyuki, in jeans and casual jackets, showed that min’yō民謡 need not be old-fashioned Tokita 290.

Hozonkai
“Traditional” songs survive either because their context survives, like Bon dances around a tower, or through a preservation society (hozonkai保存会), usually devoted to one local song. Hokkaido北海道 towns preserve the herring-fishing suite that includes Sōran bushiソーラン節, once “accompanied” only by net handles striking the gunwales.
(7.1)

Tokita 290–291. Malm finds the one-club-one-song approach “uniquely Japanese”, like the quest for the perfect brushstroke Malm 262.

G Esashi oiwake江差追分 shows the trend. After the herring vanished in 1900, Esashi promoted its song for tourism and had famous singers agree on one standard version. Since 1963 a national contest has rewarded singing it as identically as possible, down to the number of notes in a trill, taught through a special notation. Some call it a “classical folk song” (koten min’yō古典民謡); there are now over a hundred single-song contests Tokita 291.

7.2Stage Min’yō

Stage Min’yō
Stage min’yō (sutēji min’yō), the form most heard today, uses one standard format: metric songs with shamisen三味線, plus shakuhachi尺八 (or shinobue篠笛 for Bon songs) and percussion; free-meter songs with shakuhachi alone. A soloist stands center stage with backing singers.
(7.2)

Tokita 291–293.

Little effort matches style to a song’s origin. Sōran bushi, once sung only by men on the boats, is now sung by anyone and always accompanied; changes like this make some say true folk songs no longer exist. Preservation societies and professionals look down on each other’s versions Tokita 293.

Young people drawn to folk music turned to fōku songu, American-style guitar and banjo folk, which has no overlap with traditional song Tokita 296. Social engineering runs through the history: Kasai-bayashi promoted in the 1700s against delinquency; a Nikkō日光 company Bon dance (since 1914) for morale; early new folk songs commissioned to give mill girls cleaner lyrics; postwar contests sponsored for “spiritual revival” Tokita 296.

7.4Fusion and the Future

What Still Draws the Young
G Hughes sees only three folk-based genres with wide appeal today: wadaiko和太鼓 (taiko ensembles), tsugaru-jamisen津軽三味線 (once accompaniment, now a solo genre: romantic links to blind itinerants such as Takahashi Chikuzan, sheer power, no singing needed, and improvisation), and Okinawan music, whose main mode is close to Western major.
(7.3)

Tokita 300. In 1978, 24 percent of Japanese named min’yō their favorite music, second to enka演歌 at 31 percent Tokita 298.

Itō Takio sings min’yō with jazz trio and mixed bands; his “Takio’s Sōran bushi” keeps a min’yō voice while taking liberties with melody and tempo. The Yosakoi Sōran Festival (from 1992) has hundreds of teams dancing to disco and rock arrangements of the song Tokita 299–300. Malm notes that Takahashi Chikuzan’s style made tsugaru-jamisen a rival to rock guitar for young Japanese Malm 266.

8Folk Performing Arts

8.1Types and Sounds

Honda’s Types
Honda Yasuji’s typology (from 1960): kagura神楽, performances asking native gods for prosperity and long life, often enacting Shinto myths; dengaku田楽, “rice-paddy music” for the agricultural cycle and ritual transplanting; furyū風流, large summer events including Bon dances; plus local versions of classical theater. The categories are not watertight.
(8.1)

Tokita 296–297.

Instruments: transverse flutes, stick drums of many kinds, cymbals or hand gongs (kane, chanchiki); shamisen三味線 rare, shakuhachi尺八 rarer; sometimes binzasara編木 clappers. Meter and heterophony are as in min’yō民謡, ritsu律 is common, miyako-bushi都節 rare. Intonation is far less standard, partly because local flutes have evenly spaced holes and players do not correct pitch with the lips Tokita 297–298.

Semi-professional exceptions exist, such as Edo江戸 sato-kagura里神楽 and its instrumental matsuri-bayashi祭囃子 Tokita 298.

8.2Folk Theater (Malm)

Malm surveys folk theater: local dengaku, bugaku舞楽, nō能, bunraku文楽 and kabuki歌舞伎; street paper-theater (kamishibai紙芝居) men who sold candy; stick puppets and village puppet troupes such as Awaji淡路, where bunraku is said to have begun; a Gifu barn turned puppet stage; child actors performing kabuki on festival floats at Nagahama; and yamabushi kagura山伏神楽 in Iwate, with large drum and cymbals Malm 268–270.

G Today, the religious meaning of folk performing arts has largely given way to folklore and tourism, supported by cultural-property systems and campaigns to “construct a native place” (furusato-zukuri故郷づくり). Urban groups such as Kodō鼓童 learn them from many regions, and since the 1960s wadaiko和太鼓 ensembles have become a new tradition that competes for members with local arts Tokita 298.

9Urban Music and Research

9.1City Songs (Malm)

Malm traces street song: kudokiuta口説き歌 on local events or romances, the sung news of yomiuri読売, Meiji明治 music-hall patter songs (oppekepeオッペケペー) on social issues, and enka演歌, the longest-lasting music-hall genre. He links karaoke to the old expectation that everyone can sing at a party, where the goal, as in village song, is participation Malm 271–272.

Where the Sources Differ

Errors and disagreements found while cross-reading, grouped by source, with the check that exposed each one. The guide text uses the form named in each check.

Malm 2000

When kouta began. Malm: in 1855 new kouta for the kabuki歌舞伎 appeared (p. 230).
Check: Groemer: the story that the first kouta was composed in 1855 rests on the flimsiest evidence; a genre of (Edo江戸) kouta emerged only at the start of the twentieth century (Tokita 275). The guide follows Groemer.
How kouta is plucked. Malm: with the fingertip or a small plectrum (p. 230).
Check: Groemer: with the fingernails rather than the usual plectrum (Tokita 278). Probably practices vary; both agree it is soft.
Which modes dominate folk song. Malm: the yō and in scales dominate Japanese folk music (p. 264).
Check: Hughes: the min’yō scale (yō) and ritsu are common; miyako-bushi (in) is less common in min’yō and rare in folk performing arts (Tokita 289, 297–298).

Tokita and Hughes 2017

When the word min’yō arrived. Hughes: min’yō reached Japan around 1890 (p. 281).
Check: The same chapter: it began to gain currency in the early twentieth century (p. 281). Not a contradiction: the word came first, common use later.
