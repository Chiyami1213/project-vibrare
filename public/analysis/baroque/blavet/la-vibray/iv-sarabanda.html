IV. Sarabanda

The fourth movement of Michel Blavet’s D minor sonata for flute and basso continuo: a sarabande marked Largo, sixteen bars in two repeated halves. The first half plays one phrase twice over a bass that sinks by half steps, first to the dominant and then to a cadence in A minor. The second half climbs by half steps through a sequence and closes with the cadence of the first half a fourth higher. Below: the form, every cadence, the partimento patterns in the figured bass, and the flute part in three versions: as Blavet printed it, read by Quantz’s rules, and as Toshio Takahashi revised it for the Suzuki Flute School.

Cadences

Every cadence marked on the score, with the evidence from the bass and the figures. All labels are our reading ours.

  • Three cadences in sixteen bars: a half cadence (m. 4), a full cadence in A minor (m. 8), and a full cadence in D minor (m. 16). Each phrase of the first half ends with one; the second half has only the last.
  • The half cadence is the end of the chromatic bass: B♭ with 6 steps down to A with ♯ while the flute trills on C♯.
  • The two full cadences match. The bass goes 4 with 65, 5 with 4 and then ♯, 1. The flute trills on a dotted quarter, plays the closing note an eighth early and holds it for a bar: B to A in m. 7, E to D in m. 15.
  • A minor turns major at once. Under the long A of m. 8 the bass plays A, C♯ and E: the chord of the cadence becomes the dominant of D minor, for the repeat and for the second half alike.
  • A close avoided. In m. 14 the dominant is reached with C♯ and A in the bass, but the bass steps up to B♭ instead of falling to D, and the cadence takes one more bar.
  • Breath and cadence: Blavet’s only breath mark follows the half cadence (m. 4). Our reading adds one after the cadence of m. 8.

Bass motion

Lamento: Chromatic Descent

The bass falls by half steps from the tonic to the dominant: D, C♯ with 6, C♮ with 6, B♮ with 7, B♭ with 6, A with ♯ (mm. 1–4). The filled-in falling fourth is the bass of many laments of the seventeenth century, and partimento teaching keeps the name Lamento for it; here it carries a sarabande. The usual setting is a chain of 7–6; Blavet has one seventh, on B♮. It returns in m. 6 at twice the speed, D–C♯–C♮–B, and turns to A minor.

Phrase

Phrase and Answer

The first half is one phrase played twice. The first time it stops on the dominant, the bass coming down a half step from B♭ to A (m. 4). The second time the descent is pressed into one bar and a full cadence in A minor follows (mm. 6–8). Blavet marks his one breath between the two.

Sequence

Rising Sequence (Monte)

Three steps, each a bar of tension and a bar of rest. The dominant with C♯ in the bass leads to D minor (mm. 9–10). The dominant of G minor, with its seventh, C, in the bass, falls to G minor with B♭ in the bass (mm. 11–12). The dominant of A, with D in the bass, falls to C♯ and the A major chord (mm. 13–14). The last two steps, on the fourth degree and then on the fifth, make a Monte, Riepel’s name for a rising sequence of closes. In both, the bass has 4–3 of the new key and the flute its leading tone, so an augmented fourth between them opens to a sixth. Above them the flute rises by half steps, E–F–F♯–G–G♯–A.

Bass figure

Broken Chords Under Long Notes

Whenever the flute holds a note for a whole bar, the bass spreads the chord in eighths: A major (m. 8), D minor (m. 10), G minor with its third in the bass (m. 12), and D minor in the first ending of m. 16. Only in the last bar of all does the bass hold its note as well.

Cadence

Long Cadence With 65 and 4–♯

Both halves close the same way, with a long cadence (cadenza lunga) that is compound on the dominant. The fourth degree carries 65; the fifth degree carries a 4 that falls to the leading tone; the flute trills on a dotted quarter, plays the last note an eighth early and holds it for a bar. A minor: D, E, E, A (mm. 7–8). D minor: G, A, A, D (mm. 15–16), where the bass adds B♭ and G in passing.

Cadence

Phrygian Half Cadence

The first phrase ends when the bass steps down a half step, B♭ with 6 to A with ♯, under a flute line that comes down the scale to a trilled C♯ (mm. 3–4). A half cadence reached from the sixth chord a half step above the dominant is called Phrygian. Durante’s rule for a bass that falls a half step at a close says the same: the upper note takes a 6, the lower a 5 with a major third. It is the last step of the chromatic descent.

What Blavet Prints source

  • Sarabanda, Largo, and the time signature 3/4.
  • h: a breath (haleine), Blavet’s own sign according to Walter Kolneder’s preface to the Bärenreiter edition. One in 16 bars, after the half cadence of m. 4.
  • +: a trill (tremblement), ten in all: on a quarter note (mm. 2, 6), on a dotted quarter (mm. 4, 7, 15), on an eighth (m. 7) and on a dotted eighth (mm. 9, 11, 13, 15).
  • Seven slurs, none longer than a beat: over each of the three triplets, over each trilled dotted eighth with its two thirty-second notes (mm. 9, 11, 13), and over the trilled D of m. 15 with its two small notes and the E.
  • No slur anywhere in the first half, on any dotted quarter and its eighth, or on the dotted eighth and sixteenth in the middle of the sequence figure.
  • Two small notes, C♯ and D, after the trilled D of m. 15: the ending of that trill, written out.
  • Two endings for each half, both written out in full, with arcs over them.
  • No dynamics in this movement.

Rules We Apply Quantz

Paraphrased from the Versuch (1752), chapters VI, VII, IX, XI, XIV and XVII, in Reilly’s translation.

  • The sarabande moves like the entrée, the loure and the courante, which are played majestically, the bow detached at each quarter note, whether dotted or not; but it is played with a somewhat more agreeable execution (XVII.vii §58, p. 291).
  • Dotted notes in dances. The eighth after a dotted quarter is not played with its literal value but very short and sharp; the dotted note is played with emphasis, and the bow is detached during the dot. Quantz names the sarabande, and adds that all dotted notes are treated this way if time allows (§58, p. 290).
  • A gentle tongue. Slow and sustained notes take di, not ti (VI.i §6, p. 73). In a slow piece every note is caressed and none is tipped harshly (XIV §13, p. 166).
  • Long notes. A note held for a whole or half bar is tipped gently, begun very softly, swelled to its middle and diminished to its end (XIV §10, pp. 165–166). Shorter notes get their own soft and loud as far as time allows (§11, p. 166).
  • Slurs as marked. Under a slur only the first note is tipped (VI.i §10, p. 74). Dotted notes are tongued ti-ri (VI.ii §3, p. 76). Leaping notes are detached; stepwise, flattering ones may be slurred (XI §11, p. 123).
  • Unequal notes. In a moderate tempo, and even in an Adagio, the quickest notes are played a little unequally; in three-four time these are the eighths, as long as no quicker notes are mixed in (XI §12, p. 123).
  • Trills begin with their appoggiatura from above and end with two little notes (IX §7, p. 103); in sad pieces they are struck more slowly (IX §2, p. 101).
  • Breath: after a cadence, before a new idea (VII §4, p. 88); in a slow piece, sustain the melody and take breath at the proper time (XIV §12, p. 166). Blavet’s one mark fits.
  • A repeated idea in the same key can be helped with the piano and with slurred notes (XIV §15, p. 167).

Quantz’s Rules and This Movement

Rules Quantz

Paraphrased from the Versuch, XI (good execution), XIV (the manner of playing the Adagio) and XVII.vii (tempo and dance music), Reilly pp. 123–124, 164–168 and 284–291.

  • Tempo by the pulse. Quantz measures tempo by a pulse of about 80 beats a minute and sorts pieces into four classes, from Allegro assai to Adagio assai (XVII.vii §§49–51, pp. 284–286).
  • The danced sarabande. The entrée, the loure and the courante take a pulse beat on each quarter note; a sarabande has the same movement (§58, p. 291). That is about quarter note = 80.
  • Slow pieces in three-four time. In an Adagio cantabile where the bass moves in eighths, there is a pulse beat on each eighth. If the bass moves only in quarters and the melody is more arioso than sad, a beat on each quarter. If Adagio assai, Mesto or Lento is written, two beats to each quarter (§51, pp. 286–287). With a bass in eighths that is about quarter note = 40.
  • The word Largo. Quantz names Largo with Cantabile, Arioso, Affettuoso, Andante and Larghetto as slow pieces that must be clearly distinguished from a pathetic Adagio; the tempo of each has to be judged from the piece itself, so that a sad Adagio is not too quick or a singing one too slow (XIV §7, pp. 164–165; §19, p. 168). Largo assai belongs to his slowest class (XVII.vii §49, p. 284).
  • Dotted notes, twice. In dances such as the sarabande the note after the dot is short and sharp and the bow is detached during the dot (XVII.vii §58, p. 290). In a Grave made of dotted notes the long note is swelled up to the dot and, if the interval is not too great, slurred softly and briefly to the next (XIV §17, p. 167).
  • Very sad pieces are graced more with slurred notes than with leaps or shakes, though shakes are not to be avoided altogether (XIV §8, p. 165).
  • Strict tempo matters in all music and most of all in music for the dance (XVII.vii §57, p. 290).
  • Unequal notes (XI §12, pp. 123–124): see Rules We Apply above. Excepted are several notes on one pitch, notes with dots or strokes, and notes under a slur of more than two.

Guidelines and Our Readings guideline ours

  • Why Quantz for a French sonata. The sonata was printed in Paris about twenty years before the Versuch. Quantz knew Blavet in person, and his paragraphs on dance music are about the French style. Where Blavet’s own marks speak, they come first; Quantz is used to explain them and to fill the gaps. Hotteterre’s Principes (1707) would be the nearer French witness and has not been read for this page.
  • Dance or slow piece. Blavet gives both words, Sarabanda and Largo. Quantz treats the sarabande among the dances and the Largo among the slow pieces, and the two chapters do not say the same thing. We take the rhythm from the dance paragraph, which names the sarabande: a stroke for each quarter and sharp dotted notes. We take the tone from the Adagio chapter: a gentle tongue and long notes that swell and fall.
  • Tempo. Quantz’s two measures give 80 for a sarabande that is danced and 40 for a slow piece in 3/4 over a bass in eighths. Blavet’s Largo slows the dance, and his bass moves in eighths, but D minor is not one of the keys Quantz plays most slowly, and the piece has a dance step in every bar. The page starts at quarter note = 60, between the two; that is also the figure Takahashi prints. The slider goes down to 40 and up past 80 to try both.
  • Sharp dotted notes. With the switch on, the note after a dotted note starts late, at half its written length, and the dotted note is followed by a short silence: Quantz’s bow detached during the dot. It applies to the dotted quarters of the first half and of the cadences and to the dotted eighth and sixteenth of the sequence figure. How short is a matter of taste; Quantz says only very short.
  • Unequal eighths. In mm. 1–8 the eighths are the quickest notes, so the page plays the second of each pair a little late, about 7 to 5, in the flute and in the bass; repeated notes stay even. From m. 9 sixteenths, thirty-second notes and triplets are mixed in, and the eighths are even. The switch turns it off.
  • Takahashi’s long slurs read as phrase marks of a modern edition: they join whole bars across leaps and repeated notes. Blavet’s seven slurs all lie within one beat, and our reading keeps to those.
  • Repeats and endings. Blavet prints a repeat sign and two endings for each half. The page plays each half once. Quantz’s advice for a slow piece is to play the main idea as written at first and to add notes only when it returns (XIV §14, pp. 166–167).
  • Names. Lamento, the rule of the octave, and the long, compound, alto, deceptive and Phrygian cadences are names used in partimento teaching and in modern writing about it; Monte is Joseph Riepel’s term (1755) for a rising sequence, taken up by Robert Gjerdingen. Blavet uses none of them.
  • Where the pattern names come from. They follow the partimento catalog made for this site, 96 patterns that were checked there against Fenaroli, Durante, Sanguinetti’s The Art of Partimento and Gjerdingen. Those books were not read again for this page; each pattern was matched to its entry in the catalog by its bass degrees and figures.
  • Patterns that fit loosely. The Lamento usually carries a 7–6 over each pair of bass notes; Blavet’s has sixth chords and a single seventh. A Monte usually closes each step with the leading tone in the bass; here the leading tone is in the flute and the bass has 4–3, and a step on D minor (mm. 9–10) comes before the two on G minor and A.
  • Cadence types (PAC, HC) are modern labels. We call a cadence perfect only when the bass goes 5–1 in root position and the flute ends on 1 as the tonic arrives.

Notes on the Reading

The Score

Two staves as in the Paris print: flute and figured bass, on three systems that break where the print’s do. Trills are shown with Blavet’s +, the breath with his h, and the two small notes of m. 15 as he places them. A slashed 5 is a diminished fifth; 4 and ♯ over the dominant follow each other in time. The print cancels a sharp with a flat sign (the C of mm. 2 and 6); the score uses a natural. Accidentals follow modern use and hold to the end of the bar. The print writes each ending as a full bar, the second after the repeat sign, with an arc over each; the score shows them as first and second endings under brackets, and the bar numbers count each pair once.

Readings we are less sure of: the last bass note of m. 3, printed without a flat under a 6 (read B♭, as in the second quarter of the bar); the fourth bass note of m. 2, which has a speck before it (read E); the last bass note of the first ending of m. 8 (read C♯); a small arc over the bass C♯ of m. 14, which is not read as a figure; and the slur of m. 15, which begins just before the barline (read as a slur from the trilled D to the E; Takahashi reads a tie from the D of m. 14).
