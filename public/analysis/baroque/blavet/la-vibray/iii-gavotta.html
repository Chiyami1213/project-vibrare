III. Gavotta “Les Caquets”

The third movement of Michel Blavet’s D minor sonata for flute and basso continuo: a gavotte marked Moderato, with the title “Les Caquets,” the chatter. Two repeated halves. The theme, eighths slurred two by two, opens the movement and turns to A minor; the second half goes through F major and G minor, waits on the dominant, brings the theme back, and plays its last four bars again as a variation in sixteenths. Below: the form, every cadence, the partimento patterns in the figured bass, and the flute part in three versions: as Blavet printed it with his own slurs and breath marks, read by Quantz’s rules, and as Toshio Takahashi revised it for the Suzuki Flute School.

Cadences

Every cadence marked on the score, with the evidence from the bass and the figures. All labels are our reading ours.

  • One cadence bar, four times. The bass goes 5, 3 with 6, 4 with 65, 5 and then 1; the flute falls a third, steps up twice, trills on a dotted quarter and closes on a half note: A minor (mm. 11–12), G minor (mm. 25–26) and D minor (mm. 40–41, 44–45).
  • F major has its own cadence: 2 with 6 over ♭, 1, 5, 5 an octave lower, 1 (mm. 17–18). The flute’s last note is only an eighth, and the sequence starts at once.
  • Two half cadences frame the pedal: the dominant arrives through G♯ (m. 31) and again after the bass has stepped down from G to D (m. 35).
  • The first close is the weakest. In m. 6 the bass reaches D on the downbeat, but the flute is on a trilled F and falls to D a quarter later. The home key gets its first full cadence only in m. 41, and the petite reprise confirms it.
  • Breath and cadence: three of Blavet’s seven breath marks follow a cadence (mm. 6, 18, 41). He marks none after the half notes that end the cadences of mm. 12, 26 and 35; our reading adds those three.

Partimento Patterns

The bass patterns under every bar, named as in the site’s partimento catalog: the bass motions and cadences of Neapolitan partimento teaching (the tied bass, the bass that falls by fifths, the rule of the octave, the long cadence) and one galant schema, the Ponte. Bass motion

Tied Bass, 2 to 6

The bass note is tied over the barline. Under the new chord it is a dissonance, figured 2, and it must step down, to a note figured 6, which is tied in its turn. Three notes make the chain each time: D–C–B♭ (mm. 2–4, 37–39, 41–43), A–G–F (mm. 8–10), G–F–E♭ (mm. 22–24). It comes five times, and the flute’s figure above it is the second part of the theme. In partimento teaching this is the tied-bass motion, the suspension that lies in the bass.

Bass motion

Falling Fifths With Sevenths

Every half bar a new root with a 7, each a fifth below the last: B♭–E–A–D (mm. 15–16) and C–F–B♭–E–A–D (mm. 27–29). The bass circles each root in eighths while the flute has its longest notes: quarters and leaps the first time, dotted quarters the second. Those long notes are the seventh of one chord and the third of the next, the chain of sevenths that partimento teaching sets over a bass rising a fourth and falling a fifth.

Bass motion

Falling Fifths, Root Then Third

Seven roots from the middle of m. 18 to the end of m. 21: G–C–F–B♭–E♭–A–D. In each half bar the bass plays the root and leaps to the third of the chord, which leads by step to the next root. Blavet figures the first three thirds 5̸ and the last four roots 7, so each chord presses on to the next; the first three are each the dominant of the next, a chain of dominants. The flute plays one bar four times, a step lower each time.

Cadence

Long Cadence With ⁶₅

One bar makes four of the five full cadences: the bass goes 5, 3 with 6, 4 with 65, 5, and then 1, the long cadence (cadenza lunga) of partimento teaching, while the flute falls a third, steps up twice and trills on a dotted quarter. A minor (mm. 11–12), G minor (mm. 25–26), D minor (mm. 40–41, 44–45). Only F major gets a different bar, a simple cadence (mm. 17–18).

Schema

Ponte: Dominant Pedal

After the half cadence of m. 31 the bass holds A for three bars, the bridge back to the theme that Riepel called Ponte. The flute returns to its low A again and again and climbs above it in slurred figures of three notes, from D–E–D up to A–B♭–A, then runs down the scale while the bass steps down 4–3–2–1 with the chords of the rule of the octave (m. 34), to a second half cadence (m. 35). The theme comes back after it.

Form

Petite Reprise as a Double

The last four bars of the theme (mm. 37–41) are played again as mm. 41–45 over the same bass. The first three are varied in sixteenths: a turn around B♭ and A for the eighths B♭–F–G–F, broken chords and turns for the figure over the tied bass. The cadence bar is unchanged. Petite reprise is the French term for repeating the end of a piece, double for a variation in quicker notes.

Phrase

Phrase and Answer

The first half is one phrase played twice. The first time it closes in D minor with the flute on a trilled F that falls to D a quarter late (m. 6). The second time it bends toward A minor in its second bar, repeats the tied bass a fourth lower and closes with a full cadence (m. 12).

What Blavet Prints source

  • Gavotta, Les Caquets, Moderato, and the time signature 2.
  • h: a breath (haleine), Blavet’s own sign according to Walter Kolneder’s preface to the Bärenreiter edition. Seven in 45 bars: mm. 6, 14, 18, 22, 29, 33, 41. The Allemanda has fifteen in 32 bars; here the phrases are longer and the marks stand only where a new idea begins.
  • Eighths slurred two by two, 48 pairs: the upbeat and first bar of the theme wherever it comes, the scale of mm. 12–14, the falling scale of m. 30, and the pairs that leap through a chord in mm. 27–29.
  • Three eighths under a slur, 17 times: the first three notes of the figure over the tied bass (mm. 3, 4, 9, 10, 23, 24, 38, 39), notes two to four of each bar in the sequence (mm. 18–21), and the figures over the pedal (mm. 31–33).
  • Other slurs: a quarter note to the eighth a step above it (mm. 14–16), four falling eighths (m. 33), and the sixteenths of the last bars in fours and eights (mm. 41–43).
  • No slur on the last four or five eighths of the theme’s figure, in the cadence bars, or between a dotted quarter and its eighth.
  • +: a trill (tremblement), 14 in all: on the dotted quarter of every cadence bar, on the first eighth of the figure over the tied bass from m. 23 on, on two eighths over the pedal, and on three longer notes (mm. 6, 34, 35).
  • Three small notes: E between F and D (m. 6), B♭ before the trilled A (m. 25), D before E (m. 34).
  • No dynamics in this movement.

Rules We Apply Quantz

Paraphrased from the Versuch (1752), chapters VI, VII, VIII, IX, XI, XII and XVII, in Reilly’s translation.

  • Dance music is played seriously, with a heavy yet short and sharp stroke, more detached than slurred; dotted notes are heavy and the notes after them brief and sharp; fast pieces are gay, hopping and springing (XVII.vii §56, p. 290).
  • Slurs as marked. Notes that are to be slurred are played as they are marked, and notes that are to be tongued are not slurred (XII §21, p. 133). Under a slur only the first note is tipped (VI.i §10, p. 74).
  • Unequal notes. In a moderate tempo the quickest notes are played a little unequally: the first, third, fifth and seventh slightly longer than the notes between them, but less than if they were dotted. In alla breve the quickest notes are the eighths (XI §12, p. 123). Excepted: notes with strokes or dots, several notes on one pitch, and a slur over more than two notes (p. 124).
  • Tonguing. Ti-ri is the most useful stroke in passage-work of moderate speed, because its quickest notes are always a little unequal (VI.ii §1, p. 76). The stress falls on ri, which goes on the note on the beat (§4, p. 76); never begin with ri (§5, p. 76). Dotted notes need ti-ri (§3, p. 76).
  • Trills begin with their appoggiatura from above and end with two little notes (IX §7, p. 103); in gay music they are quick (XII §14, p. 132).
  • Small notes. An appoggiatura on the beat is tipped gently, swelled, and the main note slurred to it more softly (VIII §4, p. 93). A small note between two notes a third apart is a passing appoggiatura, played in the French way, in the time of the note before it (VIII §6, pp. 93–94).
  • Breath: between a long note and a short one (VII §3, p. 87); after a cadence, before a new idea (§4, p. 88); wide leaps suit it (§8, p. 89). All seven of Blavet’s marks fit.
  • Repeated ideas alternate forte and piano (XII §23, p. 133).

Quantz’s Rules and This Movement

Rules Quantz

Paraphrased from the Versuch, XI (good execution), XII (the manner of playing the Allegro) and XVII.vii (tempo and dance music), Reilly pp. 123–124, 130–134 and 284–291.

  • Tempo by the pulse. Quantz measures tempo by a pulse of about 80 beats a minute (XVII.vii §§49–51, pp. 284–286). The tempo word and the fastest notes of the passage-work both have to be considered, since no more than eight very fast notes can be played in the time of one pulse beat (§51, p. 285).
  • The large 2. The French use alla breve for bourrées, entrées, rigaudons, gavottes and rondeaux, and write a large 2 instead of the crossed C; it means the notes go twice as fast (XVII.vii §58, p. 290).
  • The gavotte. A bourrée and a rigaudon are played gaily, with a short and light stroke, a pulse beat to each bar. A gavotte is almost like a rigaudon, but a little more moderate in tempo (§58, p. 291).
  • Dotted notes in dances. The eighth after a dotted quarter is played very short and sharp, and the dotted note with emphasis. Quantz names the loure, sarabande, courante and chaconne, and adds that all dotted notes are treated this way if time allows (§58, p. 290).
  • Strict tempo matters in all music and most of all in music for the dance (§57, p. 290).
  • French moderation. Quantz remarks that French musicians of his day kept a moderate speed in lively pieces (XVII.vii §50, p. 285, note).
  • Unequal notes (XI §12, pp. 123–124): see Rules We Apply above. The rule holds only as long as no quicker notes are mixed in; then those are the quickest.
  • Variations. Few are allowed in an Allegro, and none before the repetition; a solo with two reprises suits them best (XII §27, p. 134).

Guidelines and Our Readings guideline ours

  • Why Quantz for a French sonata. The sonata was printed in Paris about twenty years before the Versuch. Quantz knew Blavet in person, and his paragraphs on dance music are about the French style. Where Blavet’s own marks speak (breaths, slurs, trills), they come first; Quantz is used to explain them and to fill the gaps. Hotteterre’s Principes (1707) would be the nearer French witness and has not been read for this page.
  • Tempo. Quantz’s danced gavotte, a little slower than a pulse beat to the bar, would make the sixteenths of mm. 41–43 sixteen to a beat, twice what he says can be played. This is a sonata movement with a title and the word Moderato, not music for dancers. We put the pulse on the half note, eight sixteenths to a beat, his limit: half note = 80. Takahashi prints 90 with the word Tranquillo. A little under 80 fits Moderato as well; the slider is there to try it.
  • Unequal eighths. The eighths are the quickest notes until m. 41, the tempo is moderate, and Blavet slurs them in pairs, the grouping that suits long–short best. The page plays the second eighth of a pair a little late, about 7 to 5, where Quantz’s exceptions do not apply: not under slurs of three or four notes, not on repeated notes, not after a dotted note. From m. 41 the sixteenths are the quickest notes and the eighths are even. How unequal is a matter of taste; Quantz says only that it is less than a dotted rhythm. The switch above the score turns it off.
  • Blavet’s slurs and the dance style. Quantz wants dance music more detached than slurred. Blavet slurs a great deal here, but almost always in pairs and threes, never across the middle of a bar or a barline, and he leaves the cadence bars and the ends of the figures to the tongue. The title suggests why: short slurred groups, tongued notes between them, and repeated figures make the chatter.
  • Dotted figures (mm. 26–29). The page plays the written values. Quantz’s rule would make the eighth shorter and sharper; a player can do so.
  • Repeats. Blavet prints a repeat sign for each half. The page plays each half once. Quantz’s advice is to keep the first time plain and to vary, if at all, in the repetition (XII §27, p. 134); Blavet writes one variation himself, at the end.
  • Names. “Les Caquets” is Blavet’s title: caquet is chatter or cackling. Petite reprise and double are French terms of the time that Blavet does not print here. Tied bass, falling fifths, rule of the octave and the long and simple cadences are terms of Neapolitan partimento teaching (Durante’s and Fenaroli’s rules); Ponte is Joseph Riepel’s word, taken up by Robert Gjerdingen. All are applied by us.
  • Where the pattern names come from. They follow the partimento catalog made for this site, 96 patterns that were checked there against Fenaroli, Durante, Sanguinetti’s The Art of Partimento and Gjerdingen. Those books were not read again for this page; each pattern was matched to its entry in the catalog by its bass degrees and figures.
  • Patterns that fit loosely. The movement opens on the dominant, so it has none of the usual opening patterns. The tied bass stops after three notes each time, where the catalog’s chain can run down to the third degree. In the long cadence the standard form holds a fourth over the dominant before the major third; Blavet’s dominant has none. The catalog marks its entry for the Ponte as taken from notes only, not yet checked against Gjerdingen’s book.
  • Cadence types (PAC, IAC, HC) are modern labels. We call a cadence perfect only when the bass goes 5–1 in root position and the flute ends on 1 as the tonic arrives.

Notes on the Reading

The Score

Two staves as in the Paris print: flute and figured bass. Trills are shown with Blavet’s +, breaths with his h, and the small notes as he places them. The dash in the figures holds the harmony of the note before; a slashed 5 is a diminished fifth; 7 6 and a dash over one note follow each other in time (m. 30). The bass goes into the tenor clef from the middle of m. 14 to the middle of m. 16, as in the print. Accidentals follow modern use and hold to the end of the bar. The bar with the repeat sign (m. 12) is engraved as one bar with the sign inside it, so the bar numbers stay those of a modern edition.

Readings we are less sure of: the C on the seventh eighth of m. 7, which carries no sign in the print (read C natural, with a natural in parentheses; the sharp on the first note of the bar may be meant to hold, and Takahashi’s edition reads C♯); the fourth eighth of m. 24, printed without a flat (read B♭, as in the parallel bars 4, 10 and 39 and with the E♭ in the bass); the second bass note of m. 27, printed without a flat (read B natural); the exact ends of the pair slurs in m. 30; and the place of the 6 over the bass of m. 30, read on the third quarter.
