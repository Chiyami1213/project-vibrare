Analysis · Blavet · Sonata II “La Vibray,” Op. 2 No. 2 · Movement V

V. Allegro

The last movement of Michel Blavet’s D minor sonata for flute and basso continuo: an Allegro in three-four time, in two repeated halves. A motto of four leaping eighths opens each half. Around it Blavet sets the stock patterns of partimento teaching: falling fifths with a Fonte inside, an Indugio and converging cadence that he uses five times in three keys, a chromatic Monte, a tied bass, and at the end a petite reprise an octave lower. Below: the form, every cadence, the partimento patterns in the figured bass, and the flute part in three versions: as Blavet printed it with his own slurs and breath marks, read by Quantz’s rules, and as Toshio Takahashi revised it for the Suzuki Flute School.

  • Two repeated halves, 28 + 49 bars
  • Keys D minor → A minor ‖ A minor → F major → D minor

Cadences

Every cadence marked on the score, with the evidence from the bass and the figures. All labels are our reading ours.

  • Five half cadences, one approach: an Indugio that ends in a converging cadence. The bass goes 4, ♯4 with 7, 6 and 5̸, 5, a bar for each of the first two, and the flute comes down to the leading tone: in D minor (mm. 8, 62), in A minor (mm. 18, 36) and in F major, where the flute lands on the third of the dominant chord (m. 44).
  • Four full cadences, all with a trilled dotted quarter in the flute and a 4 over the dominant that resolves. Three are compound cadences (cadenza composta), 1–5–1 in the bass: A minor (m. 28) and D minor twice (mm. 72, 77). The one in F major is a long cadence (cadenza lunga), 3–4–5–1 (m. 52). The flute touches the closing note an eighth early each time.
  • What follows a half cadence. After mm. 8 and 36 the falling fifths with their Fonte; after mm. 18 and 62 a held note of almost three bars over the first steps of the rule of the octave; after m. 44 the rising 5–6 in F major.
  • Elisions. The F of m. 52 and the D of m. 72 close a cadence and begin the next section in the same breath: one sixteenth and one eighth long.
  • The two halves rhyme: mm. 60–71 are mm. 16–27 a fourth higher, apart from the first two bars, which are those of the theme. The first half ends in A minor with the music that ends the second in D minor.
  • Breath and cadence: four of Blavet’s fourteen breath marks follow a cadence note at once (mm. 18, 44, 62, 72). Our reading adds one after each of the cadences he leaves unmarked before a new section (mm. 8, 28, 36).

Partimento Patterns

The bass patterns under every bar, named as in the site’s partimento catalog: the bass motions and cadences of Neapolitan partimento teaching (the rule of the octave, the tied bass, the bass that falls by fifths, the rising 5–6, Fenaroli’s cadences) and the galant schemata (Do–Re–Mi, Fonte, Monte, Indugio, converging cadence). Schema

Paired Do–Re–Mi

The opening schema of the movement. The flute’s main notes go D–E and then E–F, a question and its answer, over 1–7 and 5–1 in the bass (mm. 1–4). The second half begins with the same schema in A minor, A–B and B–C (mm. 29–32). Blavet sets each Do, Re and Mi as a whole bar: the motto on the first note of a pair, a held note on the second.

Schema

Indugio and Converging Cadence

The bass lingers on the fourth degree for a bar, raises it a half step for a bar under the figures 7, 6 and 5̸, and steps up to the dominant, while the flute comes down the scale in slurred pairs. Each time the fourth degree is reached through its own dominant seventh. Five times: in D minor (mm. 5–8, 59–62), in A minor (mm. 15–18, 33–36) and in F major (mm. 41–44).

Bass motion

Falling Fifths, With a Fonte Inside

The bass falls a fifth and rises a fourth through the whole key: D, G, C, F, B♭, E, A, D (mm. 9–16). Its first four chords are a Fonte: a cadence in G minor and the same a step lower in F major, borrowed from the relative major as a Fonte in a minor piece must be. The second half has the Fonte on D minor and C major (mm. 37–40) and then the first one again (mm. 52–55).

Rule of the octave

1–2–3 Under a Held Note

After the half cadence the flute holds one note for almost three bars and trills it in the last. Under it the bass breaks the chords of the first three scale steps in sixteenths: a triad on 1, a major sixth on 2 (♯6), a sixth on 3: A–B–C in A minor (mm. 19–21), D–E–F in D minor (mm. 63–65). The third step is the first note of the Monte.

Schema

Chromatic Monte

The bass climbs by half steps, and every raised note is a leading tone that closes onto the next: C–C♯–D–D♯–E in A minor (mm. 21–23) and F–F♯–G–G♯–A in D minor (mm. 65–67), figured 6 and 5̸ by turns: two steps, to the fourth and fifth degrees. In F major Blavet uses all three steps, to B♭, C and D, with each leading tone decorated in eighths (mm. 45–48).

Bass motion

Tied Bass, 2 to 6

The bass steps down one note a bar. Each note is first a consonance under a 6; then the chord changes over it, it becomes a dissonance under a 2, and it resolves down a step to the next 6 (mm. 23–26, 67–70, 72–75). Blavet strikes the note again where a partimento would tie it. At the end of the chain in D minor the bass comes back to its note, 1–7–1, with the figures 2 and 5̸ (mm. 70–71).

Bass motion

Rising 5–6 in Tenths

In F major the first note of each bar in the flute and the bass note under it rise together a tenth apart (mm. 44–50). Above them the top note of the flute’s sixteenths is the sixth of one bass note and the fifth of the next (mm. 46–49): the rising 5–6 of partimento teaching, both upper voices in one flute line. The bass reaches each new note through the note below it, figured 6.

Bass motion

Circle of Fifths

From the cadence in F major the bass falls by fifths through all seven degrees of D minor, D, G, C, F, B♭, E, A, D (mm. 52–59), nearly every chord with a seventh. It begins with the Fonte of mm. 9–12, and the flute plays its turning figure from there at the old pitches (mm. 53, 55, 57 = mm. 10, 12, 14). The circle ends on D with 7, the dominant of the fourth degree, and the Indugio follows.

Cadence

Compound Cadence and Long Cadence

Three of the four full cadences are Fenaroli’s compound cadence, 1–5–1 with a fourth over the dominant that falls to the major third: A minor (mm. 27–28) and D minor (mm. 71–72, 76–77). The cadence in F major is a long cadence: three sixth chords step down, then the bass steps 3–4–5 with 4 and 3 on the dominant and leaps to 1 (mm. 50–52).

Form

Petite Reprise

The cadence of m. 71 closes on a D that is only an eighth long. After Blavet’s breath the last five bars come again an octave lower in both parts (mm. 72–76), and a bar of D ends the movement (m. 77). The Allemanda of this sonata ends with a petite reprise too; the Andante marks its own with a P.

Rules We Apply Quantz

Paraphrased from the Versuch (1752), chapters VI, VII, IX and XII, in Reilly’s translation.

  • Slurs as marked. Notes that are to be slurred are played as they are marked, and notes that are to be tongued are not slurred (XII §21, p. 133). Under a slur only the first note is tipped (VI.i §10, p. 74).
  • The first note of a quick figure is stressed and held a little, which also keeps the tempo steady (XII §5, p. 130). Blavet’s slur on notes two to four of the turning figure leaves exactly that note standing alone.
  • Eighths and quarters. Where sixteenths are the quickest notes, eighths are tipped briefly and quarters are played in a singing, sustained manner (XII §22, p. 133). Leaping eighths take ti, stepwise ones di (VI.ii §7, p. 77). Dotted notes take ti-ri (VI.ii §3, p. 76).
  • Long notes are sustained by swelling and diminishing the tone, and the quick notes after them are set off by a gay execution (XII §18, p. 132). Long notes during which the other parts have quick motion represent the majestic (XII §24, p. 133).
  • Passage-work. The notes that carry the melody are slightly held and stressed, and the others may be slurred gently to them (XII §16, p. 132). The deeper a leap, the more strongly its low note is played (XII §17, p. 132).
  • Trills in gay music are played happily and quickly (XII §14, p. 132). A shake begins with its appoggiatura and ends with two little notes (IX §7, p. 103).
  • Breath: between a long note and a short one (VII §3, p. 87); after a cadence, before a new idea (§4, p. 88); after a tied note (§5, p. 88); only where it is needed (§7, p. 89). All fourteen of Blavet’s marks fit.
  • Repetitions are helped by the alternation of piano and forte (XII §23, p. 133).

Quantz’s Rules and This Movement

Rules Quantz

Paraphrased from the Versuch, XI (good execution), XII (the manner of playing the Allegro) and XVII.vii (tempo), Reilly pp. 123–124, 129–134 and 284–286.

  • Tempo by the pulse. Quantz measures tempo by a pulse of about 80 beats a minute (XVII.vii §§49–51, pp. 284–286). For an Allegro in three-four time whose passage-work is in sixteenths or eighth triplets, the beat cannot be fixed in one bar but in two: a pulse beat on the first and third quarters of one bar and on the second quarter of the next, three beats for six quarters (§51, p. 286). That is quarter note = 160.
  • Two kinds of triple time. Triple meters have a fast and a moderate kind, as common time does. Three-four time with only eighths is the fast kind; with sixteenths it is the moderate one, played twice as slowly (§50, p. 285). A very fast three-four with only six quick notes in the bar takes a pulse beat to the bar (§51, p. 286); this movement has twelve.
  • French moderation. Quantz remarks that French musicians of his day kept a moderate speed in lively pieces (§50, p. 285, note).
  • The Allegro’s character is gaiety and liveliness (XII §3, p. 129). Its passage-work is played roundly and distinctly, with a tongue that is now firm, now gentle (§4, p. 129).
  • Never hurry. The tempo is set by the hardest passage (XII §8, p. 130); what moves the listener is expression, not speed (§11, p. 131).
  • The theme and the rest. When the principal subject of an Allegro returns often, it must be set apart from the auxiliary ideas each time, by the tongue, the chest and the lips and by piano and forte (XII §23, p. 133).
  • Gay, majestic, flattering. Gaiety shows in short notes that leap and step, with a lively tongue; the majestic in long notes over quick motion in the other parts and in dotted notes; flattery in slurred notes that move by step (XII §24, pp. 133–134).
  • Variations. Few are allowed in an Allegro, and none before the repetition; a solo whose Allegro has two reprises suits them best (XII §27, p. 134).
  • Unequal notes. In a moderate tempo the quickest notes are played a little unequally, but not quick passage-work in a very fast tempo, where only the first of every four notes gets length and strength; and never under a slur of more than two notes (XI §12, pp. 123–124).

Guidelines and Our Readings guideline ours

  • Why Quantz for a French sonata. The sonata was printed in Paris about twenty years before the Versuch. Quantz knew Blavet in person, but he writes as a Berlin musician of 1752. Where Blavet’s own marks speak (breaths, slurs, trills), they come first; Quantz is used to explain them and to fill the gaps. Hotteterre’s Principes (1707) would be the nearer French witness and has not been read for this page.
  • Tempo. Quantz’s rule gives quarter note = 160; Takahashi prints 126 for students. The page starts at 144: under Quantz’s figure, with his remark on French moderation in mind, and slow enough for the slurred sixteenths of mm. 44–59 to keep their shape. The slider reaches both other figures.
  • The motto and its answer. By Quantz’s terms the theme joins two characters: four leaping eighths and a quick shake, which are gay, and a note held for a bar over a running bass, which is majestic. We tip the eighths briefly and let the held note swell and fall; the page’s playback does not swell it.
  • Blavet’s slurs and Quantz’s stress. In the turning figure Blavet leaves the note on the beat alone and slurs the other three. In mm. 44–49 he starts each pair on the moving note and slurs up to the repeated one. Both leave the stress where Quantz puts it, on the first note of a figure and on the notes that carry the line. Takahashi’s four-note slurs and regrouped pairs make the same notes smoother and more even.
  • Where the flute breathes in mm. 50–62. Blavet marks no breath between the cadence in F major and the half cadence of m. 62. The sixteenth rests of mm. 54, 56 and 58 give room for one; he did not need to mark them.
  • Unequal sixteenths. At this speed Quantz’s rule leaves only a slight length on the first of every four sixteenths, and nearly all the sixteenths are under Blavet’s slurs. The page plays them evenly.
  • Repeats. Blavet prints a repeat sign for each half. The page plays each half once. Quantz’s advice for such a movement is to keep the first time plain and to vary, if at all, in the repetition (XII §27, p. 134).
  • Names. Do–Re–Mi, Indugio, converging cadence and Comma are Robert Gjerdingen’s terms, from Music in the Galant Style (2007); Fonte and Monte are Joseph Riepel’s (1755), taken up by Gjerdingen. The rule of the octave, the tied bass with 2 and 6, the bass that falls a fifth and rises a fourth, the rising 5–6, the fauxbourdon and the simple, compound and long cadences belong to Neapolitan partimento teaching (Durante’s and Fenaroli’s rules). Petite reprise is the French term for a repeat of the closing bars. Blavet uses none of these words here.
  • Where the pattern names come from. They follow the partimento catalog made for this site, 96 patterns that were checked there against Fenaroli, Durante, Sanguinetti’s The Art of Partimento and Gjerdingen. Those books were not read again for this page; each pattern was matched to its entry in the catalog by its bass degrees and figures.
  • Patterns that fit loosely. A partimento ties its bass in the 2–6 chain; Blavet strikes the note again. A Fonte has two steps; Blavet goes on to a third and a fourth (mm. 13–16). The paired Do–Re–Mi usually answers 1–7 with 7–1, or 1–5 with 5–1; Blavet answers 1–7 with 5–1. The Indugio usually carries a 6 or 6 over 5 on the fourth degree; Blavet’s carries a plain triad, reached through its own dominant. In the long cadence the third and fourth degrees have no figures in the print.
  • Cadence types (PAC, HC) are modern labels. We call a cadence perfect only when the bass goes 5–1 in root position and the flute ends on 1 as the tonic arrives.

Notes on the Reading

The Score

Two staves as in the Paris print: flute and figured bass. Trills are shown with Blavet’s + and breaths with his h. A slashed 5 is a diminished fifth; figures printed side by side over one note (7 6 5̸, 4 ♯, 4 3) follow each other in time. Accidentals follow modern use and hold to the end of the bar, so the score adds a natural where the print relies on the older rule (the G of m. 23, the C of m. 67). From the second note of m. 62 to the end of m. 71 the print puts the bass in the alto clef, and the score does the same. The repeat sign after m. 28 is drawn both ways, and the one at the end closes the second half.

Readings we are less sure of, or that differ between matching bars: the last bass note of m. 30, F natural in the print, where m. 2 would give F♯; the C of m. 23, without the 6 that its match in m. 67 has; the 4 of m. 73 without the natural sign of m. 68; the G♯ of mm. 18 and 36 without a +, where mm. 8 and 62 have one; and the first two eighths of m. 66 without the slur of m. 22. All are given as printed. The bass goes down to low C in m. 40 and low D in m. 77, and the flute up to high E in m. 66.
