Fugue in A Minor, BWV 889 Angular Subject

A stark three-voice fugue on an angular subject in quarter notes that falls a diminished seventh and then descends in eighths. It is short, harsh and concentrated.

The Six Steps

Scott Roberts’s method (2004) asks what is notable in the opening and whether the fugue later answers it. Roberts does not analyze this fugue, and the findings below come from the automatic analysis, so all of them are ours.

  1. STEP 1

    Identify the Traditional Fugue Elements

    Mark every entry, the countersubject, codettas, episodes and coda.

    • 6 entries (5 complete), 1 in stretto.
    • 1 regular countersubject; 3 episodes; a coda.
  2. STEP 2

    Find the Notable Properties

    Look in the exposition for linear lines (complete or blocked), patterns, suspended degrees and contrasts.

    • A falling line: the subject’s longest stepwise run is ⑤ (1 notes).
    • Top note: ⑥ (F); the whole fugue reaches C at m. 10, beat 1½.
  3. STEPS 3–5

    Patterns and Other Segmentations

    Look for growing, shrinking or mirrored section lengths, and for other ways to cut the fugue.

    Section lengths in bars: 7.2 + 4.8 + 2.2 + 5.8 + 2 + 2.6 + 2.1 + 1. The cadences found cut it at m. 12, beat 3.

  4. STEP 6

    Decide How the Fugue Responds

    A property is reconciled when it comes back with the trait that made it notable, now completed.

    Not answered by this test. No line in the last bars carries the subject’s falling line further. That points to a literal fugue in Roberts’s terms; the reading is ours.

Articulation: HIP and Busoni

Bach wrote no dots or dynamics in this fugue, and no slurs. The score above can show two answers to that silence: a reading built from eighteenth-century treatises, and Busoni’s marks in his edition of Book II. Switch between them with the Articulation control on the score; playback follows the layer you pick.

  • Staccato: a little less than half the value
  • Tenuto: full value
  • Dash and dot: weighted but separated
  • Slur: full value, slight weight on the first note
  • Comma: lift before an entry

The HIP Reading ours

The rules are the treatises’ own. Deciding which notes they apply to is our reading, made by the same rules for every fugue in the book. Outside the subject, every stepwise group of three or more short notes within one beat (one bar in 3/8 and 6/16), in any voice, gets a slur as well.

  • RUnmarked quarters and eighths in a moderate tempo get the ordinary touch: about half their value, played with some fire (C. P. E. Bach III §22, p. 157). No sign is written for it, so these notes stay unmarked.
  • RDetached notes belong mostly to leaps and quicker tempos and last a little less than half their value (III §17, p. 154). Quantz gives leaping eighths the short tongue stroke (VI, p. 73).
  • RSlurred notes are held full length and belong mostly to stepwise motion; the first note under a slur gets a slight pressure (III §18, pp. 154–155).
  • RA note marked Ten., a tied note and the dot of a dotted note are held (III §22, p. 157; §23, pp. 157–158; §25, p. 159).
  • RIn runs every note is distinct, yet the fingers are not lifted early: Quantz describes drawing the fingertips back along the keys as J. S. Bach’s own manner (XVII.vi §18, pp. 259–260).
  • RGive more weight to the note on which a subject enters, to long notes among quick ones and to dissonances (Quantz XVII.vi §10–11, pp. 253–254; C. P. E. Bach III §29, p. 163).
  • RBreathe before a returning subject and take the time from the note before (Quantz VII, pp. 87–88). GAt the keyboard this becomes a small lift before each entry. ours

Busoni’s Marks Busoni

  • FTempo: (none printed; he marks the opening “NB”).
  • FOpening: sempre forte.
  • FSubject: Dashes on the quarter notes, “tenuto ma non legato,” and wedges on later eighths; he calls the countersubject’s runs “trotzig” (defiant).
  • FCountersubject 1: slurred each time it returns.
  • FThe subject’s quarters are “tenuto ma non legato” with wedges, while the 32nd-note countersubject is slurred in groups and marked “trotzig” (defiant).
  • FHe notes that the 32nd figures common to prelude and fugue call for almost the same tempo in both, and hears a hidden diminution of the subject in one 32nd passage.
  • FHe points out that the short subject motive is the same as the one in the Kyrie double fugue of Mozart’s Requiem.

How Playback Reads the Marks

Each layer changes only how long a note sounds, as a share of its written value. HIP: staccato 42%, unmarked eighths 55%, the last note of a slur 80%, quarters 70%, sixteenths 92%, slurred, dotted, tied and long notes 95–100%, the note before an entry 60%. Busoni: eighths 50%, sixteenths 85%, longer notes 97%; his staccato 45%, dash and dot 65%. Dynamics and tempo changes are not played. These numbers are ours.
