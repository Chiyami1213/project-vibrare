Fugue in C Major, BWV 846 Stretto Fugue

The first fugue of the Well-Tempered Clavier is made of almost nothing but its subject: 24 entries in 27 bars, no countersubject and no episodes. This page reads it three ways: what makes the subject good fugue material, how the strettos are built, and which cadences and schemas hold it together.

  • 27 bars, 4/4
  • 4 voices
  • 24 entries, 22 complete
  • 7 strettos
  • 0 episodes
  • 4 bars of tonic pedal

The Subject as Fugue Material

The same four questions asked of the Touhou themes: is it a good subject, what answer does it need, what goes against it, and does it work in stretto?

Subject

Fourteen Notes Inside a Sixth

① ② ③ ④ ⑤ ④ ③ ⑥ ② ⑤ ⑥ ⑤ ④ ③

  • FSix quarters long (1½ bars), from the second eighth of m. 1 to beat 3 of m. 2. Busoni points out that entries six quarters apart make the exposition sound like 3/2. Busoni
  • RRange C–A, a sixth, well inside the octave Marpurg asks for (Mann p. 163). These are the six notes of the natural hexachord: no ⑦, no upper ①, no accidentals.
  • RStarts after a rest on ① and ends on ③ on a strong beat, as Marpurg asks: any beat to begin, a strong beat to end (Mann p. 164).
  • RSteps and three small leaps (a fourth up, a fifth down, a fourth up), no runs or arpeggios, so every voice can sing it (Marpurg, Mann p. 162).
Answer

Real, a Fifth Higher

G A B C D C B E A D E D C B

  • RThe subject stays in the key and does not lean on ⑤ at the start, so Marpurg gives it an exact transposition to the dominant (Mann p. 166). Bach’s answer matches note for note.
  • RA subject that ends on ③ of the key is answered on ③ of the dominant (Mann p. 167): E is answered by B.
  • FOrder of entry: alto, soprano, tenor, bass, as subject, answer, answer, subject. The usual alternation would be subject, answer, subject, answer.
  • RMarpurg forbids repeating the theme at once on the same tone in the same register (Mann p. 176). The two answers avoid that by lying an octave apart. ours
Countersubject

None

  • FThe line the alto plays against the first answer (mm. 2–4) never returns with a later entry.
  • RCounterparts begin when the answer enters (Marpurg, Mann p. 199), and they do, but none is kept.
  • GMarpurg calls a fugue that uses almost nothing but its theme a strict fugue, a ricercar, “a master fugue” (Mann p. 156). This is one. ours
  • GWith no fixed countersubject, the subject is its own counterpoint: from m. 7 on, what sounds against the subject is mostly the subject. ours
Stretto

Yes, at Four Distances

  • FBach brings a second entry in after 1 quarter (5 times), 2 quarters (4 times) and 3 quarters (2 times), at the fifth, fourth, sixth, seventh and octave.
  • FThe favorite: one quarter later, a fifth above or a fourth below (mm. 7, 10, 14, 16, 19).
  • RMarpurg: test a theme for stretto before composing and plan the expositions from the results (Mann p. 191). A subject like this one must have been built for it. ours
  • GLater entries may follow closer than in the first exposition (Marpurg, Mann p. 180). Here they go from six quarters apart to one.

Busoni compares the fugue to a building: a flat exposition, a development that rises in three steps to the narrowest stretto in the middle, and a flat coda (his note to Fugue I). His double bars stand after mm. 13 and 18. The cadence plan is ours: the perfect cadence in A minor falls on the downbeat of m. 14, almost exactly halfway, and the fugue then restarts from C in the alto, as in m. 1, but in stretto.

Schemas and Cadences

With no episodes there are no sequences to name. What the partimento vocabulary does find here is the cadences and the two pedal points.

WherePatternWhat Happens
m. 10, beat 3Tenor cadence in GThe lowest voice steps ②–① (A–G) under the soprano’s ⑦–① (F♯–G). A light close: there is no ⑤–① in the bass, and the bass comes in with entry 8 an eighth later.
mm. 13–14Compound cadence in A minorBass ④–⑤–① (D–E–A). Over E the tenor holds A as a fourth and resolves it to G♯ (4–3). Soprano trill on ②. The strongest stop inside the fugue.
m. 19, beat 3Simple cadence on DBass ④–⑤–① (G–A–D), soprano ②–①. The arrival chord has F♯, so it works as the dominant of G and the music moves on.
mm. 21–22Dominant pedalThe bass holds G for four beats under entries 21 and 22.
mm. 23–24Compound cadence in CThe bass runs down and up a scale, then leaps an octave on ⑤ (G–G). The soprano’s C is a fourth over that G and resolves to B (4–3), then ①.
mm. 24–27Tonic pedal with the QuiescenzaOver the held C: B♭ (♭⑦) in the soprano turns the tonic into the dominant of F; entry 24 is in F major with A (⑥); B♮ (♮⑦) returns in the tenor in m. 26; C major closes. ♭⑦–⑥–♮⑦–① is the standard pedal-point close.

The Six Steps

Scott Roberts’s method (2004) asks what is notable in the opening and whether the fugue later answers it. Roberts does not analyze this fugue, so every finding below is ours.

  1. STEP 1

    Identify the Traditional Fugue Elements

    Mark every entry, the countersubject, codettas, episodes and coda.

    • 24 entries: 22 complete and 2 cut short (entries 12 and 14).
    • No countersubject, no episodes, no codetta.
    • A four-bar coda over a tonic pedal.
  2. STEP 2

    Find the Notable Properties

    Look in the exposition for linear lines (complete or blocked), patterns, suspended degrees and contrasts.

    • A blocked ascent: the subject climbs ①–②–③–④ and turns back. It reaches ⑥ by a leap, and never has ⑦ or the upper ①.
  3. STEPS 3–5

    Patterns and Other Segmentations

    Look for growing, shrinking or mirrored section lengths, and for other ways to cut the fugue.

    Busoni’s sections run 6 + 7 + 5 + 5 + 4 bars, with the densest stretto in the middle. The cadences cut it differently: 13 + 10 + 4. Both cuts put a restart at m. 14, where the alto begins from C again.

  4. STEP 6

    Decide How the Fugue Responds

    A property is reconciled when it comes back with the trait that made it notable, now completed.

    Reconciled, in our reading. In the last bar the soprano runs straight up the scale from C to the top C, ①–②–③–④–⑤–⑥–⑦–⑧. That C is the highest note of the fugue and appears only here. By Roberts’s test that makes the fugue rhetorical, but the verdict is ours, not his.

Articulation: HIP and Busoni

Bach wrote no slurs, dots or dynamics in this fugue. The score above can show two answers to that silence: a reading built from eighteenth-century treatises, and Busoni’s marks of 1894. Switch between them with the Articulation control on the score; playback follows the layer you pick.

  • Staccato: a little less than half the value
  • Tenuto: full value
  • Dash and dot: weighted but separated
  • Slur: full value, slight weight on the first note
  • Comma: lift before an entry

The HIP Reading ours

The rules are the treatises’ own. Deciding which notes they apply to is our reading. Outside the subject, every stepwise group of three or more short notes within one beat, in any voice, gets a slur as well.

  • RUnmarked quarters and eighths in a moderate tempo get the ordinary touch: about half their value, played with some fire (C. P. E. Bach III §22, p. 157). No sign is written for it, so these notes stay unmarked.
  • RDetached notes belong mostly to leaps and quicker tempos and last a little less than half their value (III §17, p. 154). Quantz gives leaping eighths the short tongue stroke (VI, p. 73).
  • RSlurred notes are held full length and belong mostly to stepwise motion; the first note under a slur gets a slight pressure (III §18, pp. 154–155).
  • RA note marked Ten., a tied note and the dot of a dotted note are held (III §22, p. 157; §23, pp. 157–158; §25, p. 159).
  • RIn runs every note is distinct, yet the fingers are not lifted early: Quantz describes drawing the fingertips back along the keys as J. S. Bach’s own manner (XVII.vi §18, pp. 259–260).
  • RGive more weight to the note on which a subject enters, to long notes among quick ones and to dissonances (Quantz XVII.vi §10–11, pp. 253–254; C. P. E. Bach III §29, p. 163).
  • RBreathe before a returning subject and take the time from the note before (Quantz VII, pp. 87–88). GAt the keyboard this becomes a small lift before each entry. ours

On the Subject

  • GC–D–E: three rising eighths in ordinary touch, unmarked. A violinist would take them as an upbeat, starting up-bow so that the F on beat 3 falls on a down-bow (L. Mozart IV §3, p. 74, and §16, p. 78). ours
  • GF–G–F–E: one slur. The dotted F is held, the two thirty-seconds are quick and light, and the figure lands on E. ours
  • GA and D: staccato dots. The subject’s three leaps (a fourth up, a fifth down, a fourth up) are its most recognizable feature, and detaching them makes every stretto entry audible. ours
  • GTied G and the four sixteenths: one slur, with the weight on the G, which is the long note of the subject. ours

Busoni’s Marks Busoni

  • FTempo: Moderato, quasi Andante.
  • FSubject: one slur from C to the D, with a tenuto dash on each of the first four notes and a staccato dot on the D. Then a tenuto dash on the tied G and a second slur that runs on into the continuation.
  • FEverything Else: long slurs, often two bars and more. At m. 14 he writes “meno legato ma sempre molto tenuto,” which tells us the first half is meant fully legato.
  • FDynamics: mf at the start, meno f (m. 4), poco f for the bass entry (m. 5), quasi f (m. 7), p (m. 9), mf poco espressivo (m. 12), marcato (m. 15), f (m. 17), calmando (m. 19), dolce (m. 20), crescendo (m. 22), sostenuto at the end.
  • FTempo Changes: pochissimo ritenuto in m. 13, a tempo in m. 14.

How Playback Reads the Marks

Each layer changes only how long a note sounds, as a share of its written value. HIP: staccato 42%, unmarked eighths 55%, the last note of a slur 60%, quarters 70%, sixteenths 92%, slurred, dotted, tied and long notes 95–100%, the note before an entry 60%. Busoni: everything 97–100%, his dotted D 50%. Dynamics and the ritenuto are not played. These numbers are ours; the treatises give “half” and “a little less than half” only.

Urtext and Busoni

The Urtext NBA

The Bärenreiter urtext (Neue Bach-Ausgabe, ed. Alfred Dürr) prints the fugue in its final form and gives an earlier version separately. The final form has the dotted figure in the subject (a dotted eighth and two thirty-seconds). In m. 15 it also records an earlier reading in which the bass entry had the plain subject rhythm.

It has no tempo, dynamics or slurs, and three ornaments: a trill in m. 13 and short trills in mm. 18 and 19, all at cadences.

Busoni’s Edition Busoni

  • Adds a tempo (Moderato, quasi Andante), dynamics, slurs and fingering, and marks every entry with S, A, T or B. He counts the same entries as this page.
  • Divides the fugue into exposition (6 bars), a development in three sections (7, 5 and 5 bars) and a coda (4 bars), and calls it one of the two most perfectly built fugues of Book I.
  • Notes that by form the section ends with m. 18, but that soprano and bass only close half a bar later. That is the cadence on D at m. 19, beat 3.
  • Calls the bass line in m. 20 a “mutilation of the theme” and observes that the stretto grows freer there. We do not count it as an entry.
  • In the last bars he gives an alternative fingering layout after Tausig. The notes are Bach’s.
