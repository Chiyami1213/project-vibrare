Fugue in C Minor, BWV 847 Rhetorical

The opposite of the C major fugue: seven entries, never in stretto, with two countersubjects in triple counterpoint and five episodes built on sequences. This page reads it three ways: what makes the subject good fugue material, which schemas the episodes use, and Scott Roberts’s six-step analysis, for which this fugue is his demonstration piece.

  • 3 voices

Subject

Twenty Notes Inside a Seventh

① ⑦ ① ⑤ ⑥ ① ⑦ ① ② ⑤ ① ⑦ ① ② ④ ⑤ ⑥ ⑤ ④ ③

  • FTwo bars long, from the second eighth of m. 1 to the downbeat of m. 3. It opens three times with the same cell, ①–⑦–① as two sixteenths and an eighth.
  • RRange E♭–D, a major seventh, inside the octave Marpurg asks for (Mann p. 163).
  • REnds on ③ on a strong beat, without a perfect cadence, so the answer can follow at once (Marpurg, Mann pp. 163–164).
  • GShort, clear and easy to remember. Busoni credits the fugue’s popularity to its “almost dance-like rhythm” and to a subject that moves by the simplest intervals. Busoni
Answer

Tonal: One Note Changed

G F♯ G C E♭ G F♯ G A D …

  • RThe subject skips from ① down to ⑤ (C–G) at its fourth note. Marpurg answers a skip between ① and ⑤ with ⑤ and ① (Mann p. 167), so the answer has G–C where an exact transposition would have G–D.
  • GThe next ⑤, six notes later, is answered exactly, by D. Marpurg allows ⑤ to be answered by ② once the theme is clearly heard in its key (Mann pp. 168, 175).
  • RThe subject ends on ③ of the key, the answer on ③ of the dominant (Mann p. 167): E♭ is answered by B♭, in G minor.
  • RIn three parts the third voice enters at the octave of the first, and may wait for a short episode (Marpurg, Mann p. 177). The bass enters on C in m. 7, after Episode 1.
Countersubjects

Two, Both Kept

  • FCS1 (alto, m. 3) runs down a scale in sixteenths, then walks in eighths. CS2 (alto, m. 7) fills in with eighths and rests. Each returns with almost every later entry.
  • RCounterparts begin when the answer enters (Marpurg, Mann p. 199). CS1 does.
  • RFor double counterpoint at the octave Fux forbids the fifth, which inverts to a fourth (Mann p. 108). Between the answer and CS1 in mm. 3–4 no perfect fifth sounds. ours
  • GCS1 moves in sixteenths where the answer has eighths, and in eighths where it has sixteenths, so the two are never rhythmically alike. ours
Stretto

None Used

  • FEvery entry is complete and stands alone. No two entries overlap anywhere in the fugue.
  • GBusoni notes the fugue’s “striking economy of contrapuntal devices.” Its interest lies in the countersubjects and the episodes instead. Busoni
  • RAn episode continues the counterpart until the theme returns, and must be related to the theme (Marpurg, Mann p. 202). All five episodes are made from the subject’s first cell and the CS1 scale. ours

Schemas and Cadences

Each episode is one sequence, and the sequences are the stock bass motions of the partimento tradition. Episodes 1 and 4, and 2 and 5, are pairs.

Episode 1
mm. 5–6		Chromatic Monte, line on topThe soprano climbs ⑤ to ① by half steps (Roberts’s second property). Each chromatic note is a leading tone: A♮ to B♭, B♮ to C. The alto runs the CS1 scale upward against it.
Episode 2
mm. 9–10		Falling fifths (fourth up, fifth down)The bass lands on C, F, B♭, E♭, running the CS1 scale between them. Soprano and alto imitate the subject’s first cell. Leading tones E♮ and A♮ turn each chord into the dominant of the next. Arrives in E♭ major.
Episode 3
mm. 13–14		MonteTwo one-bar segments a step apart: to B♭ (m. 14), then to C minor (m. 15). The soprano has the scale in sixteenths; alto and bass move in parallel thirds.
Episode 4
mm. 17–19		Chromatic Monte in the bassEpisode 1 inverted, in G minor: the bass now has D–E♭–E♮–F–F♯–G. From m. 18, beat 3, the pattern runs once more in C minor, with the scale in the bass and soprano and alto in thirds.
Episode 5
mm. 22–24		Falling fifths, all the way roundEpisode 2 with soprano and alto exchanged, extended: C, F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D, G. It stops on the dominant with a half cadence in m. 25.
mm. 28–29Compound cadenceThe bass leaps an octave on ⑤ (G–G). The alto’s C is a fourth over it and resolves to B♮ (4–3). All three of Roberts’s properties resolve in these bars.

The Six Steps

Scott Roberts’s method (2004), applied by Roberts himself to this fugue. Each card gives Roberts’s rule and what it finds here. Steps 3 to 5 come up empty in this fugue, which Roberts presents as part of the lesson: not every step applies to every fugue.

  1. STEP 1

    Identify the Traditional Fugue Elements

    Mark every entry, the countersubject, codettas, episodes and coda, with voice, key and any devices.

    • Seven entries: alto, soprano (answer), bass, then soprano in E♭, alto in G minor, soprano and bass in C minor.
    • Two countersubjects, CS1 from m. 3 and CS2 from m. 7, which return with most later entries.
    • Five episodes; Episode 4 inverts Episode 1. A brief coda.
  2. STEP 2

    Find the Notable Properties

    Look in the exposition for linear lines (complete or blocked), patterns, suspended degrees, contrasts and structural units.

    • Blocked descent: ⑥–⑤–④–③ in the subject never reaches ①.
    • Chromatic ascent: Episode 1 climbs ⑤ to ① by half steps.
    • Rhythmic cells: the subject divides 4 + 4 + 3 + 3 + 2 eighths, shrinking toward a missing cell of 1.
  3. STEPS 3–5

    Patterns, Other Segmentations Not applicable

    Step 3 looks for growing, shrinking or mirrored section lengths and simple ratios. Step 4 looks for other ways to cut the fugue; step 5 repeats step 3 on them.

    Roberts finds no structural pattern here and no reason for an alternate segmentation. The entries are two bars each and the episodes 2, 2, 2, 3 and 4½ bars.

  4. STEP 6

    Decide How the Fugue Responds

    A property is reconciled when it comes back with the trait that made it notable, now completed. All reconciled: rhetorical; none: literal.

    Rhetorical. In mm. 27–29 the descent reaches ① in soprano and bass, the ascent climbs the melodic minor scale without hindrance, and the cells grow to 4 + 4 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 1. The three resolutions are concurrent, and the fugue ends soon after.

Three Notable Properties

What Roberts found in the opening (step 2) and how the fugue answers each one (step 6). The buttons scroll the score to the place and play it.

Blocked linear descent

⑥–⑤–④–③ in the subject

Why notable
Every discussion of this fugue mentions it. Repeated in each entry, it stops on ③.
Problem
Because it belongs to the subject, every entry repeats it unchanged; only something added to the subject can finish it.
Reconciled
mm. 28–29: the line continues to ① in soprano and bass.
Linear ascent

⑤ to ① by half steps

Why notable
Episode 1 climbs G–A♭–A♮–B♭–B♮–C, through every half step of the minor-mode upper hexachord, instead of taking the melodic minor scale. Episode 4 repeats it in the bass in G minor.
Reconciled
mm. 28–29: G–A♮–B♮–C, straight up the melodic minor scale, cutting into the soprano’s descent.
Rhythmic and melodic pattern

Cells of 4 + 4 + 3 + 3 + 2 eighths

Why notable
The cells follow the subject’s motives and shrink from 4 to 3 to 2, which sets up an expected final cell of one eighth, a “rhythmic capstone”. The pattern runs through the whole fugue; Episode 3 uses cells of four in every voice.
Reconciled
mm. 26–29: 4 + 4 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 1, in all three voices. The bass shows it most plainly (brackets under the bass staff).

The cells to scale, in eighth notes (Roberts, Figures 8 and 12). A cell is grouped by melodic contour, so it can hold a rest.

A Rhetorical Fugue

Why the Descent Is a Problem Roberts

The ⑥–⑤–④–③ line is part of the subject, so it returns with every entry, always stopping on ③. A blocked line inside a subject can only be finished by changing or adding to the subject, which is why its resolution has to wait for the end.

Roberts warns that a subject whose descent stops short is not notable in itself, since a full descent to tonic is expected later anyway. It earns its place here because it is so prominent, and because of how the other properties line up with it.

Cells With a Rest Roberts

The cells are counted by melodic contour, so a cell of four can be three eighths and a rest. In m. 8 the alto drops an expected C, which would complete a tonic chord. Similar gaps appear in mm. 12, 16, 21 and 27; in mm. 12, 16 and 21 they keep the cadences of mm. 13, 17 and 22 from being weakened.

Concurrent Resolution Roberts

All three properties resolve, for practical purposes at the same moment, in mm. 27–29. The soprano and bass finish the descent to C; the rising melodic minor scale breaks in on the soprano’s descent; and the cells close with a single eighth.

Roberts draws two rules from this that hold for every rhetorical organ fugue in his study: the resolutions are concurrent, and once the challenges are resolved, the fugue ends. A brief coda, like the one here over a tonic pedal, only makes a graceful close.

Articulation: HIP and Busoni

Bach wrote no slurs, dots or dynamics in this fugue. The score above can show two answers to that silence: a reading built from eighteenth-century treatises, and Busoni’s marks of 1894. Switch between them with the Articulation control on the score; playback follows the layer you pick.

  • Staccato: a little less than half the value
  • Tenuto: full value
  • Dash and dot: weighted but separated
  • Slur: full value, slight weight on the first note
  • Comma: lift before an entry

The HIP Reading ours

The rules are the treatises’ own. Deciding which notes they apply to is our reading. Outside the subject, every stepwise group of three or more short notes within one beat, in any voice, gets a slur as well.

  • RUnmarked quarters and eighths in a moderate tempo get the ordinary touch: about half their value, played with some fire (C. P. E. Bach III §22, p. 157). No sign is written for it, so these notes stay unmarked.
  • RDetached notes belong mostly to leaps and quicker tempos and last a little less than half their value (III §17, p. 154). Quantz gives leaping eighths the short tongue stroke (VI, p. 73).
  • RSlurred notes are held full length and belong mostly to stepwise motion; the first note under a slur gets a slight pressure (III §18, pp. 154–155).
  • RA note marked Ten., a tied note and the dot of a dotted note are held (III §22, p. 157; §23, pp. 157–158; §25, p. 159).
  • RIn runs every note is distinct, yet the fingers are not lifted early: Quantz describes drawing the fingertips back along the keys as J. S. Bach’s own manner (XVII.vi §18, pp. 259–260).
  • RGive more weight to the note on which a subject enters, to long notes among quick ones and to dissonances (Quantz XVII.vi §10–11, pp. 253–254; C. P. E. Bach III §29, p. 163).
  • RBreathe before a returning subject and take the time from the note before (Quantz VII, pp. 87–88). GAt the keyboard this becomes a small lift before each entry. ours

On the Subject

  • GC–B–C: a written-out mordent, slurred, with the last C released short. On the violin it is one bow stroke with the first note a little stronger (L. Mozart VII.i §20, pp. 123–124). ours
  • GG, A♭, D, G, D: the eighths between the cells leap, so they get staccato dots. ours
  • GF–G: two rising sixteenths, distinct but not lifted, leading to the A♭. ours
  • GA♭–G–F–E♭: the A♭ is the only quarter note and the top of the minor sixth. It gets the weight, and the three notes after it fall away under one slur. ours
  • GCountersubjects: the sixteenth-note scale is played distinct and connected, the eighths in ordinary touch, with dots where they leap. ours

Busoni’s Marks Busoni

  • FTempo: Allegretto, vivacemente.
  • FSubject: a slur over each C–B–C cell; a dash with a dot on G, A♭ and the later G; a staccato dot on each D before a leap; dots on F–G with a small crescendo; a slur from the A♭ to the end.
  • FAnswer: in mm. 3–4 the two notes that correspond to the Ds carry a dash with a dot instead of a plain dot.
  • FFirst Countersubject: dots under a slur on the sixteenth-note scale, “quasi staccato.”
  • FEighth-Note Counterpoint: staccato throughout, by his footnote in m. 3.
  • FDynamics: p at the start, mp for the bass entry (m. 7), p quasi staccato (m. 9), p subito and mf (m. 15), meno leggiero (m. 20), poco a poco crescendo (m. 22), più crescendo (m. 25), f non leggiero with the bass in octaves (m. 26), poco largamente (mm. 28–29), ff at the coda, rallentando.

How Playback Reads the Marks

Each layer changes only how long a note sounds, as a share of its written value. HIP: staccato 42%, the last note of a cell 50%, unmarked eighths 55%, quarters 70%, sixteenths 92%, slurred and long notes 95–100%, the note before an entry 60%. Busoni: staccato eighths 45%, dash with dot 65%, dotted sixteenths 60–70%, other sixteenths 90%, slurred and long notes 90–100%. Dynamics, tempo changes and the added octaves are not played. These numbers are ours; the treatises give “half” and “a little less than half” only.

Urtext and Busoni

The Urtext NBA

The Bärenreiter urtext (Neue Bach-Ausgabe, ed. Alfred Dürr) gives the fugue without tempo, dynamics, slurs or ornaments. The bass ends on a plain C doubled at the octave, held for the last two and a half bars under the final entry.

Busoni’s Edition Busoni

  • Adds a tempo (Allegretto vivacemente), dynamics, articulation and fingering. He wants the eighth-note counterpoint staccato throughout.
  • Reads the form as an exposition (mm. 1–8), a development of “twice 8 measures” (mm. 9–16 and 17–24), a close, and a coda from m. 29, beat 3. In polyphonic terms he calls the whole development one long episode, divided three times by an entry of the theme.
  • At m. 11 he warns that the soprano entry sounds at first like a continuation of the sequence, and asks the player to set it apart.
  • Doubles the bass in octaves from the last bass entry (m. 26) to the end, a tradition he traces to Czerny and defends as no violation of Bach’s style.
  • R Marpurg rules out octave doublings in a regular fugue, while admitting that great masters have used them (Mann p. 201). The urtext has the octave only on the final pedal.
