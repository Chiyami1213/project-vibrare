Study guide · J. J. Fux, Gradus ad Parnassum (1725) · trans. Alfred Mann (Norton, 1965)

The Study of Counterpoint a guide to the five species, with scores you can hear and exercises that check themselves

Fux teaches counterpoint as a staircase. You climb from note against note to florid writing in two voices, then climb again in three and in four. This guide follows his table of contents. Each chapter lists the rules Fux treats as binding, the guidelines he gives as advice, and the figures and exercises of the book. Every score plays on a synthesized harpsichord. Every exercise workbench checks your notes against the rules as you write.

R Rule: a hard prohibition or requirement G Guideline: advice, taste, or “if possible” Fig Figure numbers and pages refer to the Mann translation

Tap any note head to hear it. Scores use modern treble, octave-treble (𝄞 with 8) and bass clefs in place of Fux’s C clefs. Fux’s own figures are labelled with their number; other scores are labelled “after Fig.” or “model” and were written for this guide and checked by the same rule engine.

Front matter · pp. vii–xvi

Introduction (Alfred Mann)

Mann compares the study of counterpoint with the study of perspective. Both grew from the Renaissance turn toward three-dimensional thought. Medieval discantus stacked parts like the levels of a landscape. Contrapunctus, from punctus contra punctum (point against point, note against note), appears around 1300. By 1412 Prosdocimus speaks of cantus contra cantum: judging a whole melody against another.

Tinctoris separates simple counterpoint (equal notes) from diminished or florid counterpoint (several notes against one), and prefers the second: “the variety of proportions produces the most agreeable counterpoint.” Zarlino sums up the Renaissance. Monteverdi’s “second practice” splits the old style from the new. Fux, publishing in 1725, deliberately chooses Palestrina’s style (the stile antico) as the basis for teaching and places three intermediate species between simple and florid counterpoint.

The book’s influence runs through Haydn (who worked every exercise), Leopold and Wolfgang Mozart (Attwood’s studies), Beethoven (via Albrechtsberger, Fux’s successor at St. Stephen’s), Brahms, Strauss and Hindemith.

Front matter · pp. 17–18

The Author’s Foreword

Fux writes for young people “who lack means and a teacher.” His method copies the way children learn letters, then syllables, then words. He gives little space to theory and much to practice, “since action is the test of excellence.”

The book is a dialogue. Aloysius, the master, stands for Palestrina. Josephus is the pupil. Josephus writes; Aloysius corrects. Many rules appear only when a mistake prompts them, so the figures with “mistakes” are part of the teaching.

“Drops wear down the stone not by strength but by constant falling.” (p. 48)

Intervals, Motions and the Four Rules

Before the first exercise, Josephus must know which intervals are consonant, the three kinds of motion, and four rules that govern how consonances follow each other.

R Consonance and dissonance

  1. Perfect consonances: unison, fifth, octave (and their compounds).
  2. Imperfect consonances: third and sixth (and compounds such as the tenth).
  3. Dissonances: second, fourth, diminished fifth, tritone, seventh, and their compounds. p. 20
  4. Consonance and dissonance are reckoned from the bass. Between upper voices the fourth behaves like an imperfect consonance. pp. 112, 131

R The four fundamental rules

  1. Perfect → perfect: contrary or oblique motion only.
  2. Perfect → imperfect: any of the three motions.
  3. Imperfect → perfect: contrary or oblique motion only.
  4. Imperfect → imperfect: any of the three motions. p. 22

Oblique motion, used with care, is allowed in all four. Beethoven reduced the rules to two; Martini to one: never move into a perfect consonance by direct motion.

First Part · Chapter One · pp. 27–40

Note against Note

The simplest species: two voices in notes of equal length (whole notes), consonances only. The given melody, the cantus firmus, never changes; you write a counterpoint above or below it.

R Rules

  1. Equal note values; only consonances.
  2. Begin and end with a perfect consonance. The beginning expresses perfection, the end relaxation. p. 28
  3. The counterpoint must be in the same mode as the cantus. Below the cantus, begin on the unison or octave, not the fifth. Fig. 6
  4. Next-to-last bar: a major sixth if the cantus is in the lower part, a minor third if it is in the upper part. The 7th degree is raised in D, G and A. p. 28 n.
  5. Obey the four rules: no parallel fifths or octaves, and no direct motion into a perfect consonance (hidden fifths and octaves become audible when the skip is filled in). Figs. 7–8
  6. No augmented, diminished or chromatic melodic intervals; no skip larger than a fifth except the octave and the ascending minor sixth. The major sixth and the seventh are forbidden. p. 27 n.; Figs. 15, 103
  7. Mi contra fa: no tritone skip (F–B). Fig. 12
  8. The unison only at the beginning and the end. Figs. 17–18
  9. Never approach an octave or unison by skips in contrary motion from a remote consonance, even in more than two parts. Figs. 19–20
  10. Modes may end only on D, E, F, G, A, C. B has no perfect fifth. Fig. 9

G Guidelines

  • Use contrary and oblique motion as often as possible; they make mistakes easier to avoid.
  • Use more imperfect than perfect consonances; imperfect ones are more harmonious.
  • Repeat a tone only occasionally, and never more than once. Fig. 5 n.
  • Not more than three or four parallel thirds or sixths in a row. p. 21 n.
  • Avoid skips that follow each other in the same direction, and compensate a skip by turning back. Stay within a comfortable register. p. 27 n.
  • Even stepwise, avoid outlining a tritone unless the line continues in the same direction. p. 35 n.
  • The ottava battuta (a tenth to an octave by contrary steps on the downbeat) is left to your discretion. Fig. 16
  • Leaving a unison by skip is bad in itself. Fig. 21 N.B.
  • Fa leads down, mi leads up: a sharp can underline an ascent and avoid a harsh F/F♯ relation. Fig. 21
  • Voice crossing is a permitted expedient. Keep the voices close (neighbouring clefs). Figs. 13–14

First Part · Chapter Two · pp. 41–49

The Second Species

Two half notes against each whole note, in binary meter. The first half falls on the downbeat (thesis), the second on the upbeat (arsis).

R Rules

  1. The downbeat half note must be consonant.
  2. The upbeat half may be dissonant only if it moves stepwise from the previous note and to the following note, filling in the space of a third (diminution). If it skips, it must be consonant. Fig. 24
  3. All rules of motion and progression from the first species apply.
  4. Cadence: in the next-to-last bar, a fifth followed by a major sixth (cantus below), or a fifth followed by a minor third (cantus above). Fig. 25
  5. A skip of a third between two downbeats does not rescue consecutive fifths or octaves; the upbeat note is too short. Figs. 26–29
  6. A skip of a fourth, fifth or sixth does separate them: “the ear forgets” the first downbeat. Figs. 30–32

G Guidelines

  • Consider the ending before you start. p. 42
  • You may begin with a half rest in place of the first note. Fig. 34
  • When the voices are crowded and contrary motion is impossible, a skip of a minor sixth or an octave gets you out. Fig. 34
  • In E mode the fifth in the cadence would be mi against fa; use a sixth. Fig. 37
  • Pay attention not only to the measure you are writing but to the one that follows. p. 48
  • Ternary meter (three against one) is easy: the middle note may be dissonant if all three move stepwise. Fig. 46

First Part · Chapter Three · pp. 50–54

The Third Species

Four quarter notes against each whole note.

R Rules

  1. The first quarter must be consonant.
  2. In five quarters moving stepwise: the 1st is consonant, the 2nd may be dissonant, the 3rd must be consonant, the 4th may be dissonant if the 5th is consonant. Fig. 47
  3. Exception: if the 2nd and 4th are consonant, the 3rd may be dissonant, as a passing note filling in a third. Figs. 48–49
  4. Cambiata (changing note): a dissonant 2nd quarter may skip a third down to a consonant 3rd quarter, then the line turns up by step. Figs. 50–52
  5. No eighth notes yet. p. 52
  6. All earlier rules of motion apply; the next-to-last bar follows the patterns of Figs. 53–54.

G Guidelines

  • A quarter rest may replace the first note. p. 53 n.
  • Avoid sequences (monotonia): repeating the same melodic figure. Fig. 58 n.
  • Flats may be used nonessentially to avoid mi contra fa. Fig. 60
  • Skips from an accented to an unaccented quarter, especially upward, are hard to sing. p. 52 n.
  • Give the utmost attention to the measures that follow.

First Part · Chapter Four · pp. 55–63

The Fourth Species

Two half notes on the same pitch, tied over the bar line: the first on the upbeat, the second on the downbeat. This is the ligature, or syncopation.

R Rules

  1. The upbeat half is always consonant. A consonant ligature has both halves consonant; a dissonant ligature has a dissonant downbeat. Figs. 61–62
  2. A tied dissonance is the retardation of the following note. It must resolve down by step to the consonance that would stand on the downbeat if the retardation were removed. Figs. 63–64
  3. Cantus below: 2nd → unison, 4th → 3rd, 7th → 6th, 9th → octave.
  4. Never unison → 2nd → unison or octave → 9th → octave: without the tie these are consecutive unisons or octaves. 3–2–1 and 10–9–8 are good. Figs. 65–68
  5. Cantus above: 2nd → 3rd, 4th → 5th, 9th → 10th. The seventh resolving to the octave is not used by the masters; the second resolving to the unison is. Figs. 69–71
  6. Cadence: 7–6 in the next-to-last bar (cantus below); 2–3 then the unison (cantus above). p. 60

G Guidelines

  • Resolve all dissonances downward; ascending resolution (Fig. 72) is left aside.
  • Use a ligature in every measure where possible; if none can be made, write plain half notes until one can. p. 60
  • Don’t repeat the same ligature immediately (Josephus skips one in bar 5 of Fig. 73 for that reason). p. 61
  • Successions of fifths through syncopations are judged more indulgently than octaves. p. 57 n., Figs. 75, 77
  • Ligatures may be varied (Fig. 79) or interrupted (Fig. 80); two eighths may appear on the 2nd and 4th quarters, never on the 1st or 3rd. Fig. 81

First Part · Chapter Five · pp. 64–67

The Fifth Species

Florid counterpoint. “As a garden is full of flowers,” it combines all the preceding species: whole notes excepted, any mixture of halves, quarters, ligatures and occasional eighths.

R Rules

  1. Nothing new: each note value obeys the rules of its species (halves as in the 2nd, quarters as in the 3rd, ligatures as in the 4th).
  2. Dissonance only as passing note, cambiata, or prepared suspension resolving down by step.
  3. Eighth notes only in pairs, on the 2nd or 4th quarter, moving by step. Fig. 81
  4. Same beginning and cadence rules (the 4th-species 7–6 or 2–3 is typical).

G Guidelines

  • Above all, write a singable, plastic melodic line with lively movement and variety.
  • Approach the downbeats with oblique motion or syncopations: “it brings about the greatest beauty in counterpoint.” p. 65
  • Two quarters at the start of a bar make the line lag; tie them into what follows or continue with more quarters. Fig. 89
  • Work at this species “constantly and with special diligence above all others.”

Note against Note in Three Parts

“Three part composition is the most perfect of all,” because it holds the complete harmonic triad. Whoever masters three parts finds more parts easy.

R Rules

  1. Three whole notes per bar; the upper two must be consonant with the lowest. A fourth between upper voices is acceptable. p. 71, p. 112
  2. No consecutive fifths or octaves between any pair of voices.
  3. The final chord has only perfect consonances; Fux usually omits the third. A minor third “is not capable of giving a sense of conclusion.” Fig. 105
  4. A major third may end the piece when the fifth would cause consecutive fifths. Figs. 106–108
  5. Skips of the major sixth and the seventh remain prohibited. Fig. 103

G Guidelines

  • Use the harmonic triad (third and fifth above the bass) in every bar unless there is a special reason. p. 71
  • Replace the fifth by a sixth or octave for a better line, or to avoid two perfect consonances in a row. Fig. 91
  • Follow the natural order and the principle of variety; when the bass has mi, a sixth sounds best above it. Figs. 92–97
  • Start the upper voices far enough from the bass to leave room for contrary motion. p. 75
  • Avoid all voices moving in the same direction. Figs. 98–99
  • The rules apply strictly from the bass, and between other voices as far as possible; in three parts you may depart from them when there is no other way. Figs. 100–101
  • A unison is less harmonious than an octave; don’t exceed the five lines of the staff without necessity. p. 79
  • A tenth instead of the final octave is not positive enough. Fig. 100

Second Part · Chapter Two · pp. 86–90

Half Notes against Whole Notes in Three Parts

One voice moves in half notes, the other two in whole notes.

R Rules

  1. The half notes must be concordant with both whole notes. p. 88
  2. Two half notes on the same pitch are not allowed (no ligatures in this species).
  3. All two-part second-species rules and three-part rules apply.

G Guidelines

  • For the sake of the triad, a skip of a third may now make two fifths acceptable, which two-part writing forbids. Fig. 120
  • “Hardly any rule is without exception”: a ligature or a major third may appear in the final cadence to avoid a bad unison or empty octave. pp. 87–88
  • Consider one or two measures in advance before deciding. p. 90

Quarters against Whole Notes in Three Parts

One voice in quarters, the other two in whole notes.

R Rules

  1. The quarters must concur with the whole notes of two other voices.
  2. All third-species and three-part rules apply.

G Guidelines

  • Give the greatest consideration to the notes on the downbeat. p. 91
  • If the triad cannot stand on the first quarter, place it on the 2nd or 3rd quarter.
  • Combine species: one part in quarters, one in halves, one in whole notes (Fig. 134). “I can hardly describe how great the beauty and grace are that are given the composition by this threefold variety.”
  • A whole note may occasionally replace the halves in the next-to-last bar. p. 93 n.

Second Part · Chapter Four · pp. 94–103

The Ligature in Three Parts

The syncopations work as in two parts. The new question is which consonance the third voice takes.

R Rules

  1. The ligature is “nothing but a delaying of the note following”: set in the third voice the consonance you would use if the ligature were removed. Figs. 135–137
  2. The ligature does not change the nature of the consonances: with dissonant syncopations, the essential part is the consonant upbeat. Fig. 138 n.
  3. A ligature cannot make consecutive octaves acceptable (Fig. 139), but it can make fifths acceptable (Fig. 140): the more perfect the consonance, the less harmony it has, so a dissonance resolving to a fifth is more acceptable than one resolving to an octave.

G Guidelines

  • Over a held bass (pedal point), a ligature of dissonances only is “not only correct but even very beautiful.” Fig. 142
  • The seventh should be accompanied by the third; in exercises the octave may be accepted. p. 99
  • If no ligature is possible, rest a whole bar, or let another voice take the bass for a measure. Figs. 144–145
  • Hidden fifths between the upper parts can be cured with a rest. Figs. 152–153
  • Use a ligature in every measure where possible; ligatures are “one of the chief excellences of composition.”

Third Part · Chapter One · pp. 109–116

Four Part Composition

The triad is already complete in three parts, so the fourth voice must double one of its consonances.

R Rules

  1. A four-part chord consists of third, fifth and octave. If the octave would cause bad progressions, double the third (or, more rarely, another member). p. 109
  2. The rules of motion apply from the bass to the other voices and between any two voices, as far as possible. pp. 109–110
  3. The nature of an interval is reckoned from the bass; a fourth between inner voices is fine if fifths and octaves are avoided. p. 112

G Guidelines

  • Place consonances in their natural order: the fifth low, then the octave, then the third (tenth) highest, as the harmonic division produces them. The third close to the bass sounds dull. Figs. 161–162
  • Hidden fifths or octaves are sometimes forced by the melody, by imitation, or by the cantus firmus; the less one departs from the rules, the better. p. 110
  • “The closer the parts are led together the more perfect the sound”: vis unita fortior. p. 112
  • Consider whether the first chord allows good progressions to the following measures. p. 111

Quarters against Whole Notes

Four quarters concordant with three whole notes.

R Rules

  1. The rules of the species in two and three parts, applied against three whole notes.

G Guidelines

  • Put a complete triad on the first quarter; then let the remaining quarters lead simply to the next bar. p. 122
  • A unison on the downbeat detracts from the harmony; a third that sounds only in passing is too weak. Figs. 177–179

Third Part · Chapter Four · pp. 127–134

The Ligature in Four Parts

Tied notes take the same concords as untied ones, with one important exception.

R Rules

  1. Accompany the ligature with the consonances that would be used if it were removed. Figs. 187–189
  2. When the seventh would sound with the fifth in another voice, divide the whole note in that voice (fifth, then sixth). With the octave there is no need. Figs. 190–192

G Guidelines

  • The rule of three undivided whole notes cannot be observed strictly here. p. 130
  • A fourth in the inner voices is barely prominent and acts like an imperfect consonance. Fig. 194
  • Double the second rather than the fourth, so the resolution yields a complete harmony of third, fifth and octave. Figs. 198–199
  • Slight lapses are allowed: the student learns how one may depart, if need be, from the strict rules. p. 134

Third Part · Chapter Five · pp. 135–139

Florid Counterpoint in Four Parts

The florid voice moves over three voices in whole notes, by the principles of four-part writing. The book ends here.

R Rules

  1. The other voices remain in whole notes wherever possible; divide them only where necessary. p. 136

G Guidelines

  • Once the species are mastered singly, combine them: halves, quarters and ligatures in different voices over the same cantus, “so that each part will have its own characteristic motion.” Fig. 204
  • Use the fourfold interchange of the cantus; find new cantus firmi; spend “a year or two” on these exercises before free composition.
  • For more than four voices, “the rules are to be less rigorously observed.”

“The mountain of the muses is to be reached only by a very precipitous path.” (p. 138)

Appendix: Harmonic and Arithmetical Division

Fux derives the intervals and their ranking by dividing the octave on the monochord.

Arithmetical proportion 4:3:2 (equal differences) splits the octave into a fourth below and a fifth above. Here the fourth sits on the bass and counts as a dissonance. Harmonic proportion 6:4:3 (equal differences of reciprocals) splits it into a fifth below and a fourth above; here the fourth is not against the bass and may be treated as an imperfect consonance.

Dividing the fifth harmonically (15:12:10) gives a major third below and a minor third above. The sequence octave → fifth → major third is the natural order Fux uses for spacing chords (Fig. 161) and for the closing chords of old polyphony. Mann adds that the overtone series (C c g c′ e′ g′) shows the same order with reciprocal numbers.
