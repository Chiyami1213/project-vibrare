What music theory is · 500 BCE–today

Introduction: theory that resists its own history

Antiquity Early Middle Ages Late Middle Ages Renaissance Baroque Classical era Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 0 (Thomas Christensen), pp. 1–26

Christensen asks how one can write a history of a subject whose very content keeps changing. Counterpoint and figured bass were once outside "theory", while the old mathematical science of music (musica theorica) now looks marginal. He proposes a flexible network of meanings instead of a fixed definition, and organizes the book by three broad traditions: speculative, regulative and descriptive (analytic).

Key points

  • Three texts from about 1618 show how wide the field was: Campion's practical rule for four-part writing, Descartes's rational Compendium musicae, and Fludd's cosmological Utriusque cosmi.
  • The Greek root theoria means contemplation. Aristotle set it against practical knowledge; the Middle Ages turned this into musica theorica (a quadrivial science of number) versus musica practica (singing, notation, composition).
  • From the 19th-century conservatories onward, theory split into competing disciplines (harmony, counterpoint, form, acoustics, psychology). In the late 20th century it became an academic field of its own.
  • Plan of the book: Part I disciplining theory; Part II speculative traditions; Part III regulative traditions (tonal space, composition, time, tonality); Part IV descriptive traditions (analysis, psychology).
  • Theory is reflexive and historical: it claims timeless truths, yet each version is bound to its own practice and culture.

What music theory is · 300–1900

Mapping the terrain

Antiquity Early Middle Ages Late Middle Ages Renaissance Baroque Classical era Nineteenth century
Cambridge History, ch. 1 (Leslie Blasius), pp. 27–45

Every musical culture draws a "map" of what its theory covers. Blasius follows these maps from Aristides Quintilianus' complete architecture, through scholastic and Renaissance schemes, to the Enlightenment taxonomies and Guido Adler's 1885 plan for musicology.

Key points

  • Aristides Quintilianus' On Music (early 4th c. CE) is the first complete surviving architecture: a theoretical side (physics; technical study of harmonics, rhythmics and metrics) and a practical side (composition and performance).
  • Marchetto of Padua's Lucidarium (1317–18) sorts music by purely sounding (phenomenal) criteria, typical of 14th-century scholasticism.
  • In the 16th century, mapping becomes synthetic: treatises gather everything into one harmonized whole.
  • Mersenne's Harmonie universelle (1636–37) is organized by physics, mechanics and instruments. Kircher's Musurgia universalis (1650) is organized by similitudes, magic and correspondence. Their contrast marks the 17th-century epistemic shift.
  • Forkel's plan (late 18th c.) divides music into a grammar and a rhetoric. Adler's 1885 plan divides musicology into a historical and a systematic field; he printed it beside Aristides' table.

What music theory is · 800–1990

Musica practica: music theory as pedagogy

Early Middle Ages Late Middle Ages Renaissance Baroque Classical era Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 2 (Robert W. Wason), pp. 46–77

Wason tells how practical teaching (singing, counterpoint, thoroughbass, harmony) slowly absorbed the old speculative theory. The story runs from medieval schools and universities to Renaissance composition manuals, thoroughbass, the conservatories of Paris, Vienna and Berlin, and the North American academy.

Key points

  • Medieval teaching was practical and Carolingian in spirit (chant, notation, the monochord). The universities taught Boethian musica as a quadrivial art.
  • Renaissance composition pedagogy grew with humanism and with more ambitious genres (Tinctoris; Zarlino's Le istitutioni harmoniche, 1558).
  • Heinichen's thoroughbass treatise (1728) is a complete composition course that teaches styles through the figured bass.
  • Koch's Versuch (1782–93) builds an empirical vocabulary of phrase and period, borrowed from grammar and rhetoric.
  • Around 1800 the national traditions blend: French fundamental bass, Italian species counterpoint (and partimento), German thoroughbass.
  • Conservatory texts follow: Catel in Paris (1802); Sechter in Vienna (Grundsätze, 1853–54; he gave Schubert a counterpoint lesson); Marx in Berlin.
  • In the 20th century came Louis & Thuille (1906), Schoenberg's Harmonielehre (1911), Schenker's posthumous American influence, Boulanger, and Hindemith at Yale. Babbitt's Princeton program and Perspectives of New Music made the composer-theorist a model; Wason regrets theory's loss of contact with living composition.

What music theory is · 1600–today

Epistemologies of music theory

Baroque Classical era Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 3 (Nicholas Cook), pp. 78–108

What kind of knowledge is music theory? Cook follows how its grounding moved over time. It began in theology and cosmology, passed to natural science (Rameau's claims about nature), and ended in psychology and phenomenology. He argues that modern theory is best understood as performative and intersubjective rather than objectively true.

Key points

  • Dahlhaus's framework: speculative, regulative and analytical traditions rest on different epistemologies. The empirical could live inside a theological world view.
  • The Pythagorean world view survives anachronistically in the 20th century (Schillinger).
  • "Between art and nature": Rameau and later theorists tried to ground harmony in acoustics. Sauveur claimed one could hear up to the 128th partial; Momigny admitted he heard some partials only "by analogy".
  • A performative turn: Rameau created the discursive space theory still inhabits. Theories are validated by intersubjectivity, meaning one theorist can replicate another's "thought experiments", not by objective truth.
  • GTTM (Lerdahl & Jackendoff, 1983) models the intuitions of experienced listeners as a grammar, and Krumhansl's experiments quantified tonal stability. Cook concludes that the epistemologies are plural.

Number, cosmos and science · 550 BCE–400

Greek music theory

Antiquity
Cambridge History, ch. 4 (Thomas J. Mathiesen), pp. 109–135

Greek writings on music and harmonics span more than eight centuries. Mathiesen describes three traditions. The Pythagorean works with number and cosmos. The Harmonicists build rational scale grids. The Aristoxenians start from what the ear perceives. He also describes the systems of tetrachords, genera, tonoi and harmoniai that later reached the West through Latin writers.

Key points

  • Corpus: the Division of the Canon (attr. Euclid, 4th–3rd c. BCE); Aristoxenus' Harmonic Elements (4th c. BCE); Cleonides; Nicomachus' Manual; Theon of Smyrna; Ptolemy's Harmonics; Gaudentius; Porphyry; Aristides Quintilianus; Bacchius; Alypius (notation tables).
  • Pythagorean tradition: numbers constitute reality. The tetractys (1:2:3:4) yields the consonances octave 2:1, fifth 3:2, fourth 4:3; the tone is 9:8; semitones are the limma 256:243 and the apotome 2187:2048. Archytas's arithmetic and harmonic means divide the octave; Plato's Timaeus builds the world-soul from them.
  • The Harmonicists (known through Aristoxenus' criticism) mapped one genus in an octave as a grid of smallest intervals.
  • Aristoxenian tradition: pitch space is continuous and judged by the ear; the tone is divided into 12 parts. Three genera of tetrachord (enharmonic, chromatic, diatonic) have "shades" (Cleonides).
  • Systems: the Greater Perfect System (two octaves from proslambanomenos to nete hyperbolaion) and the Lesser Perfect System (with the synemmenon tetrachord). Tonoi (transposition keys: 13 in Aristoxenus, 15 in later writers, 7 in Ptolemy) and the ethically charged harmoniai of Plato and Aristotle.
  • By the late 4th century CE the traditions blurred. Martianus Capella, Boethius and Cassiodorus passed on what remained.

Number, cosmos and science · 500 BCE–today

Music theory and mathematics

Antiquity Early Middle Ages Late Middle Ages Renaissance Baroque Classical era Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 10 (Catherine Nolan), pp. 272–306

Nolan follows the bond between music theory and mathematics: Pythagorean number, geometric images of pitch and time, combinatorics, and the 20th-century algebra of sets, groups and transformations.

Key points

  • Nicomachus tells the (physically impossible) story of Pythagoras discovering the consonances from the weights of blacksmiths' hammers. It is a founding myth.
  • Pythagoreanism holds that numbers are the elements of reality and that ratios explain cosmic order. Zarlino's senario (numbers 1–6) was challenged by Vincenzo Galilei's physical explanation (1589).
  • Geometric images: Boethian monochords, de Muris' diagrams (1320s), Descartes, Euler's Tonnetz (1739).
  • Combinatorics: Mersenne and Kircher counted melodies; Euler's "degree of agreeableness" (gradus suavitatis).
  • Sets and groups: Babbitt (combinatoriality, 1946–60s), Forte's Structure of Atonal Music (1973), Lewin's Generalized Musical Intervals and Transformations (1987).

Number, cosmos and science · 100 BCE–1050

The transmission of ancient music theory into the Middle Ages

Antiquity Early Middle Ages
Cambridge History, ch. 5 (Calvin M. Bower), pp. 136–167

Bower follows how Greek musical thought turned into Latin "musica". Its path ran through Roman encyclopedists, Martianus Capella and Boethius to the Carolingian revival, where it met chant and produced a new kind of theory.

Key points

  • Latin sources: Varro's liberal arts, Censorinus, Calcidius and Macrobius on Plato, Augustine's De musica (387–89, on rhythm and number).
  • Martianus Capella's De nuptiis (early 5th c.) fixed the seven liberal arts. Its book on Harmonia borrows from Aristides Quintilianus.
  • Boethius' De institutione musica (c. 500) passed on Pythagorean ratios, the Greater Perfect System and the monochord. It also gave the triad musica mundana (of the spheres), humana (of body and soul) and instrumentalis (sounding music). Music belongs to the quadrivium as discrete quantity related to other quantity.
  • Cassiodorus (Institutiones) and Isidore (Etymologiae) were encyclopedists of a Christian culture of learning.
  • In the Carolingian era (Charlemagne, d. 814; Alcuin), Martianus and Boethius were glossed. Aurelian of Réôme and Regino of Prüm wrote early "theory". The Musica and Scolica enchiriadis invented a new tetrachord (T–S–T) of the four finals and the daseian notation.
  • The problem of the 10th–11th centuries was how to fit chant into the quantitative system and account for its chromatic "faults" (vitia).

Number, cosmos and science · 1550–1750

The role of harmonics in the scientific revolution

Renaissance Baroque
Cambridge History, ch. 8 (Penelope Gouk), pp. 223–245

Between the 16th and early 18th centuries, harmonics (the mathematical science of musical ratios) was a model for natural philosophy. Gouk shows it at work in Fludd's neo-Platonic cosmos, in Kepler's planetary music and in Newton's colour spectrum. She then explains why music lost that status.

Key points

  • Fludd's divine monochord shows a neo-Platonic universe held together by sympathy and tension (tonos).
  • Kepler's Harmonices mundi (1619) found the just (syntonic diatonic) scale in the angular velocities of the planets.
  • Newton divided the colour spectrum like the musical octave and saw the inverse-square law as foreshadowed in Pythagorean harmony. Hooke objected to his theory of colour.
  • The body, too, was imagined as harmonic (Cheyne's nerves as strings).
  • In the 18th century music lost its role as an intellectual model. Diderot's Encyclopédie follows Bacon's classification, not the quadrivium.

Tuning and acoustics · 1000–1500

Medieval canonics

Early Middle Ages Late Middle Ages Renaissance
Cambridge History, ch. 6 (Jan Herlinger), pp. 168–192

The canon is the monochord. Medieval theorists defined pitches by dividing its string, and that was the only exact way they had to represent tuning. Herlinger surveys diatonic Pythagorean divisions, 15th-century just divisions, and the rarer chromatic and enharmonic monochords.

Key points

  • A division defines pitches step by step as ratios of string lengths. Meyer catalogues 143 medieval divisions; 13 of them are chromatic or enharmonic.
  • Magadis in utraque parte (by c. 1100) reproduces the Greek system in Pythagorean tuning: tones 9:8, semitones 256:243, and 2187:2048 between b♮ and b♭.
  • Pythagorean tuning makes octaves and fifths pure. Thirds and sixths are harsh (the ditone is 81:64), so they were treated as dissonant or imperfect. The archetypal medieval cadence expands a major sixth to an octave.
  • Just tuning adds pure thirds (5:4). Ramis de Pareia's Musica practica (1482) gave a just monochord and an eight-syllable solmization. This provoked Burzio's attack (1487) and Spataro's defence (1491).
  • Some 15th-century "schismatic" divisions (e.g., Divide primo) obtain nearly pure triads from a chain of fifths.
  • Marchetto's division of the whole tone into five dieses may reflect chromatic monochord theory.

Tuning and acoustics · 1300–1900

Tuning and temperament

Late Middle Ages Renaissance Baroque Classical era Nineteenth century
Cambridge History, ch. 7 (Rudolf Rasch), pp. 193–222

Rasch traces how theorists calculated tunings. They moved from string lengths to root extraction (16th c.), logarithms (17th c.) and cents (Ellis, 19th c.). The systems run from Pythagorean and just intonation through meantone, irregular "well" temperaments and equal temperament.

Key points

  • Pythagorean: pure fifths; 12 fifths overshoot 7 octaves by the Pythagorean comma (≈23.5 cents).
  • Just intonation (Fogliano, Salinas 1577): pure fifths and thirds. It is impossible on a fixed keyboard because of the syntonic comma 81:80 (≈21.5 cents): four pure fifths make a third a comma too wide.
  • Meantone: fifths narrowed by a fraction of the syntonic comma. Quarter-comma meantone makes major thirds pure; Zarlino described 2/7-comma (1558); Salinas described 1/3 and 1/4. The price is a "wolf" fifth (G♯–E♭) and unusable keys on 12-key instruments.
  • Equal temperament: known in theory (Stevin c. 1600 by root extraction; Mersenne; Faulhaber with logarithms) long before it became general practice in the 19th century.
  • Irregular / well temperaments (Werckmeister 1691, Neidhardt, Kirnberger, Vallotti): all keys usable, with different colours.
  • Logarithmic measures: Sauveur's merides (1701); Ellis's cents (1/100 of an equal semitone).

Tuning and acoustics · 1580–1930

From acoustics to Tonpsychologie

Renaissance Baroque Classical era Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 9 (Burdette Green & David Butler), pp. 246–271

The chapter runs from the discovery that pitch is frequency to Helmholtz's physiological acoustics and Stumpf's psychology of tone. It shows how the harmonic series became the foundation for theories of consonance, and how that foundation was then questioned.

Key points

  • Beeckman and Galileo independently linked pitch to frequency. Mersenne gave laws of vibrating strings (length, tension, mass) and heard upper partials.
  • Sauveur (c. 1700) described the harmonic series, nodes and the fundamental, and founded "acoustique". Rameau first used the senario (1–6), then the corps sonore (1737).
  • Beats, and combination or difference tones (Sorge 1745; Tartini 1754 "terzo suono").
  • Helmholtz's Lehre von den Tonempfindungen (1863; Ellis's English translation 1875) offered a resonance theory of hearing. Dissonance is roughness caused by beating partials; consonance is their absence.
  • Stumpf's Tonpsychologie (1883–90) explained consonance as tonal fusion (Verschmelzung), graded octave > fifth > fourth > thirds. Psychology then took over from physics.

Pitch systems: modes to keys · 800–1100

Notes, scales, and modes in the earlier Middle Ages

Early Middle Ages
Cambridge History, ch. 11 (David E. Cohen), pp. 307–363

Two foundations of Western music, the discrete pitch and the scale, were built in the Carolingian era, when cantors joined the chant tradition to Greek harmonics. Cohen follows the eight modes, the note-letters, the tetrachord of the finals and Guido's innovations.

Key points

  • The eight modes (toni) of chant probably came from the Byzantine oktoechos. They were paired as authentic/plagal on four finals: protus, deuterus, tritus and tetrardus (D, E, F, G).
  • Tonaries list chants by mode. Aurelian's Musica disciplina describes modes in qualitative, grammatical terms.
  • Hucbald (c. 900) gives the nine intervals of chant and alphabetic signs, and shows that the finals form a T–S–T tetrachord (D E F G). The Enchiriadis treatises do the same with daseian signs.
  • The Alia musica applies Greek ethnic names to the modes. That is the origin of "Dorian" for mode 1, a misreading of the Greek tonoi.
  • The Dialogus de musica (c. 1000) fixes letters A–G with Γ below and b♭/b♮, and defines modes by final and range (ambitus).
  • Guido (Micrologus c. 1026–28; Prologus; Epistola ad Michahelem) introduced staff lines (F and C), the syllables ut re mi fa sol la from the hymn Ut queant laxis, and the affinities of notes a fifth apart.
  • Hermannus Contractus defined modes by the species of fourth and fifth.

Renaissance modal theory

Renaissance

"Mode" is one of the most layered Renaissance terms. Judd reads it from three sides: theory treatises, printed music collections and editorial practice. Zarlino serves as the lens, and the central question is how mode relates to polyphony.

Key points

  • Practical manuals (the cantorinus; Vanneus' Recanetum, 1533, after Gaffurio's Practica musice, 1496) kept mode tied to chant, psalm tones and mnemonics. They also attached affective characters to modes (mode 3 harsh, mode 4 adulatory, mode 6 pious…).

  • Modern debate: whether modal categories actually governed polyphonic composition between 1475 and 1525.

Pitch systems: modes to keys · 1600–1720

Tonal organization in seventeenth-century music theory

Baroque
Cambridge History, ch. 13 (Gregory Barnett), pp. 407–455

The 17th century is often painted as confused, caught between modes and keys. Barnett shows a coherent practice made of solmization, transposition and key signatures, the organists' church keys, cadence systems, and the gradual emergence of a two-mode, 24-key system.

Key points

  • Tonal space rests on the gamut of letters (litterae) and hexachord syllables (voces): natural (C), hard (G) and soft (F) hexachords. Cantus durus has no flat; cantus mollis has one flat.
  • Attempts to replace solmization (Banchieri's extra syllable; bocedization) failed. Musicians kept the Guidonian practice and imagined transposed pieces as untransposed (Penna, 1672).
  • The modes were adapted to polyphony: authentic and plagal ranges were shared out among the voices, and fugue subjects and answers kept to the species of fifth and fourth.
  • Organists' "church keys" (tuoni ecclesiastici) based on psalm tones coexisted with the twelve modes. Cadence classifications (regular, irregular, peregrine) helped define a mode (Bononcini, 1673).
  • English theory (Campion 1618, Simpson, Playford) identified the "key" by its bass note and its third.
  • By about 1700 the system had settled into two modes, major and minor, transposable to all degrees (Masson 1697; Mattheson 1713 on 24 keys and their characters; Heinichen's circle, 1711/1728).

Counterpoint and composition · 850–1420

Organum – discantus – contrapunctus in the Middle Ages

Early Middle Ages Late Middle Ages
Cambridge History, ch. 15 (Sarah Fuller), pp. 477–502

Fuller follows medieval writing on music in two or more parts, in three stages. Organum (c. 850–1030) added a voice to chant by rule. Discantus (13th c.) is note-against-note polyphony in measured rhythm. Contrapunctus (14th c.) is a teachable system of consonances and progressions that leads directly to Renaissance counterpoint.

Key points

  • Organum (c. 850–1030): the Musica and Scolica enchiriadis describe singing a second voice to chant in parallel or at the fourth, with a converging cadence (occursus).
  • Guido's Micrologus (c. 1026–28) describes diaphony with oblique motion. Around 1100, treatises such as Ad organum faciendum and the Montpellier treatise show contrary motion and note-against-note writing.
  • Discantus (13th c.) belongs to the new measured music of Paris (musica mensurabilis), preserved in the great Notre Dame codices. Johannes de Garlandia (c. 1250) ranked the concords and discords.
  • Contrapunctus (14th c.): short manuals such as "Quilibet affectans", and later Prosdocimo (1412), turned two-voice writing into a teachable system of consonances and progressions.
  • Fuller's theme is the change from adding a voice to a chant to a combinatorial system that composers could learn. That system leads directly into Renaissance counterpoint.

Counterpoint and composition · 1430–1620

Counterpoint pedagogy in the Renaissance

Renaissance Baroque
Cambridge History, ch. 16 (Peter Schubert), pp. 503–533

For almost two centuries, Renaissance teachers explained the elaboration of a given line (soggetto) with remarkably consistent principles. They moved from note-against-note formulas to florid writing, imitation, double counterpoint and four-part sonority.

Key points

  • Sources fall into four groups: 15th-century writers (Ugolino, Tinctoris 1477, Ramis, Gaffurio); 16th-century Italians (Aron, Lusitano, Vicentino, Zarlino, Zacconi, Artusi, Cerone, Diruta); Spaniards (Sancta Maria, Montaños, Bermudo); Germans and English (Burmeister, Morley, Campion).
  • Contrapunctus simplex: note against note, consonances only. Pupils memorized lists of two-note progressions (e.g., from a sixth to an octave) for improvisation.
  • Modal considerations: Aron objects to a b♭ in the bass that would change its melodic species. Cadences must suit the mode.
  • Florid (diminutus) counterpoint: Tinctoris's eight general rules, including variety (varietas). Dissonances pass on weak parts of the beat or appear as prepared syncopations (suspensions).
  • Motives (inventioni, passaggi) and imitation (contrapunto fugato); improvised canon in Lusitano (1553).
  • Double counterpoint at the octave, tenth and twelfth (Zarlino; Chiodino 1610).
  • Sancta Maria (1565) teaches four-part writing as cycles of consonant sonorities (5/3, 6/3), a step toward chordal thinking.

Counterpoint and composition · 1558–1930

Steps to Parnassus: contrapuntal theory in 1725, precursors and successors

Renaissance Baroque Classical era Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 18 (Ian Bent), pp. 554–602

For 250 years Fux's Gradus ad Parnassum (1725) has been the reference point of counterpoint teaching. Bent shows what Fux inherited (the motion rules, the species), what he systematized, and how later writers rewrote him, from Martini and Albrechtsberger to Schenker and Jeppesen.

Key points

  • The Gradus is a Latin dialogue between Aloysius (Palestrina) and the student Josephus, a guided ascent like Virgil leading Dante. Mizler translated it into German (1742), and there was an Italian version (1761).
  • Fux sorts motion into direct, contrary and oblique, and maps it onto four rules about moving between perfect and imperfect consonances. Bent stresses that the substance of these rules goes back to Zarlino and earlier.
  • The species existed before Fux (Diruta, Banchieri's Cartella 1614, Zacconi, Bononcini 1673). Fux made them a graded method: 1 note against note; 2 two against one; 3 four against one; 4 syncopation (ligature); 5 florid.
  • Fux continues with three and four parts, imitation, fugue and double counterpoint, all in the modes.
  • Successors include Martini's Esemplare (1774–75), Albrechtsberger (1790; Beethoven's teacher), Cherubini, Bellermann, Schenker's Kontrapunkt (1910–22) and Jeppesen (1930–31). Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven all studied the Gradus.

Counterpoint and composition · 1920–1990

Twelve-tone theory

Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 19 (John Covach), pp. 603–627

Twelve-tone theory began among composers in the 1920s: Hauer's tropes and Schoenberg's method as introduced by Stein. It grew through the "modal" systems of the 1930s and 40s and, after 1945, became one of the founding subjects of American academic theory.

Key points

  • Josef Matthias Hauer argued for twelve-tone music on gnostic grounds and discovered the 44 tropes (1921). Herbert Eimert's Atonale Musiklehre (1924) is a practical account.
  • Erwin Stein's "Neue Formprinzipien" (1924) introduced Schoenberg's method. The Grundgestalt (basic shape) is the source of a work's material. Schoenberg himself wrote little until "Composition with Twelve Tones" (1941).
  • Rows appear in four forms (prime, inversion, retrograde, retrograde inversion) at 12 transpositions.
  • 1930–45: Hill (1936), Krenek (1940) and Perle (1941) proposed twelve-tone "modes" and functional relations.
  • After 1945: Leibowitz (1947), Rufer (1952), then Babbitt (combinatoriality, aggregates, invariants, time-points), Forte and Perle (Twelve-Tone Tonality, 1977). Covach asks whether twelve-tone theory has declined.

Performance theory

Theory usually takes the written score as its object. Cohen looks at the other tradition, theories of performance: improvised counterpoint, Mersenne's interviews with performers, thoroughbass, division and ornament manuals, partimento, and the 18th-century performance treatises.

Rhythm, meter and notation · 1200–1500

The evolution of rhythmic notation

Late Middle Ages Renaissance
Cambridge History, ch. 20 (Anna Maria Busse Berger), pp. 628–656

Rhythmic notation made Western polyphony possible. Busse Berger follows it from the contextual rhythmic modes of Notre Dame, through Franco's fixed note values and the Ars nova's mensurations, to the Italian divisions and the Renaissance tactus.

Key points

  • Modal notation (13th c.): Garlandia (c. 1250) gives six rhythmic modes, read from ligature patterns in context. Modes 1, 2 and 6 are the "correct" modes because they keep the long–breve proportion.
  • Franco of Cologne, Ars cantus mensurabilis (c. 1280): each note shape has its own value (duplex long, long, breve, semibreve). Perfection is three tempora, with rules of imperfection and alteration; ligatures carry propriety and perfection.
  • Ars nova (Vitry; de Muris' Notitia, 1321): the minim; modus, tempus and prolation, each perfect or imperfect; mensuration signs; red notation.
  • Italian notation (Marchetto's Pomerium, c. 1318): divisions of the breve (quaternaria, senaria, octonaria, duodenaria) and dots of division.
  • The mensural system is a chain of values, each a multiple or divisor of the next, like the Roman system of weights. Later came proportions (Tinctoris) and the tactus as a steady beat measuring time.

Theories of musical rhythm in the 18th and 19th centuries

As the mensural system gave way to bar lines, time signatures and accents, theorists had to explain the modern system of meter. Caplin traces the problem from Mattheson's sound-feet to Koch's phrase grammar and Riemann's upbeat-oriented system.

Key points

  • Before the 17th century rhythm followed the mensural system and the tactus. Change came gradually, and proportions gave way to time signatures.
  • Accent was tied to meter: good and bad notes (nota buona / cattiva), "struck" versus "passing" notes, linked to consonance and dissonance.
  • Mattheson (Der vollkommene Capellmeister, 1739) borrows poetic feet (Klangfüße) for rhythm, though he never relates them consistently to meter.
  • Kirnberger and Schulz (Die Kunst des reinen Satzes, 1771–79) discuss tempo giusto, kinds of meter, compound meters and phrase segments.
  • Koch (Versuch, 1782–93): phrase units (Einschnitt, Absatz, Periode), punctuation, extension and compression, the norm of four-bar phrases.
  • 19th century: Momigny (upbeat-to-downbeat groupings, 1803–06), Hauptmann (dialectic of 2 and 3, 1853), Lussy (expressive accents, 1874), Westphal (a revival of ancient Greek metrics), Riemann (Musikalische Dynamik und Agogik, 1884; System der musikalischen Rhythmik und Metrik, 1903: upbeat orientation and eight-bar norm).

Rhythm, meter and notation · 1910–today

Rhythm in twentieth-century theory

Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 22 (Justin London), pp. 695–725

Twentieth-century rhythmic theory split into several lines. One treats rhythm as motion and energy; one takes the Schenkerian view of rhythm emerging from voice leading; another uses the grouping-and-meter models of Cooper & Meyer and Lerdahl & Jackendoff. A further line comprises serial and composerly theories, alongside new cognitive work.

Key points

  • Motion and energy: Kurth; Neumann's Zeitgestalten; Hasty's Meter as Rhythm (1997), where meter is projection.
  • Schenker: the fundamental structure is arrhythmic, and rhythm arises in the middleground. Schachter's durational reduction and Rothstein's Phrase Rhythm in Tonal Music (1989) develop this.
  • Yeston (1976) treats meter as the interaction of rhythmic strata, and metrical dissonance as conflicting levels (developed by Krebs).
  • Cooper & Meyer (1960) analyse rhythmic groups on the model of poetic feet at architectonic levels. Lerdahl & Jackendoff (1983) separate grouping from meter, with well-formedness and preference rules.
  • Kramer, The Time of Music (1988): hypermeter and linear versus nonlinear time.
  • Composers' theories: Messiaen (added values, non-retrogradable rhythms), Stockhausen ("…how time passes…"), Babbitt's time-point system. Lewin's transformational approach to rhythm.

Harmony and tonality · 1600–1800

Rameau and eighteenth-century harmonic theory

Baroque Classical era
Cambridge History, ch. 24 (Joel Lester), pp. 753–777

Rameau joined separate strands into one theory of harmony: the triad as a unit, thoroughbass practice, and the directionality of cadences. Lester explains the fundamental bass, its successes and its problems, and the theory's reception.

Key points

  • Before Rameau: 5/3 and 6/3 sonorities as building blocks (Zarlino; Lippius's "trias harmonica", 1612), thoroughbass rules, rules of the octave, and directed cadential voice leading.
  • Traité de l'harmonie (1722). Book I derives all chords from divisions of a string; triads and seventh chords are generated by a fundamental and are invertible. Book II shows how chords move.
  • Fundamental bass: the succession of roots. Ideally it moves by fifths and thirds, the intervals generated by the string. A dissonance (the seventh) gives a chord its drive to resolve.
  • Cadences: perfect (V7–I), irregular (IV with added sixth to I), and evaded or broken.
  • Problem cases: "chords by supposition" (ninths, elevenths, with a bass below the fundamental); double emploi (the same chord read as IV with added sixth or as ii7); stepwise root motion.
  • Later works: Nouveau système (1726), Génération harmonique (1737: the corps sonore and the harmonic series), Démonstration (1750). Reception: d'Alembert's Élémens (1752), Marpurg, Sorge, Kirnberger (essential and inessential dissonances), Vogler, Tartini, Rousseau.

Harmony and tonality · 1776–1911

Nineteenth-century harmonic theory: the Austro-German legacy

Classical era Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 25 (David W. Bernstein), pp. 778–811

Rameau's lasting legacy was the idea that chords are generated on scale degrees of a key. Bernstein follows this idea through Vogler's Roman numerals and Weber's multiple meaning, Sechter's rigorous fundamental bass, Mayrberger's Tristan analysis, and the function and degree theories that led to Schenker and Schoenberg.

Key points

  • Vogler (from 1776): triads on every scale degree, Roman-numeral thinking, and multiple meaning (Mehrdeutigkeit) of chords.
  • Gottfried Weber (Versuch einer geordneten Theorie der Tonsetzkunst, 1817–21): upper- and lower-case Roman numerals, a table of key relationships, and multiple meaning used in analysis (e.g., the opening of Mozart's "Dissonance" Quartet).
  • Sechter (Grundsätze, 1853–54): fundamental-bass progressions by fifths and thirds; stepwise root motion explained by an implied intermediate root; a diatonic-chromatic system. He taught Bruckner.
  • Mayrberger (1878) analysed the Tristan Prelude with Sechterian fundamentals (the "Zwitterakkord").
  • Riemann's function theory (T, S, D) competes with degree (Stufen) theory: Schenker's Harmonielehre (1906), Schoenberg's Harmonielehre (1911).

Harmony and tonality · 1810–1960

Tonality

Classical era Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 23 (Brian Hyer), pp. 726–752

"Tonality" is a 19th-century word with many meanings. Hyer traces it from Choron and Fétis, through function theories and tonal grids, to Schenker and Schoenberg, and shows how its images of presence and centre shape what theorists hear.

Key points

  • Choron first used the term in 1810, for the arrangement of dominant and subdominant around a tonic, and contrasted tonalité moderne with ancient tonality.
  • Fétis popularized it in the 1830s–40s (his Traité of 1844). He said the idea came to him in the Bois de Boulogne in 1831. Tonality is the sum of necessary relations among the notes of the scale, its attractions are "metaphysical", and the dominant seventh in Monteverdi's Stracciami pur il core (1592) marks the birth of the modern kind.
  • Hyer lists many senses of the word: pitch-centredness in general; tonal versus modal or atonal; the European practice of about 1680–1910; a synonym for "key"; and others.
  • Tonal theories picture the tonic as presence and plenitude, and lay relations out on mental grids (Euler, Weber's key table, Oettingen and Riemann).
  • Schenker: tonality is the composing-out of the tonic triad, including mixture. Schoenberg: monotonality and a chart of regions.

Harmony and tonality · 1850–1920

Dualist tonal space and transformation in 19th-century thought

Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 14 (Henry Klumpenhouwer), pp. 456–476

Harmonic dualism treats the minor triad as the exact mirror (inversion) of the major. Klumpenhouwer follows the idea from Hauptmann's dialectic through Oettingen's tables to Riemann's Klang transformations, Schritte and Wechsel, which anticipate today's neo-Riemannian theory.

Key points

  • In 19th-century German theory two traditions coexisted: thoroughbass-based theory and harmonic dualism.
  • Hauptmann (Die Natur der Harmonik und der Metrik, 1853) explained the triad dialectically: the octave is unity, the fifth duality, the third union. The minor triad is the major triad "reversed".
  • Helmholtz denied that undertones exist physically. Oettingen (1866) nonetheless set major "tonicity" against minor "phonicity" and drew fifth/third tables.
  • Riemann: every triad is a Klang. Major is an Oberklang generated upward from its root; minor is an Unterklang generated downward from its top note.
  • Riemann's transformations: Schritte (steps, e.g., Quintschritt) and Wechsel (changes of mode, e.g., Terzwechsel, Leittonwechsel). Function labels T, S, D came in the later texts (1893).
  • Modern neo-Riemannian theory takes over his P, L and R transformations without the dualist ontology.

Harmony and tonality · 1900–1990

Heinrich Schenker

Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 26 (William Drabkin), pp. 812–846

Schenker's theory can be summed up in one word, hierarchy. Drabkin explains four concepts (Stufe, composing-out, fundamental structure and structural levels) and the transformations that link the levels. He also covers Schenker's work as editor and his ideology, and the theory's American reception.

Key points

  • Main works: Harmonielehre (1906), Kontrapunkt I–II (1910, 1922), Der Tonwille (1921–24), Das Meisterwerk in der Musik (1925–30), Fünf Urlinie-Tafeln (1932), Der freie Satz (1935).
  • Stufe: a harmonic degree governing a span, not just a chord. The Harmonielehre analyses Bach's "Buß und Reu".
  • Auskomponierung (composing-out): the tonic triad, the "chord of nature", is unfolded in time.
  • Ursatz: the fundamental line (Urlinie) descends from ③, ⑤ or ⑧ to ①, over the bass arpeggiation I–V–I. It is the background.
  • Levels: background, middleground (several layers), foreground. Transformations include linear progressions (Züge), unfolding, reaching-over, interruption (③–② ‖ ③–②–①), mixture, neighbour notes and concealed repetition.
  • The Ursatz is arrhythmic. Schenker's organicism and cult of genius sit alongside nationalist views. The American reception (Jonas, Salzer 1952, Forte and others) turned the theory into an analytical method.

Analysis and form · 1537–1800

Music and rhetoric

Renaissance Baroque Classical era
Cambridge History, ch. 27 (Patrick McCreless), pp. 847–879

Rhetoric was the West's original language for talking about discourse, and from the 16th century to about 1800 it shaped how music was taught and described. McCreless follows the musica poetica tradition, Burmeister's figures, Mattheson's plan for an oration, the decline of rhetoric around 1800, and its 20th-century revival.

Key points

  • Classical rhetoric (Sicily c. 485 BCE; Aristotle; the Rhetorica ad Herennium; Cicero; Quintilian) has five canons: inventio, dispositio, elocutio, memoria and pronuntiatio. Elocutio includes the figures.
  • Listenius's Musica (1537) adds musica poetica, the making of a finished, lasting work, to theorica and practica.
  • Burmeister (1599, 1601, 1606) took figures from rhetorical elocutio (e.g., noema, anaploce, fuga realis, palilogia, climax) and analysed Lasso's In me transierunt as an oration with exordium, body and ending.
  • Bernhard (c. 1660) explained figures as licensed dissonance treatment (passus duriusculus, superjectio, anticipatio…). The tradition continues with Walther, Mattheson (Der vollkommene Capellmeister, 1739: exordium, narratio, propositio, confirmatio, confutatio, peroratio) and Forkel.
  • Rhetoric declined around 1800. It was revived in 20th-century historiography (Schering, Unger) and critiqued (Vickers). Its heirs include topic theory (Ratner 1980) and theories of expressive meaning.

Analysis and form · 1790–today

Form

Classical era Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 28 (Scott Burnham), pp. 880–906

The Formenlehre tradition centres on sonata form. Burnham traces it from Koch's description of an expanded binary plan, through Reicha's thematic diagrams and Marx's organic system of forms, to 20th-century theories of formal function and the Schenkerian view of form.

Key points

  • Koch (Versuch vol. 3, 1793) describes the first movement as an expanded binary form built from punctuated phrases. Galeazzi (1796) names its melodic parts.
  • Reicha (Traité de haute composition, 1824–26) gives the grande coupe binaire, analyses the Figaro overture, and treats "développement" as the working-out of ideas.
  • Marx (Lehre von der musikalischen Komposition, 1837–47) derives all forms from the opposition of the closed Satz and the open Gang: Lied forms, five rondo forms, and sonata form with Hauptsatz, Seitensatz and Schlusssatz. Czerny's School of Practical Composition (c. 1848) spread the model.
  • 20th century: Schoenberg (sentence and period), Ratz, Tovey, Rosen, Caplin's formal functions, Hepokoski & Darcy's sonata theory, Lorenz on Wagner. Schenker derives form from the voice-leading structure.

Thematic and motivic analysis

Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 29 (Jonathan Dunsby), pp. 907–926

Western music is inherently melodic, and much analysis tracks themes and motives. Dunsby surveys Schoenberg's developing variation, Réti's thematic process, Keller's functional analysis, Schenker's motivic parallelisms, and semiotic paradigmatic analysis.

Key points

  • "Theme" was in use by the 17th century and "motive" became common later. Themes and motives are the material of developing variation.
  • Schoenberg: Grundgestalt and developing variation ("Brahms the Progressive").
  • Réti, The Thematic Process in Music (1951): one thematic kernel unifies a whole work (e.g., Beethoven's Op. 135).
  • Hans Keller's functional analysis: wordless analytical scores that show unity behind contrast.
  • Semiotics: Ruwet's paradigmatic analysis of a medieval flagellant song (1972), Nattiez's tripartition. Signs can be described synchronically or diachronically, and paradigmatically or syntagmatically.

Perception and energy · 1850–1960

Energetics

Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 30 (Lee Rothfarb), pp. 927–955

Energetics treats music as motion, force and tension. Rothfarb traces dynamic metaphors from Aristoxenus' dynameis and medieval intervals that "seek" resolution, through Marx and Fétis, to Halm and Kurth around 1900, and on to later heirs.

Key points

  • Musical motion is intuitively obvious but hard to define: pitches are discrete plateaus, yet we hear movement.
  • Ancient and medieval roots: Aristoxenus' "functions" (dynameis) of notes; 14th- and 15th-century intervals that "desire" a following interval.
  • 19th century: Marx (energy of the Hauptsatz), Fétis and Vivier (tendencies and attractions), Lipps, Riemann.
  • August Halm (Von zwei Kulturen der Musik, 1913): fugue and sonata as two cultures. Music has a will of its own, and form is the obligation to follow it.
  • Ernst Kurth: Grundlagen des linearen Kontrapunkts (1917), Bach's melody as kinetic energy; Romantische Harmonik (1920), the Tristan crisis; Bruckner (1925); Musikpsychologie (1931). Also Mersmann's graphs of dynamic form.

Perception and energy · 1850–today

The psychology of music

Nineteenth century Twentieth century
Cambridge History, ch. 31 (Robert Gjerdingen), pp. 956–981

Music psychology asks how the mind perceives, imagines, performs and evaluates music. Gjerdingen traces it from philosophical roots through Wundt's laboratory and the Stumpf controversy, Gestalt and American functionalism (Seashore), to Meyer, Narmour and the cognitive revolution.

Key points

  • Roots in philosophy: Locke, Hume and associationism; Comte's positivism. Basevi's 1862 harmony treatise rests on psychological premises.
  • Wundt founded a laboratory (Leipzig, 1879). His student Lorenz's interval experiments sparked a bitter controversy with Stumpf (1890–91).
  • Gestalt psychology (Wertheimer, Köhler, Koffka) supplied grouping laws later used by Meyer.
  • American functionalism: Seashore's measures of musical talent (1919) and Psychology of Music (1938).
  • Meyer, Emotion and Meaning in Music (1956): expectation and Gestalt laws such as good continuation. Narmour's implication–realization model (1990, 1992).
  • Cognitive science: Krumhansl's tonal hierarchies, Bregman's auditory scene analysis, Lerdahl & Jackendoff, and schema-based approaches.
