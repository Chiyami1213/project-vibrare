# Quantz, On Playing the Flute (1752; trans. Reilly 1966) — notes (page refs = Reilly pages)
## Ch. VII Of Taking Breath (pp. 87–90)
- Breath at the proper time essential for wind players and singers; separating notes that belong together = like breathing in the middle of a word or before the sense is clear. (87)
- Because the quickest notes are played a little unequally (XI §12), breathe between a LONG and a SHORT note; never after a short note, much less after the last note of a bar. Exception: fast stepwise triplets (may breathe after the last note of the bar); with a leap of a third breathe inside the leap. (87)
- Upbeat beginnings / after a cadence when a new idea begins: breathe before the restatement or the new idea, separating ideas. Before a held note of one or more bars you may breathe even after a short note. Tied notes (suspension figures): split a tied quaver into two semiquavers on the same pitch and breathe between them. (88)
- Long passagework: inhale slowly a full supply (enlarge throat, expand chest), spend economically; if forced to breathe in quick notes: make the preceding note very short, inhale quickly, rush the next two or three notes a little so the beat is not retarded and no note is omitted; breathe in good time rather than at the last moment. Breathe only when necessary. (88–89)
- Best place within a bar of semiquavers: after the first note of the first crotchet (unless first four move by step and later ones leap: wide intervals are best for breath). (89)
- You can play twice as much in one breath alone as before listeners (nerves). Learn what makes "musical sense" (a phrase): don't separate what belongs together nor link several phrases — "a great part of true expression depends upon this". String players have the advantage, but must not join everything "in the fashion of a hurdy-gurdy". (90)
## Ch. V §20–23 Dotted notes (pp. 66–69)
- Dotted quavers and shorter: the note after the dot is played as short as the shortest in the piece, whatever the tempo, so the dot is lengthened (over-dotting). Dotted minims/crotchets keep their written value.
- Dotted notes against triplets: the short note comes AFTER the third triplet note, not with it (CPE disagrees: assimilate).
- Lombard (short–long): first note as short as possible; the shorter, the bolder. Long dots held longer → more flattering.
## Ch. VI Tonguing (pp. 71–85)
- Tongue = bow of the flute. Syllables: ti/di (single), tiri (dotted & moderately quick), did'll (double, quickest).
- ti = short, lively, quick, equal notes; leaps in Allegro; notes with strokes; dotted notes even in Adagio. di = slow, sustained, pleasing; stepwise; always in Adagio (except dots). Many shades between.
- Air released by the withdrawal of the tongue tip, not by the stroke itself. Tongue position moves forward as pitch rises.
- Separate slightly before an appoggiatura. Slur: only first tongued (di). Repeated notes under slur: chest (huffed). Quick passagework single tongue makes notes too equal → use tiri / did'll.
- tiri: accent on ri (long) for the downbeat note, ti (short) on upbeat; start with ti ti. Faster passages: slur pairs, ti ri ti ti.
- did'll: accent on the first syllable, on the good note; practise on one pitch; don't let tongue anticipate fingers.
## Ch. VIII Appoggiaturas & small graces (pp. 91–100)
- Appoggiaturas add dissonance (4ths, 7ths over 3rds/6ths) to rouse the ear; small notes take value from the following note.
- Taken from the side of the previous note: from above if the previous note is higher, from below if lower.
- Tipped with the tongue, swelled if time permits, resolution slurred more softly (Abzug).
- Passing appoggiaturas (falling thirds, French coulé) tipped BEFORE the beat in the time of the previous note — CPE rejects.
- Accented: half of a plain note; 2/3 of a dotted note; in 6/8 tied dotted notes = the whole first dotted note; before a note followed by a rest: appoggiatura takes the note's time, note takes the rest's.
- Before trills on dissonances (aug4, dim5, 7th, 2nd): very short so as not to turn the dissonance into a consonance.
- Add unmarked ones where a long consonant note follows short notes. Half-shakes, pincés, doublés, battemens = French brilliants; plain appoggiaturas = tenderness; use sparingly, like seasoning.
## Ch. IX Shakes (pp. 101–108)
- Speed by room (resonant hall slower) and character (sad slower); not bleating; not trill in thirds; even and moderate.
- Long cadential shake ≈ 4 finger movements (8 notes) per pulse (pulse ≈ 80/min); quick pieces a bit faster; lower registers slower.
- Every shake begins with its appoggiatura (above or below) and ends with a two-note termination at the same speed; implied when only a plain tr is written.
- Appoggiatura tongued; shake and termination slurred to it. Semitone shakes on flute: moderate breath, finger low.
- At a cadence: no appoggiatura between the termination and the closing note (CPE allows it).
## Ch. XI Good execution (pp. 119–128)
- Orator analogy. Execution must be true & distinct, rounded & complete, easy & flowing, varied (light and shade), expressive.
- Separate musical ideas even without rest, esp. when phrase ends and next starts on the same pitch.
- §12 Inequality: in moderate/slow pieces, quickest notes ("good" 1st,3rd,5th,7th) held slightly longer, less than dotted. Exceptions: very fast passagework, vocal passagework, strokes/dots, repeated notes, slurs over >2 notes, gigue quavers.
- Affect clues: mode; intervals & articulation; dissonances (XVII/vi §13–17); tempo word. Adopt different sentiment almost every bar.
- Ornaments sparing; instrumentalist sings like a singer. List of faults of poor execution.
## Ch. XII Allegro (pp. 129–135)
- Tempo set by the hardest passage; keep the beat at each crotchet; stress/hold first of quick figures to avoid rushing; fingers move with the tongue's withdrawal.
- Triplets round, first slightly held; after a short rest on the beat wait slightly longer; expression over speed (machine analogy).
- Gaiety: short notes, lively tonguing; majesty: long notes, sharp dotted figures; boldness: Lombard; flattery: slurred steps, syncopation (second half reinforced).
- Allegro assai with semiquavers: quavers tipped short, crotchets singing. Few free variations in Allegro, only on repeats.
## Ch. XIV Adagio (pp. 162–178)
- French manner (essential graces only) vs Italian (free divisions; needs harmony). Learn French first.
- A true Adagio = a flattering petition. Messa di voce on held notes: tip gently, pp→swell to middle→diminish, finger flattement; keep pitch (turn flute in/out). Rubato examples; note-by-note dynamics (§§25–43). Breath at rests: hold last note slightly longer.
## Ch. XV Cadenzas (pp. 179–195)
- On the penultimate bass note (dominant) at the fermata; short and surprising like a bon mot; from the principal sentiment, can reuse the piece's best phrase; no strict metre; wind/voice: one breath; short cadenza stays in key; longer → subdominant; longer still → subdominant and dominant; avoid remote keys; resolve dissonances. Two-part cadenzas: imitation and dissonance rules, may be longer (breath allowed).
## Ch. XVII Sec. VI §12–17 Keyboard accompanist (pp. 252–259)
- Dissonances louder than consonances; three classes: mf (2/4, 5/6, 6/3 major? 7 minor with minor 3, major 7), f (2/♯4, ♭5/6 minor), ff (♯2/♯4, 3/♯4, ♭5/♮6, aug6, dim7, 7/2/4); consonant Adagio chords mezzo piano; scale classes down in p/pp.
- Harpsichord means: fewer parts (p), bass in octaves (mf), add chord notes in LH (f), quick arpeggiation + full + forceful touch (ff); second manual for pp; piano best; clavichord lacks ff.
## Ch. XVII Sec. VII §45–59 Tempo (pp. 282–289)
- Pulse ~80/min. Four classes: Allegro assai (minim=pulse), Allegretto (crotchet), Adagio cantabile (quaver), Adagio assai (two pulses per quaver); alla breve all values twice as fast. Plain "Allegro"/Vivace/Poco allegro = pulse per 3 quavers (≈ crotchet 120). 2/4 or quick 6/8 Allegro: pulse per bar; 12/8 Allegro: two per bar; 3/4 Allegro with semiquavers: 3 pulses per 2 bars; fast 3/4 or 3/8 with 6 notes/bar: pulse per bar; Adagio cantabile 3/4 quaver bass: pulse per quaver; arioso crotchets: pulse per crotchet; Siciliana 12/8 pulse on 1st & 3rd quaver (quaver ≈ 120); all-triplet fast pieces may be a bit faster. Arias and church music somewhat slower. Not absolute: CPE says Berlin adagios slower, allegros faster.
