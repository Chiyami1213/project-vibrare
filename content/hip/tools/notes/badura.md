# Badura-Skoda, Interpreting Bach at the Keyboard (1993) — notes (page refs = book pages)
## Ch. 2 Studies in Rhythm (pp. 15–70)
- Baroque "uniform rhythm": movements built on rhythmically uniform motifs → even flow; metre observed more strictly than in Classical music. (15–16)
- Contemporaries: maintaining strict time is a chief quality (Muffat 1698: tempo "in constant uniformity"; Quantz: after short rests better to begin later and rush the following notes a little than anticipate; Bach's conducting "very precise… tempo usually very fast, extremely steady"; Schütz: beat = soul of music; also Walther, L. Mozart). (16)
- Few notated tempo changes; little liking for ritardandos except toccatas, fantasias, recitatives; final ritardandos much less than today (organ-barrels corroborate). (17)
- BUT regular beat ≠ motoric: between the "pillars" of the beats freedom — rubato, breathing pauses, breaks, dovetailing. "Even the strongest lungs have to be able to breathe somewhere"; breathing pauses most noticeable in wind solo works (flute, oboe). (17)
- Performer's means: greater/lesser accentuation, tension/relaxation, breathing and articulation. (19)
- Scan the metre: triple time — first beat far stronger (dances); 4/4 — beats 1 & 3 strong (3 less), 2 & 4 weak (schlecht); CPE p. 156. Apply to subdivisions (heavy–light). Declamation like verse (dactyl, anapaest; Mattheson, Walther, Huber); Walther's "Meine Seele ruft und schreyet" — 1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th notes long (stressed), others short; on harpsichord unstressed notes can only be made shorter (agogic/articulation) since finger pressure gives no dynamics. Kirnberger: whole bars alternate strong/weak (hypermetre). First task: make the metre audible by articulation/weighting, especially at the beginning. (19–21)
- Rubato (Ch. 2 §"Rubato, syncopation, notes inégales", pp. 63–69): rubato with strict beat and even lower voices (Partita 6 Allemande); short notes before dotted ones accelerated (Quantz p. 226; CPE p. 158); syncopations should sound as displaced downbeats, not glued to the beat (Partita 6 Corrente); Mozart: "the left hand does not know what the right hand is doing" (letter 1777); CPE's rubato quoted; Italian Concerto Andante = written-out Italian rubato — harpsichordist plays a note "a shade early" to sustain it longer (cantabile on harpsichord via timing); tirades in overtures delayed then accelerated to arrive on time; French overture dotted rhythms exaggerated, short notes played together as very short (Quantz p. 290: notes after dots short and sharp; three+ demisemiquavers at the extreme end) — but questioned for Handel.
- Notes inégales: avoid wholesale application to Bach (stammering); Birnbaum praised Bach's evenness; but a "mini-rubato": Quantz (p. 123) in moderate/adagio, strong notes (1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th) slightly longer and stronger; exceptions: very fast passages, coloratura, repeated notes, staccato, slurs over more than two notes, gigue quavers. Inegalité exception even in French-influenced music. (68–69)
- Concluding remarks: steady beat (image of heartbeat/walking) vs free melody; polyphony unified by the beat (flock of birds); duple from heartbeat/walking. (69–70)
## Ch. 3 Tempo (pp. 71–91)
- Dance pieces: find the kind of step (also some fugues are gigue-like: WTC II C♯ minor, G♯ minor; chorale preludes in dance style). Slow movements moulded "in one breath", not too slow. 18th-c. tempos generally faster and lighter than today; local traditions; allemande marks ranged from very slow to presto; Bach's pupils' tradition: his allemandes slow, gavottes fast. Handel's act timings (Judas 105 min vs a modern 151). Forkel: Bach usually very lively tempo, with variety "as eloquent as speech". (73–74)
- Quantz's pulse system (80 beats/min): common time Allegro assai = minim per pulse; Allegretto = crotchet; Adagio cantabile = quaver; Adagio assai = two pulses per quaver; alla breve one level larger. Four classes (Allegro assai incl. Allegro di molto/Presto; Allegretto incl. Allegro ma non tanto, moderato…; Adagio cantabile incl. Arioso, Larghetto, Soave, Dolce, Poco andante, Affettuoso, Pomposo…; Adagio assai incl. Adagio pesante, Lento, Largo assai, Mesto, Grave). (75–76; Quantz pp. 282–288)
- Bach rarely marked tempo; deduce from time signature, rhythmic patterns, note values, text, movement titles (Kirnberger/Schulz on metre and tempo giusto). Different tempo words across versions (BWV 1039 Presto vs 1027 Allegro moderato). "con discrezione" = free rhythm (Mattheson). (79–81)
## Ch. 4 Articulation (pp. 92–129)
- Most serious fault in modern Bach: non-existent or incorrect articulation — like wrong punctuation (Türk's comma example); all-staccato or all-legato = no punctuation; CPE "glue / red-hot". Gould's staccato C minor fugue WTC II wrong: vocal subject, small intervals, quiet sustained character → legato. (92–93)
- Bach rarely marked keyboard articulation (trusted player's education); teachers should mark articulation and BREATHING in every voice, like bowings in a string quartet (string players tend to over-legato and too few breathing pauses). Use Bach's marked works and vocal text underlay as models; his slurs are often too short/arbitrarily placed. (93–94)
- Walking basses (B minor Prelude WTC I andante; Partita 2 Sinfonia; Partita 6 Corrente): slightly detached, like a cellist/gambist — expressive non-legato not too short (≈ tenuto semiquaver + rest); faster tempo → shorter. Andante = flowing ("walking"), crotchet ≈ pulse 80. CPE's "half value" rule is an extreme; French organists only slight shortening. Modern over-shortening "ugly and choppy", historically untenable; CPE also demands exact values, singing, broad slurs in adagios. Staccato "in general" for brisk movements and leaps; Bach's staccato less sharp than Mozart's (harpsichord resonance; old bow played closer to the string — Melkus). (97–99)
- Figura corta (short-short-long / long-short-short): legato in an ingratiating adagio; non-legato/staccato with energy in faster tempi (Brandenburg 2 trumpet motifs detached). (99)
## Ch. 5 Dynamics (pp. 130–143)
- Bach's own dynamic marks for two-manual harpsichord: Italian Concerto, French Overture, Goldberg (manual indications) — model for unmarked works. Italian Concerto: tutti/solo by forte/piano manuals; solo cantilena (2nd mvt) with one or two 8' stops, accompaniment quietly on second manual; pianists: full expressive tone. French Overture: fugal sections forte, episodes piano (second manual); trio sections (Gavotte II) softer. Contrasting themes (Partitas 4, 6; English Suite 6) should not be "rattled off" at one level. Same passage need not always have the same registration. (136–138)
- Forkel: Bach's playing "as varied as a conversation"; his unusual organ registrations. (136)
## Ch. 6 Harpsichord or piano? (pp. 170–176)
- Not either–or but both; historically correct sonority usually sounds more beautiful and meaningful; copies of historical instruments have an advantage; but banning the piano illogical (why not modern strings, women's voices?). Many available harpsichords unsuitable for Bach (French/Flemish/Italian). (170–172)
- Modern piano for Bach: advantages (dynamic range, cantabile treble, precise action, fast repetitions); weaknesses (grey thick tone, opaque bass, heavy action, poor blend, unsuitable for continuo). Acceptable for most keyboard works if dynamic extremes avoided; no "Busoni basses". Pedal: discreet use reproduces harpsichord's natural resonance (dampers cut tone dryly); no long pedals; pedal = piano's substitute for stops/vibrato; musette movements imitate drones. Don't "play the harpsichord" on the piano or vice versa. Geminiani 1751: violin vibrato often. (174–176)
## Ch. 7 Legato and cantabile (pp. 186–187)
- Three rules of legato: linking notes, correct pressure, dynamic shaping. Rameau: release one finger at the moment the next strikes; legatissimo overlaps. Lift fingers quickly. "Articulated legato" (Troeger): release just before next note — string resonance continues. Touch = pressure (CPE); first note of a legato phrase accented by gentle pressure; Bebung/rocking on clavichord; shaping lines by tension and strong/weak notes most important. (186–187)
