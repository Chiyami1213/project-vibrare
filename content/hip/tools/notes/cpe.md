# CPE Bach Essay (Mitchell 1949) notes — page refs = Mitchell edition pages (running heads)
## Ch. 3 Performance (pp. 147–166)
- Mere technicians "overwhelm our hearing without satisfying it"; speed reserved for passages needing it, without advancing overall tempo. Some countries: adagios too fast, allegros too slow. (147–148)
- §2 Good performance = making the ear conscious of the true content & affect. §3 subject matter: loudness/softness, touch, snap, legato/staccato, vibrato (Bebung), arpeggiation, holding, retard & accelerando. §4 correct time, fitting volume, touch related to content → rounded, pure, flowing manner; test instrument in advance; portray rage etc. by harmonic/melodic devices not heavy attack. (148–149)
- §5 In general allegros = detached notes; adagios = broad slurred notes ("in general" — all kinds of execution in any tempo). (149)
- §6 Not sticky (holding too long) nor leaving keys "as if they burned" — middle best. (149)
- §7 Keyboard can't sustain/shade; adagio singing w/o empty space or too many fast notes; broken chords, trills, mordents as sustaining devices; "Play from the soul, not like a trained bird!" (150)
- §8 Listen to soloists and ensembles to learn the manner (slurred/detached) when not indicated; ornament volume/time value by affect; rests & notes exact except fermatas/cadences; purposeful violations of the beat: in solo/small ensemble may affect tempo; in large ensembles only within the bar. (150–151)
- §9 Master passagework; simple notes harder; don't attempt in public what you can't control. (151)
- §10 Tempo from general content + fastest notes/passages → allegro not rushed, adagio not dragged. (151)
- §12 Hear artistic singing; think in terms of song; sing instrumental melodies to understand performance. (151–152)
- §13 "A musician cannot move others unless he too is moved"; feel affects; avoid dragging from excess melancholy; fitting gestures, no grimaces. (152)
- §15 Free fantasia: unbarred, declamatory, change affects freely. (153)
- §17 Detached notes (strokes/dots): held a little less than half their notated length; mostly in leaps and rapid tempos. (154)
- §18 Legato under slur held full length; groups of 2 and 4 slurred notes: slight increase of pressure on 1st and 3rd; groups of 3: first; otherwise only first; passing notes/appoggiaturas against bass played legato even without slur; mostly stepwise & slower tempos. (154–155)
- §19 Portato (Tragen der Töne): slurred + dotted — legato each tone noticeably accented (clavichord). §20 Bebung (vibrato) on long affettuoso note: finger gently shaken, best after half the note's value (clavichord only). (156)
- §21 First of each slur slightly accented; slur + last note detached. (157)
- §22 Notes neither detached, slurred nor held → sounded for HALF their value (unless Ten.); quarters & eighths in moderate/slow tempos usually semidetached, with fire and slight accent. (157)
- §23 Short notes after dots always shorter than written; dots after long notes or in slow tempos held; prolonged successions of dots in rapid tempos may be performed as rests; ≥4 short notes after a dot played rapidly; short notes before dotted ones (Lombard) faster than written; exceptions: ornaments over them, sad/slow passages. (157–158)
- §25 Long tied note may be re-struck; voice-leading ties etc. (159). §26 arpeggiation signs. (159)
- §27 Triplets vs duple: dotted figures & duplets against triplets assimilated (Fig. 177) [Mitchell note: J. S. Bach E minor Partita Gavotte, WTC II E minor fugue]. (160)
- §28 Affective lengthening of notes/rests; retard fits slow/moderate tempos; avoid frequent retards in affettuoso; end a piece in the same tempo as begun (rare!); minor repetitions of major passages may broaden; broaden slightly into a languid fermata. TEMPO RUBATO: more or fewer notes than the bar's division; one hand against the bar, the other strictly with it; end of rubato all parts together over the bass; best in slow, caressing, sad melodies, dissonant chords; can't be learnt by practice without sensitivity; solo keyboardist may alter bass but not harmony. (160–162)
- §29 Dynamics: p, pp, mf, f, ff; firm grip but don't flog; no rule for forte/piano — exceptions everywhere; repetitions & sequences may be f then p; IN GENERAL dissonances loud, consonances soft (dissonances rouse, consonances quiet); exceptional turn → loud; deceptive progressions brought out; tones outside the key emphasized, those within the key piano; avoid constant changes; on 2-manual harpsichord don't change manuals for detailed shadings, only for whole passages; clavichord expresses all shades. Loud accompaniment balanced by stronger melody touch. Mitchell: Quantz's theory of dynamic level by dissonance type (mf, f, ff) accepted only broadly. (162–164)
- §30 Cadenzas (elaborated cadences) like improvisations, unmeasured; pauses between voice statements like conversation. (164–165)
- §31 Varied reprises: not everything varied; affettuoso/declamatory passages hard to vary; variants must suit the affect and be as good or better than the original; keep the contrasts and the lineaments by which the affect is recognized; bass may be varied keeping harmony. (165–166)
## Introduction to Part One (pp. 30–40)
- Three inseparable factors: correct fingering, good embellishments, good performance. Bad playing = hacking, thumping, stumbling; "All other instruments have learned how to sing" — keyboard must too. (30)
- French keyboard pieces = good schooling (flowing correct style, embellishments indicated, left hand used, held notes). (31)
- Left hand should be trained equal to right; arias for taste, not dexterity; drum basses harmful. (31)
- Keyboard's asset: keeping time, exact small fractions (syncopations, short rests, dotted rhythms). Quantz advises delaying entry after short rests (Ch. 12 §12). (32)
- Quick repeated bass notes in continuo with another bass instrument: omit 1, 3 or 5 notes, strike the others in octaves with heavy sustained attack — counter to Quantz XVII/iv §32 who wants every note (Mitchell: Quantz's jab at Bach). Keyboard = "guardian of the beat"; repeat sustained chords to mark meter; keyboard in centre of ensemble, first violinist near it; makes minor tempo changes possible. (33–35)
- Instruments: harpsichord (ensembles), clavichord (solo), pianoforte (if well built; fine alone & small ensembles; touch must be worked out); clavichord best for evaluating a player; has Bebung & Tragen. Good clavichord C–e''' range, resilient strings; good harpsichord uniform quilling, not too light action. (35–37)
- Temperament: take a barely noticeable amount from most fifths → all 24 keys usable; keyboard so tuned the purest in practice. (37)
- Own both harpsichord & clavichord; clavichord for good performance, harpsichord for finger strength; clavichordist plays harpsichord with varied touch; harpsichord-only players play in one color. (37–38)
- Method: fingering examples slow→fast; embellishments chapter (lifetime study); Lessons first without ornaments, then with; study voice & listen to singers; play memorized pieces in the dark. (38–39)
- Reichardt on CPE: sustained a note six eighths long with all degrees of loudness on Silbermann clavichord; Burney on Bebung "a cry of sorrow". (36, 156)
## Ch. 2 Embellishments — General (pp. 79–86)
- Embellishments indispensable: connect & enliven tones, give stress/accent, heighten expression; improve mediocre music; but prodigal use = spices that ruin a dish; notes sufficiently brilliant themselves stay plain (orator stressing every word). (79–81)
- Composers should specify ornaments; French notate precisely; Berlin school uses few, select ornaments (Marpurg). Mitchell: shift c.1750 from performer-added to composer-specified; CPE asks to play a Trio as written. (79–81)
- Two groups: signed/small-note ornaments vs unsigned free ornaments (many short notes) — latter governed by taste, usually written out on keyboard. Sad/simple affects take fewer ornaments. (80–81)
- Vary ornaments on repetition (caressing then brilliant, or plain). Need thorough-bass knowledge to ornament well. (82)
- Ornament tones follow key signature + modulations. Ornaments proportioned to the main note's length, tempo, affect: more tones → longer note; no hurried blurring. Last tone held until next note (purpose = connect). Better in slow/moderate tempos and long notes; at half/full closes, caesurae, fermatas. (83–84)
- §23–24 ALL ornaments in small notes belong to the FOLLOWING note: the preceding note is never shortened; small notes struck WITH the bass, glide into the main note. (84–85)
- §25 Best style combines correctness and brilliance of French ornaments with the suavity of Italian singing; Germans can unite them. J. S. Bach (via Marpurg): every country's music has good and bad. (85)
- Practise ornaments with both hands; imitations must be exact. (86)
## The Appoggiatura (pp. 87–99)
- Essential; joins notes, fills sound, origin of syncopations and dissonances. Variable (long) vs invariable short. Now notated in real length (older: all as eighths) (Mitchell: rules refer to a dying practice, not always applicable to J. S. Bach). (87)
- Appoggiatura LOUDER than its resolution; joined to it (with or without slur); release = light, undecorated following tone. (88)
- Variable appoggiatura: duple time on down- or upbeat; triple only on downbeat; before relatively long notes, cadential trills, half cadences, caesurae, fermatas, final tones; ascending appoggiatura after a trill better than descending; slow dotted notes. (89)
- DURATION RULE: takes HALF of a duple note, TWO-THIRDS of a triple (dotted) note; exceptions written as large notes. Shortest never quicker than an eighth in allegretto. (90)
- Short (invariable) appoggiatura: before quick notes, repeated notes, caesurae before rapid note, syncopated/tied/slurred passages; filling thirds played quickly (in adagio as first of a triplet); before triplets quick; forming an octave with the bass quick; diminished octave prolonged; substituted for cadential trill short. (91–92)
- Lengthen for affect; shorten when the accompaniment would make fifths/harsh 7ths; never corrupt voice leading. Many appoggiaturas good in affettuoso (releases fade pianissimo). (94–96)
- Unaccented "Nachschlag" (passing appoggiatura before the beat) = "repulsive", remedy: shift it to the next accent — CPE vs Quantz disagree (Quantz's durchgehende Vorschlag / French "couler les tierces"; Quantz puts the first of two appoggiaturas before the beat). (97–98)
## The Trill (pp. 99–112)
- Trills enliven melodies; earlier mainly after an appoggiatura (enclosed trill) or on repeated tone; now in steps, leaps, beginning, successions, cadences, held tones, fermatas, caesurae. Use circumspectly in affettuoso. (99–100)
- Four trills: normal (m sign), ascending (from below), descending (from above), half/short (Pralltriller). (100)
- Normal trill ALWAYS begins on the upper note (Mitchell: no form of CPE trill starts on the main note). Suffix (2 notes from below) makes it faster; written or by sign. (100–101)
- Rapid trill preferable to slow; broaden slightly in sad pieces; pressure by passage dynamics; even, relaxed; never faster than can be even; upper tone snapped at the end. (101)
- Suffix on long notes; with dotted notes a tiny separation between suffix and next note; suffix as fast as trill; no suffix in descending successions of trills, short notes, triplets, when following notes replace it; no trill/suffix in an augmented second. (103–105)
- Don't trill slurred legato notes; tones sing on harpsichord too if not detached. Errors: limp suffix; trill not lasting the full note value (except short trill); plunging into trill without joining a preceding appoggiatura; loud trill in plaintive context; trilling every long note ("pretty little trills"). (106–107)
- Ascending trill (from below) on long notes, cadences, fermatas, renewing long trills; minor cadences sometimes trill on the 6th above the bass. Descending trill longest, now limited to repeated tones, descending successions, falling 3rds. Ornaments must not create forbidden fifths. (107–110)
- Half/short trill (Pralltriller): only on a descending second, joins the previous note; crackling speed, upper tone snapped; indispensable; hard to play softly on the pianoforte. (110–112)
