# Bodky, The Interpretation of Bach's Keyboard Works (1960) — notes
## Ch. 2 Instrument question (pp. 31–88)
- Bodky examines which instrument Bach intended (harpsichord one/two manuals, clavichord, home organ) piece by piece; some pieces suit both; owners of small harpsichords played everything; standard equipment of a real musician: large harpsichord, clavichord, pedal clavier (+ home organ). Some WTC pieces (E♭ prelude, B♭ minor P&F I, E♭ and E major fugues II, C♯ minor P&F I?, B♭ minor P&F II) suit the home organ — sustained notes; organ-fugue themes have pedal-playable figures unlike harpsichord themes. (86–87)
## Ch. 4 Tempo (pp. 100–145)
- Bach's tempi fall into a limited number of groups by affect and rhythmic organization; Bodky uses five: adagio, andante, moderato, allegro, presto ≈ MM 40, 60, 80, 100, 120 (80 = heartbeat pivot; ~120 upper limit set by historic harpsichord mechanism; ~40 lower limit). The same inner beat crosses time signatures. (Contrast: Quantz's four classes.) Bodky admits these rely on inner evidence and are debatable. (143–145)
- Rejects Rothschild's theory that metre + note values alone fix tempo (Bach notated the same piece in different metres). (143)
## Ch. 7 Articulation conclusions (pp. 221–222)
- Quantz and CPE agree; their remarks are the maximum definite information. Guide: allegro → nonlegato to staccato more probable; adagio → legato atmosphere; moderato → close intervals (2nds, often 3rds) slurred, larger intervals detached (also valid in fast pieces: Italian Concerto slurs). Harpsichord pieces in allegro/moderato lean to nonlegato/staccato; clavichord pieces to quasi-legato/legato; adagio quasi-legato on harpsichord, molto legato on clavichord. Each piece mixes affects (pathetic, caressing, gay, splendid, joking — Quantz) → vary articulation. (221–222)
## Ch. 3 Dynamics (pp. 89–99)
- Instrument reveals dynamics; few autograph f/p (mostly registration). Harpsichord: terrace dynamics — dance movements one colour throughout; usually two terraces (single tone vs octave doubling), large works a third (full work) entered after rests/fermata; concerto-grosso and fugue forms (development vs episodes) make f/p placement clear; unison runs (Chromatic Fantasy, toccatas) in octaves or full. Clavichord: range ppp–mf; beware overusing Bebung; clavichord pieces as intimate "confessions" — play as reading a lyric poem to oneself. (89–90)
## Ch. 6 Conventional alterations of rhythm (pp. 183–200)
- Notes inégales: Couperin "we do not play as we write"; rule: in conjunct passages, pairs of notes one degree shorter than the time-signature denominator are unequal (long-short), from slight hold to dotting; words louré/pointé vs notes égales/détaché/marqué. All sources restrict it to French music; Muffat (Lullists), Walther, Heinichen (first and third inherently long — maybe nomenclature only), Quantz (adds rules). Bodky: put aside inégales for Bach until better evidence. (183–186)
- Dotted notes: German composers unanimously adopted shortening the note after a dot (also groups of short notes in overtures; brief rest before the short note possible); Quantz XVII/7 §58: the French shorten an eighth after a quarter in bourrées, entrées, rigaudons, gavottes, rondeaus (4/4 or 2) and loures, sarabandes, courantes, chaconnes (3/4); synchronising with dotted rhythms in other voices (Partita 5 Sarabande print). Real overtures (French Overture, Partita 4, Partita 2 Sinfonia, Goldberg 16) clear; elsewhere delicate (French Suite 1 Gigue saturated with dotted rhythm → assimilate). (186–188)
