Part Two · Fux

From Gradus ad Parnassum (1725) · pp. 75–138
Fux · Introduction · pp. 75–77

Fux as Disciple of Palestrina

Fux himself called fugue the most important part of his book. Species counterpoint was a preparation for the moment when the student, “released from his fetters,” writes free composition. In imitation, the student’s own invention then serves as a cantus firmus from bar to bar.

The Gradus appeared in Latin in 1725 at imperial expense and sold out within a year. Bach’s pupil Mizler translated it “under the very eyes of Bach as it were.” Haydn and Beethoven made abstracts of it, and Strauss and Hindemith still taught from it. Fux teaches fugue in the six modes. Subjects use only the “natural tones,” and sharps and flats appear only to form a cadence or avoid a tritone, so that the student learns melody before harmony. The dialogue makes Palestrina (Aloysius) the master and Fux (Josephus) the pupil.

“Since God is the highest perfection, the harmony composed for his praise should follow the strictest rules that perfection can claim, as far as human imperfection can realize them.” (p. 77)

Fux · A Lesson on Imitation · pp. 78–79 · Exx. 26–33

Imitation

The first free composition: two voices, no cantus firmus. One voice follows the other after some rests with the same intervals, at any distance from the unison to the octave.

R Definition

  1. Imitation: a part follows another after rests, forming the same intervals, without regard for the mode or the position of whole and half steps. p. 78
  2. It may occur at the unison, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th or octave (Exx. 26–33). pp. 78–79

G Guidelines

  1. Not every note need be taken over; only a few notes need follow. Taking over every note would make a canon. p. 78
  2. Imitation may lead out of the mode (C to G in Ex. 29). It is also used later in a piece, where the mode matters less. p. 79
  3. Keep the rules of good, singable writing in both parts. p. 79
Fux’s assignmentTake a succession of notes with whatever intervals you choose, write a second part in counterpoint against it, and complete a two-part setting imitating at various intervals. p. 79
Fux · Fugues in General · pp. 79–81

Fugue, Mode, and the Exchange of Fifth and Fourth

Fugue differs from imitation in one point: the following part takes over the subject with due regard for the mode, and above all for where its half steps fall.

R Rules

  1. Fugue: a succession of notes in one part is taken over in another with regard for the mode, especially the position of whole and half steps. p. 80
  2. A mode is an octave divided into a fifth and a fourth. If the leading part skips a fifth, the following part must skip a fourth (and vice versa), so neither exceeds the octave of the mode. This does not apply to imitation. p. 80
  3. Fugal voices enter only at the unison, octave or fifth. An entry “at the fourth” is a fifth counted from the fundamental understood below. pp. 80, 84
  4. A subject suited to one mode will not suit another, because the half steps fall differently in each. p. 81
  5. No sharps or flats in the subject, so the nature of the modes is learnt. In the free continuation they are allowed, and needed to avoid mi contra fa. pp. 81, 84

G Guidelines

  1. Count the six modes as D, E, F, G, A, C, in the order they appear. Their half steps fall in six different places. p. 80
  2. The A mode comes closest to the D mode, but the answer would need a sharp on its next-to-last note to copy the D-mode subject. p. 81
Fux · Fugues in Two Parts · pp. 81–90 · Exx. 42–49

Fugues in Two Parts

Fux gives a fixed plan for the first fugues. It has three cadences, on the fifth, the third and the final of the mode, with the subject brought back after each one and pressed closer together at the end.

R The plan

  1. Choose a short subject suited to the mode and place it in the part you start with. p. 82
  2. The second part takes the same notes at the 4th below or 5th above, unaltered unless the mode requires a change. Meanwhile the first part continues in florid counterpoint. p. 82
  3. After a short continuation, the first cadence on the 5th of the mode. p. 82
  4. Resume the subject, usually in the first part on another tone. Mark the entry with a rest or a large skip. The other part may enter before the subject ends. p. 82
  5. After free writing, the second cadence on the 3rd of the mode. p. 82
  6. Finally, subject in one part and the other part following in the very next bar, “locking the two parts as closely as possible.” End with a cadence on the final. p. 82

G Exceptions and advice

  1. The theme’s note values may be changed (tied, shortened) near the end, where close entries cannot otherwise be combined. It even “lends a particular charm.” p. 85
  2. E mode: the first cadence falls on the 6th (C), not the 5th. A B major third would clash mi contra fa with the F that follows. p. 86
  3. F mode: a second cadence needing two or three sharps leaves the mode. Use a 7–6 cadence instead. p. 88
  4. A mode: an entry may be moved to another tone (B instead of A) when the lower part would clash. That is fine in the middle of a fugue but not at the beginning. p. 88
  5. Test a subject for close entries before choosing it. Diatonic subjects are simple on purpose: they teach the modes. p. 89
  6. These exercises are the “vestibule” of fugue, as drawing is to painting. Repeat them often. p. 90
Fux’s assignmentJosephus writes two-part fugues in the E, F, G, A and C modes after the master’s model in D, each on a subject suited to that mode alone. pp. 85–90
Fux · Fugues in Three Parts · pp. 90–102 · Exx. 50–69

Fugues in Three Parts

Three-part writing is “the most perfect,” because it allows complete triads. The new questions are where the third voice enters and how cadences are handled once a full close becomes possible.

R Rules

  1. The third part enters after the subject has appeared in the two others, with or without a short free continuation. p. 90
  2. Make its entry on a perfect triad, or better, on a tied dissonance, so it does not sound thin or indistinct. p. 91
  3. Avoid any perfect cadence (major third to octave) that does not coincide with an entry of the subject. A perfect cadence means complete rest, so it belongs only at the end or where a section closes and a new subject begins. pp. 91, 94
  4. After a rest a voice must bring back the subject (old or new), “if you do not want to make yourself open to the reproving words of the gospel”: “Friend, how camest thou in hither not having a wedding garment?” p. 96

G Guidelines

  1. The third part usually takes the interval of the first part, for variety, unless the context suggests otherwise. p. 91
  2. When an entry coincides with it, a cadence may fall on any degree not too far from the mode, perfect or deceptive. A deceptive cadence (inganno) uses a minor third, or the bass leaves the octave. pp. 91–92
  3. Two-part “cadences” (7–6, 2–3) are only preparations; a third part turns them into full cadences. p. 94
  4. Under upper parts descending in 7–6 or 2–3 suspensions, a bass may move freely in alternating 5ths and 6ths. This makes it easier to prepare entries. p. 94
  5. Rest a voice when two others crowd it, so it can return with the subject. Entries over 5–6 motion are strong. An entry may be marked by a strong dissonance. p. 95
  6. Use tied notes (syncopations) as often as possible. They make each part distinct and easy to follow. p. 101
Fux’s assignmentWrite three-part fugues in the E, F, G, A and C modes on the two-part subjects (Exx. 65–69). Return to three-part writing “again and again,” since “triplicity expresses perfection.” pp. 96–102
Fux · Fugues in Four Parts · pp. 102–106 · Exx. 70–72

Fugues in Four Parts

The fourth voice brings a rule of correspondence between voices, and a warning against crowding them.

R Rules

  1. The soprano’s entry corresponds to the tenor’s, the alto’s to the bass’s. p. 102
  2. Do not crowd the parts. “Just as a person walking through a dense crowd would be pushed from left, right, and front,” a voice without space cannot unfold. p. 102

G Guidelines

  1. If a part has no room: rewrite, or rest it and let it return later with the subject. Better still, plan ahead. pp. 102–103
  2. Four parts need not always sound together. Three may move while the fourth rests before its next entry. p. 104
Fux · Double Counterpoint at the Octave · pp. 106–114 · Exx. 73–83

Double Counterpoint at the Octave

Fugues with several themes need parts that can be exchanged, the upper becoming the lower. Fux teaches three species: at the octave, the tenth and the twelfth.

R Rules

  1. Several subjects must move differently (different note values), must not begin together (one enters after rests), and must keep within the interval limit of the species. p. 108
  2. At the octave, avoid the fifth: it inverts to a fourth, a dissonance in two parts. A tied fifth is allowed. p. 108
  3. Do not approach the octave by skip: it inverts to a unison reached by skip. p. 109
  4. Avoid the octave on a strong beat except in a syncopation, since it inverts to a unison. p. 109
  5. Keep the parts within an octave. Beyond it, inversion only shifts the intervals’ register without producing a new harmony. p. 110

G Guidelines

  1. If every downbeat is reached by contrary or oblique motion, a third part can be added by doubling the counterpoint a tenth below. p. 111
  2. In a fugue with a countersubject (Ex. 81), the countersubject begins after a half-bar rest and keeps answering the subject at the octave in outer and inner voices. A compound interval (15th) counts as simple. p. 112
  3. Change note values so that both themes can be combined in stretto. This “will be considered much to the composer’s credit.” p. 113
  4. Let the countersubject enter sometimes in the second or third bar of the subject, not always in the first. p. 114
Fux · Double Counterpoint at the Tenth · pp. 114–122 · Exx. 84–97

Double Counterpoint at the Tenth

Either part may move a tenth up or down. Thirds and sixths become octaves and fifths, so parallel imperfect consonances are now forbidden.

R Rules

  1. No two thirds or tenths in direct motion: they invert to unisons and octaves. p. 114
  2. No two sixths in succession: they invert to fifths. p. 114
  3. No tied fourth in the upper part. It would invert to a seventh resolving to an octave, which Fux does not accept. p. 115
  4. Do not exceed the tenth. p. 115

G Guidelines

  1. Transpose either part a tenth, or raise the cantus a third and drop the counterpoint an octave. p. 116
  2. A third part can be added by doubling at the tenth when downbeats are reached by contrary or oblique motion. p. 117
  3. To stay in the mode when inverting at the start, open on the first or third degree. Inversion need not run from beginning to end; use it where judgment suggests. p. 118
  4. Counterpoint at the octave and at the tenth can be combined in the same theme. p. 119
  5. A rest may be followed by the countersubject in contrary motion: “not only permissible” but a source of variety. p. 121
Fux · Double Counterpoint at the Twelfth · pp. 122–131 · Exx. 98–120

Double Counterpoint at the Twelfth; Melodic Inversion

At the twelfth every interval may be used except the sixth (it becomes a seventh). A counterpoint at the twelfth built mostly on fifths, octaves and thirds also inverts at the octave and the tenth, so two parts yield many three- and four-part settings.

R Rules

  1. Avoid the sixth and the 7–6 suspension. A tied sixth is possible. pp. 122, 127
  2. Do not exceed the twelfth. p. 122
  3. For a three-part setting: a fifth at the beginning and at the end, contrary or oblique motion throughout, no dissonant ties. p. 125
  4. Melodic inversion only for settings without dissonant ties, because syncopated dissonances must resolve downward. p. 130

G Guidelines

  1. End the upper part on the 5th so that, transposed a twelfth, it ends properly in the mode. Use B♭ where needed to keep the intervals. p. 123
  2. Fifths and octaves in contrary motion, and thirds by oblique motion, give the richest results. p. 128
  3. Simple (free) inversion reverses direction only. Strict inversion keeps mi–fa: in the diatonic system it mirrors the Dorian scale (d↔d, e↔c, f↔b, g↔a). In the mixed system it is enough to keep the half-step relations, choosing the mirror that stays in the mode. pp. 130, 134
  4. Original and inverted forms may alternate. Smaller note values let them sound together, and the inversion can be saved for the middle of the piece. p. 135
Fux · conclusion · pp. 131–138 · Exx. 121–128

Cantus Firmus, Several Subjects, and Release

The last lessons combine a fugue with a cantus firmus, combine a subject with its strict inversion, and write a fugue on three subjects. Then the student is released into free invention.

R Rules

  1. With a cantus firmus, first examine every bar of it to see where the subject (original or inverted) can enter. A subject may even be drawn from the cantus itself, with changed or syncopated note values. p. 132
  2. In a fugue on several subjects, give each subject its own note values. Where one subject is to appear at the twelfth, the other parts must not form a sixth with it. p. 137

G Guidelines

  1. Now use the mixed (chromatic) system, any meter, smaller note values and “irregular themes”, meaning those whose tonal answer needs several changes. p. 134
  2. A subject need not appear complete every time. Three subjects almost require a fifth voice. p. 137
  3. Without double counterpoint, a sacred work a cappella is “bare and void of distinction.” p. 131
  4. “To him who masters four parts, the path to composition with more than four voices is cleared; for as the number of voices increases, the rules may be less rigidly observed.” p. 138

“Farewell, and pray to God for me.” Fux, ending the Gradus because of illness (p. 138)
