Marpurg · Introduction · pp. 139–141

Marpurg as Interpreter of Bach

Marpurg wrote the preface to the second edition of the Art of Fugue (1752), then the first book devoted to fugue. He had talked fugue with Bach himself, and two of his rules come from that conversation.

G From the conversation with Bach

  1. Bach called a fugue that “stuck continuously to its principal subject, without any change” “dry and wooden”. Marpurg’s rule: invent a theme that admits many changes of accompaniment. p. 140
  2. Bach also faulted a composer who “had not shown enough fire to reanimate the theme by interludes.” Marpurg’s rule: if no episode precedes a cadence, put an episode rather than an entry on the cadence, to raise anticipation of the next entry. p. 141
  3. Examine a theme thoroughly before writing. C. P. E. Bach recalled that his father could predict, after the first entries, which devices a fugue would use, and nudged his son when he was right. p. 141

G Mann’s assessment

  1. Marpurg gives the first modern frame of reference for fugal terms. He is encyclopedic, not didactic. p. 141
  2. He uses instrumental and keyboard thinking (G clef, keyboard score) and quotes real works, especially Bach’s, instead of writing his own models. p. 57
  3. His weakness: he hardens into rules what Rameau left to judgment, and he forbids progressions “even though examples … are found in the writings of the great masters.” p. 56

“To be an excellent musician and not to appreciate the virtues of the late Bach is a contradiction.” Marpurg (p. 141)

Marpurg · Imitation and Fugue in General · pp. 142–160

Imitation, Its Kinds, and the Classes of Fugue

Marpurg sorts every way one part can follow another, then defines the fugue as periodic imitation governed by rules. It has five characteristic elements.

R Definitions

  1. Repetition: same tones in the same part. Transposition: other tones in the same part. Imitation: either one in another part. p. 142
  2. Eight species by interval, from unison to octave. The upper 2nd equals the lower 7th, and so on. pp. 143–146
  3. Four melodic motions: direct; inverted (free, al rovescio, or strict, al contrario riverso); retrograde; inverted retrograde. Also augmentation, diminution, interrupted imitation, contrary rhythm (per arsin et thesin) and invertible imitation. pp. 146–152
  4. Periodic imitation takes over part of the leader; canonic imitation takes over all of it. A canonic fugue is a canon; a periodic fugue governed by rules is a fugue. pp. 152–154
  5. The fugue is continuous, not divided into sections. A fugal gigue is two fugues, the second usually on the inverted theme. p. 154
  6. Five characteristic elements: (1) theme (dux, subject); (2) answer (comes); (3) exposition (repercussio); (4) counterparts; (5) episodes. pp. 154–155

G Classes and terms

  1. Regular fugue: the elements are arranged by the rules. Irregular: they are handled arbitrarily, and these are often called caprice. Newer irregular fugues may open with all voices in octaves (Scheibe). p. 155
  2. Strict fugue (fuga obbligata): almost nothing but the theme, with every counterpart and episode derived from it. Long and full of artifice, it is a ricercar, “a master fugue,” as most of Bach’s are. Free fugue (fuga libera): episodes use a related secondary idea, as most of Handel’s do. p. 156
  3. Fuga reditta: a short canonic section on the theme. Stretto: overlapping statements, complete or shortened, direct or inverted. p. 157
  4. Double fugue: several themes (theme and countersubjects), developed together by means of double counterpoint. p. 157
  5. Six classes: by interval of answer; by motion (counterfugue, retrograde); by augmentation or diminution; by contrary rhythm; by interrupted imitation; mixed. p. 158
  6. The ordinary fugue is the fugue at the fifth, combining answers at the 5th, 4th and 8ve, because tonic and dominant are a key’s two main triads. Fugues at other intervals are “extraordinary” and serve as ornaments inside it. pp. 158–159
Marpurg · The Theme or Opening Statement · pp. 160–164

The Theme

Length, melody, range, and the possibility of many accompaniments: what makes a theme fit for fugue.

R Rules

  1. Fit the theme to the medium and the number of voices. p. 160
  2. No lively runs, large unnatural skips or arpeggios: these belong to caprices, not fugues, at least in vocal and keyboard fugues. p. 162
  3. Keep the theme within an octave. Instrumental themes may reach a tenth or twelfth, but the octave is best. p. 163
  4. Do not end the theme with a perfect cadence. If one seems unavoidable, enter the answer on the cadence or hide it with added notes. p. 163
  5. Begin on any beat. End on a strong beat, except for a feminine ending in vocal fugues. p. 164

G Guidelines

  1. The slower the tempo, the fewer bars. Plain unaccompanied notes in a slow tempo become “unbearable.” p. 161
  2. The shorter the theme, the more often it can return, and “the more often a theme is repeated, the better the fugue.” Short themes help the listener and the improviser. p. 162
  3. A theme is long enough “whenever it expresses a clear thought,” sometimes in a single bar. No fixed length, because variety is “the soul of all music.” p. 162
  4. “The melodic, natural element—simple, noble, and singable—leads to the best fugues” (Mattheson). p. 162
  5. Imagine the bass and other parts while inventing the theme, to see whether it can be developed. p. 163
Marpurg · The Answer · pp. 164–175 · Exx. 156–178

The Answer: Similarity and Adjustment

The answer is a transposition of the theme to the dominant, as similar as possible. The octave, however, divides into two unequal halves (C–G and G–C), so the melody sometimes has to be adjusted so as not to leave the key.

R Rules

  1. Principle 1, similarity. Same rhythm and rests, same mode (no change from major to minor), same intervals with the same quality. The first note may be lengthened or shortened by half its value. p. 165
  2. Principle 2, adjustment. A theme that keeps the character of the key gets an exact (real) transposition to the dominant. A theme that moves toward the dominant at its end has its answer adjusted back, to avoid modulating to the dominant of the dominant. Look at what follows, not only at what came before. p. 166
  3. Adjust by skipping a tone (widening, in the larger half-scale) or repeating one (narrowing, in the smaller half-scale). A unison becomes a second, a second a third, and so on. p. 166
  4. First note: theme on ①, answer on ⑤; theme on ⑤, answer on ①. p. 167
  5. Last note: ① ↔ ⑤; a theme ending on ③ of the key is answered on ③ of the dominant, and vice versa. Unusual endings (②, ④, ⑥) go to the same degree of the dominant unless the context forbids it. p. 167
  6. Inside the theme too, a skip between ⑤ and ① is answered by ① and ⑤. p. 167

G Guidelines and exceptions

  1. Find the correspondences by laying the dominant scale under the tonic scale so that ① meets ④ and ⑤: ②↔⑥, ③↔⑦, ④ and ⑤ → ①. Other pairings (⑥↔③, ⑤↔②) also occur. p. 164
  2. A theme’s end is found by seeing how far the answer reproduces it. The answer may begin before the theme has ended. p. 168
  3. ⑤ may be answered by ② (not ①) when the theme can still be heard in the main key, or reaches ⑤ without a perfect cadence. pp. 168, 175
  4. Chromatic themes: strip the accidentals, find the diatonic answer, then put the chromatic steps back on the corresponding notes (A minor c–c♯, d–d♯ → f–f♯, g–g♯). p. 170
  5. A Phrygian close on ⑤ in minor is answered on the ⑤ of the dominant (D minor: theme ends on A, answer on E). This is an exception to the ①/⑤ rule. p. 172
  6. The leap from the octave down to the 5th was forbidden by the “old masters,” yet it works well: “none of these rules is without exception” (Mattheson). p. 168
  7. Between two possible answers, prefer the one that changes less and keeps the theme’s characteristic (chromatic) steps (Fux, quoted). p. 175
Marpurg · Exposition and Development · pp. 176–198 · Exx. 179–189

Exposition and Development

Rules for the order of entries in the first exposition, and a six-step plan for everything after it. An analysis of a four-part fugue in seven expositions shows the plan at work.

R Order of entries

  1. Don’t repeat the theme at once in the same register or on the same tone. p. 176
  2. In a two-part fugue for unequal voices (soprano and bass, as in Bach’s Inventions), the voices take the theme alternately. After the first or second exposition a voice may repeat it, preferably in a new key. pp. 176–177
  3. In three parts the third voice enters at the octave of the first (better, for variety) or of the second. It may enter on time, after a short episode, or even before the second has finished. p. 177
  4. In four parts the voices pair, inner with outer: soprano with tenor, alto with bass. Entries 1 and 3 have the theme, 2 and 4 the answer. The same applies to double fugues, and in five or more voices odd numbers pair with odd, even with even. pp. 177–178
  5. Use the accepted order in the first exposition. Of the 24 possible orders, the others are for later. p. 179

G Development of a simple fugue

  1. After the first exposition, continue with an episode and a cadence (tonic or dominant), or cadence at once if the theme suggests it. p. 179
  2. On the cadence, enter with theme or answer in a voice that did not just have it, or with an episode to raise expectation. p. 179
  3. In later expositions the answer may come closer than in the first. p. 180
  4. Rest the voice that will start the next exposition. Episodes lead to cadences in related keys: ordinary keys (V, VI, III) before extraordinary ones (IV and II in major, IV and VII in minor). The subdominant is saved for the end. p. 180
  5. In a new key keep the mode relationship: after a move from major to minor, answer in a related minor key, and vice versa. p. 180
  6. Present the theme in as many related keys as possible, complete or shortened. Return home by careful modulation with periodic or canonic imitation, and end with a final cadence, perhaps over a pedal point. p. 180
  7. Don’t keep the theme in the outer voices; put it in inner voices too, with full and with sparse harmony. p. 179
  8. Voices may rest to return with fresh effect, generally resting when another voice enters. p. 185

G The seven expositions (model analysis)

  1. Bars 1–9: tenor and soprano have the theme, alto and bass the answer, with no episodes. The third entry comes on a tied 2nd, the fourth on a consonance. Lesson: don’t delay entries when the texture allows them. p. 182
  2. Bars 9–20: same order, but theme and answer change places. Short imitative interpolations cancel cadences. p. 183
  3. Bars 20–27: theme and answer each taken twice in succession, at the octave and closer. p. 183
  4. Bars 27–34: stretto at unequal distances, with double counterpoint at the octave. p. 184
  5. Bars 34–37: the theme in parallel thirds, answered by the other pair. p. 184
  6. Bars 37–44: crowded entries in double counterpoint, then an episode. p. 184
  7. Bars 44–end: shortened theme, stretti, answers in thirds, an extended final cadence. Entries stay on tonic and dominant: in the old style, episodes do the modulating. pp. 182–184

G Two parts, counterfugue, inversion

  1. Three parts: omit the fourth entry of each exposition. Two parts: omit two. Two-part fugues are usually double fugues with a countersubject in double counterpoint at the octave. p. 185
  2. A flawless two-part fugue is “a model of the art of composition”: there the melody, not the texture, carries everything. p. 185
  3. Counterfugue (fuga contraria): the answer is inverted (freely or strictly) at the 5th, octave, 3rd or another interval. Keep the chosen manner until the stretti, though an occasional direct answer is allowed if the next entry returns to inversion. p. 185
  4. Invertible fugue (fuga inversa): the whole fugue, counterparts included, can be read inverted, a mirror fugue (Art of Fugue, Exx. 183–184). Do not confuse it with the counterfugue. p. 186
Marpurg · Double Fugues · pp. 190–198 · Exx. 185–189

Double Fugues

Several themes, developed together. Plan them in double counterpoint before writing a note.

R Rules

  1. Distinguish the themes rhythmically and melodically. p. 191
  2. Themes should not end together. p. 191
  3. Before composing, try all the themes in double counterpoint, and test each one for imitation, stretto, augmentation, diminution, contrary rhythm and inversion. Plan the expositions from the results. p. 191

G Guidelines

  1. Have one more voice than themes. It allows rests and freer imitation. p. 191
  2. The themes need not always be complete, nor always appear together. After the first exposition, either one may return first. p. 191
  3. Method (a): develop theme 1 like a simple fugue, cadence (usually in the main key), develop theme 2, then combine them unexpectedly (Battiferri, Gottlieb Muffat). p. 192
  4. Method (b): introduce all the themes at once. (i) Theme 2 enters before theme 1 is complete, as in most of Handel’s fugues. (ii) Theme 2 comes with the entry of the second voice. (iii) The first voice takes up theme 2 after a short rest while the second voice answers theme 1, as in Kuhnau and Bach. p. 192
Marpurg · The Counterparts and the Episodes · pp. 199–204

Counterparts and Episodes

Every voice that is not stating the theme is a counterpart. Every passage without the theme is an episode. Both should come from the theme.

R Counterparts

  1. The opening statement is normally unaccompanied, and always in keyboard fugues. Counterparts begin when the answer enters. p. 199
  2. Never forfeit an opportunity for an entry. An entry on a tied dissonance is excellent; otherwise it forms a consonance. p. 200
  3. All voices compete on the same level, with good melody and strict independence. No unison or octave doublings in a regular fugue, even though great masters have used them (instruments doubling voices excepted). p. 201

G Counterparts

  1. Write the counterpart in one basic species and keep to it. Florid writing tends to drift away from the theme. Best of all is a counterpart borrowed from the theme. p. 200
  2. In a fugue on several themes, one theme is the other’s counterpart. Themes that go in thirds make this easiest. p. 201
  3. The more voices, the less florid the counterparts. A counterpart is not a figured-bass filler; distinguish the voices by syncopations and passing or changing notes. pp. 201–202
  4. Keep the voices neither too close nor too far apart. Rest the voice that will soon enter with the theme. p. 202

R Episodes

  1. An episode continues the counterpart until the theme returns, and must be related to the theme. p. 202
  2. No large leaps, arpeggios, extended runs, accompaniment figures, unisons or octaves, and no arioso or galant lines: nothing that cannot be imitated in all voices. p. 202

G Episodes

  1. Take episode ideas from the theme and its counterpart. If neither yields any, invent simple progressions in the theme’s character. p. 203
  2. Not too long or too frequent, or the theme is forgotten. p. 203
  3. Shape an episode so that an entry can break into it, or let it end in a cadence where the theme returns. p. 203
  4. Not all voices need take part. A voice that rests re-enters with more emphasis, especially an inner voice. p. 203
  5. End with a statement of the theme (better) or a short harmonic turn after it. p. 204
Marpurg · Conclusion · pp. 204–212

A Model Vocal Fugue: Kyrie on Three Themes

Marpurg ends by analysing a four-voice Kyrie with doubling instruments. Its three themes are “Kyrie eleison,” “eleison” and “Christe eleison,” and its two first themes are answered in contrary motion: a double counterfugue.

First part: tonic and dominant. Themes 1 and 2 are answered in contrary motion and exchanged by double counterpoint at the octave, with stretto diminutions of theme 2. Second part: theme 3 enters at successive lower fourths, like a modulating perpetual canon (“he might do this for the sake of exercise” through all twelve keys). It centres on III and VI (B minor, E minor), and full cadences are avoided by modulation, extended lines or new entries. Third part: A minor and D minor, stretti of theme 1 against the shortened theme 3. Fourth part: a dominant pedal, final entries, a tonic pedal, the end.

“The best and most direct way to gain competence in the composition of fugues is to adhere to the examples of the great masters.” Marpurg (p. 212)
