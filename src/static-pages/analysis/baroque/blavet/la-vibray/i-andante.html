I. Andante

The opening movement of Michel Blavet’s D minor sonata for flute and basso continuo, from the Paris print. A theme of two phrases, a move to A minor, a Fonte and a Monte, the theme again, and a closing sequence that Blavet repeats piano. Below: the form, every cadence, the partimento patterns in the figured bass, and the flute part in three versions: as Blavet printed it with his own breath marks, read by Quantz’s rules, and as Toshio Takahashi revised it for the Suzuki Flute School.

  • 64 bars, 3/4, no repeats
  • Keys D minor → A minor → G minor → F major → D minor
  • 16 cadences marked
  • 38 pattern brackets
  • 11 breath marks by Blavet

Partimento Patterns

Schema

Do–Re–Mi

The theme is the opening pattern Do–Re–Mi, a melody that rises 1–2–3: D, a trilled E, F (mm. 1–3, 8–10, 43–45). The pattern usually has a bass 1–7–1 or 1–5–1 under it; Blavet leaves the flute alone and brings the bass in under the third note. In the Fonte the same bar returns in G minor and F major (mm. 28–29, 32–33), and there the bass supplies 5 with a seventh under the second note and 1 under the third.

Rule of the octave

Rising 1–2–3–4–5

The bass climbs from the tonic to the dominant with the figures of the rule of the octave (♯6 on 2, 6 on 3, a fifth or 65 on 4) while the flute rises in tenths. It carries the theme each time (mm. 4–6, 11–13, 46–48), and returns in G minor (mm. 29–30) and, with a raised 4, in F major (mm. 33–34). The first time 4 has a plain fifth, as in Durante’s older scale; after that it has 65, Fenaroli’s standard chord.

Cadence

Passo Indietro, the Alto Cadence

The dominant steps back: 5, then 4 under the held harmony (Blavet’s dash, a 42 chord), then 3 with 6. Passo indietro is Gjerdingen’s name; partimento teaching calls the bass 5–4–3 the alto cadence, the weakest close, which a stronger one follows. In D minor before the full cadences (mm. 13, 48) and twice in A minor (mm. 20, 25).

Cadence

Long Cadence With ⁶₅

3 with 6, 4 with 65, 5, 5 an octave lower, 1, under a trill on the second degree: the long cadence (cadenza lunga), whose dominant is prepared by the fourth degree. The same close in D minor (mm. 14–15, 49–50, 56–57, 63–64), A minor (mm. 26–27), G minor (mm. 30–31) and F major (mm. 34–35). The dominant has no suspended fourth, so in Fenaroli’s terms each is a simple cadence.

Schema

Comma

The leading tone in the bass, figured with a diminished fifth (5̸), resolves to the tonic: C♯–D (m. 7) and, in A minor, G♯–A under the bar of the theme (m. 21). Each is followed by a half cadence.

Bass motion

Tied Bass

The bass note is tied over the barline and becomes a dissonance (figured 2), then resolves down a step: A–G with 2 to 6 (mm. 17–18), continuing as a descending tetrachord A–G–F–E, and A–G–F with 2, ♯4, 6 (mm. 22–24), which leans toward D minor for a bar. In partimento teaching this is the suspension that lies in the bass; the second half cadence that follows (m. 24) is the Phrygian one, F with 6 falling a half step to E.

Schema

Fonte

Four bars in G minor, then the same four bars a step lower in F major (mm. 28–31, 32–35): the classic pair, minor then major, each with its Do–Re–Mi over a dominant seventh, its rising bass and its cadence. In a piece in minor the Fonte is borrowed from the relative major: G minor and F major are the second and first degrees of F. Riepel named the pattern in 1755.

Schema

Monte

The bass rises by half steps, F–F♯–G–G♯–A, a bar to each note (mm. 35–39). The sharpened notes carry a diminished fifth (5̸) and act as leading tones, so the harmony climbs F, G, A: a close on the fourth degree of D minor and then on the fifth, which is what Riepel’s Monte does. It leads from the relative major back to the dominant.

Bass motion

Third Down, Second Up, With 9

The closing figure falls a step in each bar (mm. 50–54). Under it the bass falls a third and rises a step, E–C♯, D–B, C–A, B♭–G, A: a triad on each downbeat, a diminished fifth or 65 on each third beat, and a 9 over each arrival. Flute and bass move in tenths on the downbeats. Blavet repeats it piano with the bass an octave lower (mm. 57–61).

Cadence

Evaded Cadence

Before each final close the dominant is held for two beats (♯, then 4) and falls to F with a 6 instead of D (mm. 55, 62): 5–3 in the bass, the alto cadence. The flute’s D is tied over the barline, and the long cadence follows in the next bar.

Performing the Flute Part

Blavet’s print already carries a performance layer of its own: slurs, trills, small notes and eleven breath marks. The blue marks above the flute staff show those breaths (’), the few we add by Quantz’s rules ((’), in parentheses), and Quantz’s tonguing and dynamics for the passage ours. The red marks above them are the printed marks of Toshio Takahashi’s revision for the Suzuki Flute School. Hover any mark for its source; both layers can be switched off above the score.

What Blavet Prints source

  • h: a breath. Walter Kolneder’s preface to the Bärenreiter edition identifies the small h as Blavet’s own breath sign (h for haleine). It stands between the note that ends and the note that begins again: mm. 3, 6, 8, 10, 13, 22, 27, 31, 43, 45, 48.
  • +: a trill (tremblement). It stands on the dotted note of the theme’s figure and on the note that prepares each cadence.
  • Small notes: a single small note between two notes a third apart (mm. 13, 48, 55, 62: C♯, small B, A), two small notes after a trill (its ending: mm. 13, 23), and one accented small note before a dotted note (m. 42).
  • Slurs: on the pairs of eighths, on the dotted figure with its two sixteenths, and nowhere over a leap.
  • P.: piano, once, for the repeat of the closing sequence (m. 57).

Rules We Apply Quantz

Paraphrased from the Versuch (1752), chapters VI, VII, VIII, IX, XI and XVII, in Reilly’s translation. Quantz met Blavet in Paris in 1726–27; Reilly’s introduction speaks of a warm friendship (p. xviii), and Quantz names him among the players who surpassed their predecessors (I, p. 30).

  • Breath: between a long note and a short one, never after the last note of a bar (VII §3, p. 87); after a cadence, before a new idea or the return of the subject (§4, p. 88); wide leaps suit a breath best (§8, p. 89); only when needed (§7, p. 89). All eleven of Blavet’s marks fit these rules.
  • di: the soft stroke for slow and sustained notes (VI.i §6, p. 73); under a slur only the first note is tipped (§10, p. 74). di ri for stepwise eighths of moderate speed (VI.ii §1, p. 76), ti for leaps (VI.i §7, p. 73).
  • Slurs: sustained and flattering notes are slurred, gay and leaping ones detached (XI §11, p. 123).
  • Small notes: a passing appoggiatura between notes a third apart belongs to the time of the note before it, “the French style of playing” (VIII §6, pp. 93–94). An accented one is tipped gently, swells, and takes half its note, or two thirds of a dotted one (VIII §4, p. 93; p. 95).
  • Trills begin with their appoggiatura and end with two little notes, written or not (IX §7, p. 103).
  • Dissonances are played more strongly than consonances (XVII.vi §12, p. 254); a repeated passage alternates forte and piano (XII, p. 133).

Quantz’s Rules and This Movement

Rules Quantz

Paraphrased from the Versuch, XI (good execution), XIV (the Adagio and its relatives) and XVII.vii (tempo), Reilly pp. 123–124, 168 and 284–287.

  • Tempo by the pulse. Quantz measures tempo by a pulse of about 80 beats a minute and by the fastest notes in the piece (XVII.vii §51, p. 285). In a singing piece in 3/4 with the bass in eighths the pulse falls on each eighth; where the bass moves in quarters and the melody is more arioso than sad, on each quarter (pp. 286–287). Blavet’s bass moves in quarters, so the page starts at quarter = 80.
  • French moderation. Quantz remarks that French musicians of his day kept a moderate speed in lively pieces (XVII.vii §50, p. 285, note).
  • Not every slow piece is an Adagio. The Cantabile, Arioso, Andante, Largo and the like must be clearly distinguished in playing from a melancholy, pathetic Adagio (XIV §19, p. 168).
  • An Andante in 3/4. When the air leaps in quarters over a bass in eighths that stay on one harmony, it may be played a little more seriously, and with more graces, than an Arioso; where the bass moves by step, added graces need care, so that they make no forbidden fifths and octaves with it (XIV §21, p. 168). Blavet’s bass moves by step under the theme (mm. 4–6), the second case.
  • Unequal notes. In a moderate tempo the quickest notes are played a little unequally, the ones on the beat longer: in 3/4 the eighths, unless quicker notes are mixed in; never under a slur of more than two notes, on repeated notes, or under dots (XI §12, pp. 123–124).
  • Light and shade. Forte and piano alternate continually, and the execution suits each passion (XI §§14–15, p. 124).

Guidelines and Our Readings guideline ours

  • Why Quantz for a French sonata. The sonata was printed in Paris about twenty years before the Versuch. Quantz knew Blavet and describes the French manner of small notes at first hand, but he writes as a Berlin musician of 1752. Where Blavet’s own marks speak (breaths, slurs, trills), they come first; Quantz is used to explain them and to fill the gaps. Hotteterre’s Principes (1707) would be the nearer French witness and has not been read for this page.
  • Unequal eighths. By Quantz’s rule the eighths of a 3/4 Andante would be slightly unequal, but sixteenths are mixed into this movement, which moves the inequality to them; and French usage, the nearer guide, is not covered by our sources. The page plays the eighths as written and leaves the lilt to the player.
  • Names. Do–Re–Mi, Comma and Passo indietro are Robert Gjerdingen’s terms, from Music in the Galant Style (2007); Fonte and Monte are Joseph Riepel’s (1755), taken up by Gjerdingen. The rule of the octave, the tied bass, the bass that falls a third and rises a step, and the long, simple, alto and Phrygian cadences belong to Neapolitan partimento teaching (Durante’s and Fenaroli’s rules). Blavet uses none of these words here.
  • Where the pattern names come from. They follow the partimento catalog made for this site, 96 patterns that were checked there against Fenaroli, Durante, Sanguinetti’s The Art of Partimento and Gjerdingen. Those books were not read again for this page; each pattern was matched to its entry in the catalog by its bass degrees and figures.
  • Patterns that fit loosely. A Do–Re–Mi has a bass under it; Blavet’s theme starts without one. A Fonte is usually two short steps; Blavet’s are two whole phrases of four bars, and their bass goes 5–1 where the short form goes 7–1. A Monte starts from the leading tone of the fourth degree; Blavet reaches each leading tone by a half step from the note before, so the bass is fully chromatic. The long cadence in its standard form holds a fourth over the dominant before the major third; Blavet’s dominant has none.
  • The form has no repeat signs. We read three statements of the theme (complete, complete with a full cadence, shortened) around a middle part in A minor, G minor and F major, and a closing sequence with its echo. The echo, marked only by P., is what French sources call a petite reprise; the name is ours here.
  • Cadence types (PAC, HC) are modern labels. We call a cadence perfect only when the bass goes 5–1 in root position and the flute ends on 1.
  • The 9 in the closing sequence. The flute rests on each downbeat that is figured 9, so the ninth sounds only in the continuo. We take the figures as printed.

Notes on the Reading

The Score

Two staves as in the Paris print: flute and figured bass. Trills are shown with Blavet’s +, breaths with his h, and the small notes as he places them, including the two that end a trill before the barline. The dash in the figures holds the harmony of the note before; a slashed 5 is a diminished fifth. Where the print puts a dot after the barline to lengthen a note (mm. 55–56, 62–63), the score writes a tie.

Readings we are less sure of: the first slur of m. 2 (hidden under the title in the scan, taken from m. 9); the bass’s third note in m. 7 (a low E on a ledger line, unfigured); the figure on G in m. 5 (a fifth and a flat, against 6, 5 and a flat in mm. 12 and 47); the 9 on the downbeats of mm. 51–54 and 58–61; the ♭6 printed over the rest in mm. 54 and 61, which we give to the third beat before its 5; the ♯ and 4 over the half note A in mm. 55 and 62; the unsigned B in the bass of m. 34, read as B natural; and in Takahashi’s part the V inside his own slur in m. 18.
