II. Allemanda

The second movement of Michel Blavet’s D minor sonata for flute and basso continuo, marked Allegro in the Paris print. Two repeated halves: the first goes from D minor to A minor, the second comes back through G minor and F major, and its last three bars are played twice. Below: the form, every cadence, the partimento patterns in the figured bass, and the flute part in three versions: as Blavet printed it with his own slurs and breath marks, read by Quantz’s rules, and as Toshio Takahashi revised it for the Suzuki Flute School.

  • 32 bars and an upbeat, common time
  • Two repeated halves, 12 + 20 bars
  • Keys D minor → A minor ‖ A minor → G minor → F major → D minor

Cadences

Every cadence marked on the score, with the evidence from the bass and the figures. All labels are our reading ours.

  • Half cadences stand on the third beat, under a trilled leading tone or just after it: in D minor (mm. 4, 25) and in A minor (m. 7). Each is followed by a sequence.
  • Full cadences use one bass pattern, 4 with 65, 5, 5 an octave lower, 1: A minor (mm. 11, 12) and D minor (m. 32). Two more arrive with other basses: G minor (m. 19) and F major (m. 21).
  • Imperfect cadences: in m. 2 the bass closes while the flute is on the third; in m. 29 the flute closes while the bass comes from the leading tone. The second is what makes the petite reprise necessary.
  • Elision: the A that ends the cadence of mm. 10–11 is the first note of the closing bars.
  • The two halves rhyme: mm. 26–28 are mm. 8–10 a fourth higher, so the first half ends in A minor with the music that ends the second in D minor.
  • Breath and cadence: five of Blavet’s fifteen breath marks follow a cadence (mm. 4, 19, 21, 25, 29); the others stand before an upbeat that begins the next idea or the next step of a sequence.

Partimento Patterns

The bass patterns under every bar, named as in the site’s partimento catalog: the bass motions and cadences of Neapolitan partimento teaching (the bass that falls a third and rises a step, the returning bass suspension, the rule of the octave, the long, compound and alto cadences) and the galant schemata (Do–Re–Mi, Fonte, Monte). Each reading rests on the bass line and Blavet’s own figures. The names are labels we apply, not Blavet’s guideline. The buttons scroll to the place and play it.

Schema

Do–Re–Mi

The theme’s three slurred figures begin on D, E and F: the opening pattern Do–Re–Mi, a melody that rises 1–2–3. In m. 1 it has the pattern’s most common bass, 1–7–1 (D, C♯ with 6, D). In the second half it comes in A minor with the flute alone at first (m. 13) and in D minor over D, C♯ with 5̸, D (m. 15).

Schema

Fonte

One bar in G minor, then the same bar a step lower in F major (mm. 5–6): minor first, major second, each with its own tonic, dominant and rising bass. The flute is transposed note for note; only the last eighth of each bar differs, because it leads on. G minor and F major are the second and first degrees of F: a piece in minor borrows its Fonte from the relative major. Riepel named the pattern in 1755.

Bass motion

Third Down, Second Up

The bass takes the sixteenths: three repeated notes, a step down, and a circling figure around a note a third lower, figured 5̸ or 65, which rises a step to the next. Triad on the strong beat, 65 on the weak one, and the chord roots fall by fifths: from E to A (mm. 7–9) and from G to D (mm. 26–27, 29–30). Above it the flute moves slowly, a tenth above the strong beats: a small note leaning into a long one, a slurred turn, a trill.

Cadence

Long Cadence

The dominant is prepared by the fourth degree. In m. 2 the long cadence (cadenza lunga) has its standard form: 3 with 6, 4 with 65, 5 with a fourth that falls to the major third, 1. It starts from 1 in A minor (mm. 10–11, 12) and D minor (m. 32), and has a plain triad on 4 in F major (m. 21). In G minor (mm. 18–19) the close is Fenaroli’s compound cadence, 1–5–1 with the fourth over the dominant.

Bass motion

Returning Bass Suspension

The bass holds its note while the harmony changes above it (figured 52), falls a half step to the leading tone with 5̸, and comes back: 1–7–1. Blavet uses it under the second bar of the theme, in A minor (m. 14), D minor (m. 16) and G minor (m. 17). In partimento teaching it is the suspension that lies in the bass and returns; Gjerdingen’s Comma is the same 7–1.

Schema

Monte

Three bars, each starting on a leading tone in the bass with 5̸ and resolving through the dominant with 7♯: to G minor, to A, to D minor (mm. 22–24), the fourth degree, the fifth and the tonic, which is the three-step Monte of a minor key. The flute plays one idea a step higher each time, with a tied note that turns into a dissonance on each downbeat.

Cadence

Passo Indietro, the Alto Cadence

The dominant steps back through 4 to 3 with a 6: Gjerdingen’s Passo indietro, and in partimento teaching the alto cadence, the weakest close. In mm. 1–2, G carries ♯4 and falls to F with 6 before the first close; in m. 25, A–G–F follows the half cadence and leads into the closing sequence.

Form

Weak Close, Then Petite Reprise

The first time, the bass reaches the tonic through its leading tone, C♯ (mm. 28–29), so the cadence does not fully close. The last three bars then return half a bar later in the measure, and the bass ends 5–5–1 (mm. 29–32).

Performing the Flute Part

Blavet’s print carries its own performance layer: slurs placed with care, trills, small notes and fifteen breath marks. The blue marks above the flute staff show those breaths (’), the one we add ((’), in parentheses), and Quantz’s tonguing and dynamics for the passage ours. The red marks above them are the printed marks of Toshio Takahashi’s revision for the Suzuki Flute School. Hover any mark for its source; both layers can be switched off above the score.

What Blavet Prints source

  • h: a breath (haleine), Blavet’s own sign according to Walter Kolneder’s preface to the Bärenreiter edition. Fifteen in 32 bars, about one every two bars: mm. 4, 5, 6, 8, 11, 14, 16, 19, 21, 22, 23, 25, 26, 29, 30. Fourteen stand directly before an upbeat eighth or two upbeat sixteenths; the one in m. 19 follows a cadence note.
  • Slurs on sixteenths come in three shapes. Two sixteenths slurred into an eighth (the theme: mm. 1, 13, 15; also mm. 17, 18, 20). Four sixteenths with the first tongued and the next three slurred, where the three turn around one note (mm. 5, 6, 8–10, 17, 18, 20, 22, 23, 26–29, 31). Three rising steps slurred (m. 19). Pairs: rising thirds (m. 11) and the two upbeat sixteenths of mm. 22 and 23.
  • No slur on scale runs (mm. 7, 24, 25), on leaping eighths, or on the dotted cadence figures.
  • +: a trill (tremblement), 26 in all: on the dotted notes of the cadence figures, on dotted quarters, on single eighths, and once on a sixteenth (m. 2). Four have their two-note ending written in small notes and slurred into the next beat (mm. 5, 9, 27, 30).
  • Small notes on the beat before the long notes of the sequence (mm. 8, 9, 26, 27, 29, 30): each repeats the note before it.
  • No dynamics in this movement.

Rules We Apply Quantz

Paraphrased from the Versuch (1752), chapters VI, VII, VIII, IX and XII, in Reilly’s translation.

  • Slurs as marked. Notes that are to be slurred are played as they are marked, and notes that are to be tongued are not slurred (XII §21, p. 133). Under a slur only the first note is tipped (VI.i §10, p. 74).
  • The first note of a quick figure is stressed and held a little, which also keeps the tempo steady (XII §5, p. 130). Blavet’s slur on notes two to four leaves exactly that note standing alone.
  • Tonguing. Dotted notes take ti-ri (VI.ii §3, p. 76). Passage-work without dots takes di-ri after a first ti; leaping eighths take ti, stepwise ones di (VI.ii §7, p. 77). If passage-work is too quick for di-ri, slur the first two of each four notes and tongue the others (VI.ii §8, p. 78). The double tongue is for the very quickest passages only (VI.iii §1, p. 79).
  • Eighths and quarters. Where the passage-work is in sixteenths, eighths are tipped briefly and quarters are sung (XII §22, p. 133).
  • Trills in gay music are played happily and quickly, and half-shakes may go on descending steps in passage-work (XII §14, p. 132). A shake begins with its appoggiatura and ends with two little notes (IX §7, p. 103).
  • Breath: between a long note and a short one (VII §3, p. 87); after a cadence, before a new idea (§4, p. 88); wide leaps suit it (§8, p. 89). All fifteen of Blavet’s marks fit.
  • Repeated ideas alternate forte and piano (XII §23, p. 133); dissonances are played more strongly than consonances (XVII.vi §12, p. 254).

  • Fl. Q. changes nothing in Blavet’s notes, slurs, trills and small notes. His h becomes a modern breath mark, one breath is added at the repeat sign (m. 12), and the six small notes before long notes get an accent.
  • Fl. T.: Takahashi’s slurs, dots, breath marks, accents, tenuto marks and trill signs, and the places where his notes differ from the print: the long note of m. 4, the rest in m. 12, and the trill endings he leaves out. His dynamics are listed in the red layer of the Score view.
  • Takahashi keeps fourteen of Blavet’s fifteen breaths exactly, moves one (m. 11) and adds two (mm. 2, 24). The main difference is the slurring of the sixteenths: where Blavet tongues the first note of a group and slurs the other three, Takahashi slurs the first two and puts dots on the last two. That is the pattern Quantz recommends most for fast passage-work without marks, so the two editions give the same notes two different characters.

Quantz’s Rules and This Movement

Rules Quantz

Paraphrased from the Versuch, XI (good execution), XII (the manner of playing the Allegro) and XVII.vii (tempo), Reilly pp. 123–124, 129–134 and 284–286.

  • Tempo by the pulse. Quantz measures tempo by a pulse of about 80 beats a minute and sorts pieces into classes (XVII.vii §§49–51, pp. 284–286). In common time an Allegro assai has one pulse beat to the half note and an Allegretto one to the quarter; a plain Allegro lies between them, a pulse beat for every three eighths. That gives quarter = 120, where the page starts.
  • French moderation. Quantz remarks that French musicians of his day kept a moderate speed in lively pieces (XVII.vii §50, p. 285, note).
  • The Allegro’s character is gaiety and liveliness (XII §3, p. 129). Its passage-work is played roundly and distinctly, with a tongue that is now firm, now gentle (§4, p. 129).
  • Never hurry. The tempo is set by the hardest passage (XII §8, p. 130); what moves the listener is expression, not speed (§11, p. 131).
  • Gay and flattering. Gaiety shows in short notes and a lively tongue, flattery in slurred notes that move by step (XII §24, pp. 133–134). Low notes after a deep leap are played more strongly (§17, p. 132), and a long note among quick ones is given weight (§19, p. 132).
  • Variations. Few are allowed in an Allegro, and none before the repetition; a solo whose Allegro has two reprises suits them best (XII §27, p. 134).
  • Unequal notes. In a moderate tempo the quickest notes are played a little unequally, but not quick passage-work in a very fast tempo, where only the first of every four notes gets length and strength; and never under a slur of more than two notes (XI §12, pp. 123–124).

Guidelines and Our Readings guideline ours

  • Why Quantz for a French sonata. The sonata was printed in Paris about twenty years before the Versuch. Quantz knew Blavet in person, but he writes as a Berlin musician of 1752. Where Blavet’s own marks speak (breaths, slurs, trills), they come first; Quantz is used to explain them and to fill the gaps. Hotteterre’s Principes (1707) would be the nearer French witness and has not been read for this page.
  • Blavet’s slurs and Quantz’s stress. Blavet’s commonest slur, on notes two to four of a group, leaves the note on the beat alone, the note Quantz wants stressed and slightly held. Takahashi’s slur on notes one and two joins that note to the next and makes the group lighter and more even. Both are playable; the first is the composer’s.
  • Tempo. The title is a dance name, the tempo word is Allegro. We take the speed from the word and from Quantz’s pulse, and it happens to be Takahashi’s figure too. With Quantz’s remark on French moderation in mind, a little under 120 is as defensible; the tempo slider is there to try it.
  • Unequal sixteenths. At this speed Quantz’s rule leaves only a slight length on the first of every four sixteenths, and most of the sixteenths are under Blavet’s slurs. The page plays them evenly.
  • Repeats. Blavet prints a repeat sign for each half. The page plays each half once. Quantz’s advice for such a movement is to keep the first time plain and to vary, if at all, in the repetition (XII §27, p. 134).
  • Names. Do–Re–Mi, Comma and Passo indietro are Robert Gjerdingen’s terms, from Music in the Galant Style (2007); Fonte and Monte are Joseph Riepel’s (1755), taken up by Gjerdingen. The bass that falls a third and rises a step, the bass suspension, the rule of the octave and the long, compound, simple and alto cadences belong to Neapolitan partimento teaching (Durante’s and Fenaroli’s rules). Petite reprise is the French term for a repeat of the closing bars; Blavet does not mark it here, he writes it out.
  • Where the pattern names come from. They follow the partimento catalog made for this site, 96 patterns that were checked there against Fenaroli, Durante, Sanguinetti’s The Art of Partimento and Gjerdingen. Those books were not read again for this page; each pattern was matched to its entry in the catalog by its bass degrees and figures.
  • Patterns that fit loosely. A partimento ties the bass note of a bass suspension; Blavet strikes it again (mm. 14, 16, 17). In “third down, second up” each bass note is spun out into four sixteenths, so the pattern shows only in the first note of each beat. The Fonte’s two steps are a bar each, with a bass that goes through 7 and 5 to 1 and then climbs, where the short form has only 7–1. The long cadence of m. 21 has a triad on the fourth degree, and those of mm. 10–12 and 32 start from 1, not 3.
  • Cadence types (PAC, IAC, HC) are modern labels. We call a cadence perfect only when the bass goes 5–1 in root position and the flute ends on 1 as the tonic arrives.

Notes on the Reading

The Score

Two staves as in the Paris print: flute and figured bass. Trills are shown with Blavet’s +, breaths with his h, and the small notes as he places them, including the two that end a trill. The dash in the figures holds the harmony of the note before; a slashed 5 is a diminished fifth. Accidentals follow modern use and hold to the end of the bar, where the print repeats them. The bar with the repeat sign (m. 12) is engraved as one bar with the sign inside it, so the bar numbers stay those of a modern edition.

Readings we are less sure of: the first two slurs of m. 1, where the flourish of the title covers the notes (taken from m. 15, the same bar, which prints all three); the stack of figures on the first bass note of m. 4 (read 6, 3, ♭; it could be 6, 5, ♭); the sharp printed above, not before, the trilled C♯ of m. 4; the two small notes of m. 5, read F♯–G; the first slur of m. 11, a short hook under A–C, and the exact place of the h in that bar; the plain 5 in mm. 23 and 26, where the parallel bars 22 and 29 print a slashed 5; the figure over the rest in m. 20; and the last bass note of m. 28, read C♯.
