II. Arioso e Mesto

The slow movement in the tonic minor, for flute and muted strings. Three short ritornellos frame two solos; the opening tutti has no basso at all, and the viola carries its lament bass. Below: the form, every cadence, the partimento patterns (many of them in the violins or viola while the basso rests) and a performance layer for the flute after Quantz’s chapter on the Adagio, all on a playable full score.

  • 3 ritornellos, 2 solos
  • Keys G minor → B♭ → C minor → G minor

Bass figure

Lament Bass

G–F–E♭–D, the descending minor tetrachord, closed by C–C♯–D. Unfigured in the viola (m. 5), figured in the basso (mm. 13–14, with 4♮2 on F leaning toward C minor), in C minor in Ritornello 2 (m. 35), and fortissimo with the whole band to open the last tutti (m. 65).

Rule of the octave

Rising 5–♮6–♯7–1

D–E♮–F♯–G with the raised degrees of the ascending minor scale: in the viola (mm. 3–4), in the basso when it enters (mm. 11–13), in C minor (mm. 33–35), and at the very end (mm. 68–69).

Schema

Passo Indietro

4 with 42 stepping back to 3 with 6 (V42–I⁶): in B♭ in Violin II (m. 21), in C minor in the viola (mm. 27–28), at the head of each descent of mm. 25–26, and in G minor before the cadenza (m. 63).

Schema

Indugio

Violin II lingers on E♭, 4 of B♭, for most of a bar, then moves E♮–F (♯4–5) into the cadence (mm. 17–19): the bass delays the dominant.

Schema

Monte

The forte-piano cadence pair of mm. 19–20 in B♭ comes back a step higher in C minor (mm. 23–24), note for note in flute, Violin I and pizzicato basso: a rising Monte with two bars between its steps.

Rule of the octave

Descending Octave

4 with 4♮2, 3 with 6, 2 with 6♮, 1: in C minor, then a fifth lower in F minor (mm. 25–26); and 5 to 1 in G minor with 64♯3 on 4 and 6♯ on 2 (m. 49).

Motive

Cadence Motive

An arpeggiated V–i in sixteenths (D–F♯–D–G–D–B♭–G), first in the viola (m. 7), then pizzicato in the basso at the cadences of both solos, in the tutti interruption (mm. 56–57) and with strokes in the last tutti (mm. 66–67).

Sequence

Circle of Fifths

C–F⁷, B♭–E♭⁷, A–D⁷, G (mm. 59–62), with an appoggiatura in the flute on each seventh chord: the last approach to the cadenza. A shorter chain, C–F–B♭, in the viola leads from C minor to B♭ (mm. 39–41).

Cadence

Cadential Six-Four

64 → 5♯ on D closes every main section: C minor (m. 30), G minor (m. 55, with a major third on arrival), the cadenza (m. 64) and the end (m. 69).

Rules We Apply Quantz

Paraphrased from the Versuch, chapters VI (tonguing), VII (breathing), VIII (appoggiaturas), XIV (the Adagio), XV (cadenzas) and XVII (dissonances), Reilly pp. 71–185 and 254–255.

  • Breath ’: from a long note, never from a short one (p. 87); before a new idea, and before a held note, splitting a tie if needed (p. 88); a wide leap is the best place (p. 89). At rests, hold the last note a little, let it die away, and begin again with renewed force (XIV §12, p. 166).
  • di: in the Adagio every note is caressed, never tipped harshly (XIV §13, p. 166); di is the soft tongue for sustained notes (pp. 71–72). Dotted notes are swelled up to the dot and slurred softly to the next note (XIV §17, p. 167).
  • Messa di voce: a note of half a bar or more starts pianissimo, swells to the middle and fades, with a flattement on the nearest open hole (XIV §10, pp. 165–166). The flute’s G of mm. 56–58 is the longest.
  • Light and shade: piano and forte alternate, but swell and fade imperceptibly, never with vehemence (XIV §9, p. 165); each note has its own piano and forte (§11, p. 166).
  • Accent: appoggiaturas take half the main note (p. 95) and, like suspensions, are louder than their resolution (pp. 254–255).
  • Graces: in a very melancholy Adagio, slurred notes rather than leaps and shakes, without banning shakes (XIV §8, p. 165). Cadenza: on a wind instrument, in one breath (XV §17, p. 185).

Takahashi’s Edition comparison

Printed marks only (page 9 of the flute part); the handwritten notes in the copy are left out. Breitkopf, the source of our score, prints the slurs, strokes, appoggiaturas, trills and the f and p of the echo bars, and Takahashi keeps nearly all of them.

They agree on most breaths (14 of Takahashi’s 18 at the same place, 3 more one note apart, where he breathes after the first note of a forte bar and we just before it), on the written-out trill endings and on a soft start to the long G. They differ on one breath (m. 39 against m. 40), on the trill Takahashi adds to the long G, and on the [f] at the flute’s entry in m. 36.

Quantz’s Rules and This Movement

Rules Quantz

Paraphrased from Versuch XVIII §§35–37, the Adagio of a concerto (Reilly pp. 313–314), with XVII.vii on tempo, XV on cadenzas and XIV on the Adagio.

  • The Adagio differs from the first Allegro in rhythm, meter and key; after a major-key Allegro, the tonic minor is one of the natural choices (§35, p. 313). Here: common time and G major, then 3/4 and G minor.
  • Ritornellos and solos as short as possible (§37, rule 1). Here: tutti of 8½, 5½ and 6 bars; two solos.
  • A ritornello that is melodious, harmonious and expressive (rule 2): muted strings, a sighing head and a lament bass.
  • A solo melody that allows graces but pleases without them (rule 3), and that moves like a melody with words (rule 5). Here the flute takes over the ritornello melody itself (mm. 9–16).
  • The solo alternates with the tutti, and parts of the ritornello come back (rules 4 and 6): Ritornello 2 enters on the flute’s cadence, and the tutti interrupts the flute’s held note (mm. 56–57).
  • Not too many keys (rule 7). Here: only III and iv.
  • Plain accompaniment in the solos (rule 8): repeated eighths in the violins, a pizzicato basso, often no basso at all.
  • An epithet that tells the tempo and the sentiment (rule 9). Quantz puts Arioso in his third tempo class (Adagio cantabile) and Mesto in the fourth (Adagio assai) (XVII.vii §49, p. 284). In 3/4, Mesto gives two pulse beats to each quarter note (§51, pp. 286–287), so the page starts at quarter = 40; use the slider if you read Arioso as the governing word.
  • Cadenzas belong in pathetic and slow pieces or serious quick ones (XV §4, p. 180), on the fifth of the key (XV §1, p. 179): m. 64.

Guidelines and Our Readings guideline ours

  • Pattern names (Passo indietro, Indugio, Monte) are Robert Gjerdingen’s, from Music in the Galant Style (2007), applied by us. The rule of the octave and the lament bass are partimento and modern terms; we apply them from the bass line and the printed figures.
  • Bass in the upper strings. The whole first ritornello, most of the second, and the Indugio (mm. 17–18), two Passi indietro (mm. 21, 27–28) and the fifths of mm. 39–41 lie in the viola or Violin II while the basso rests, so their brackets sit under those staves.
  • A Monte with a gap. The cadence pairs of mm. 19–20 (B♭) and 23–24 (C minor) are the same music a step apart; reading them as one Monte across the inserted mm. 21–22 is ours.
  • Cadence types (PAC, IAC, HC) are modern labels. We call a cadence perfect only when the bass goes 5–1 in root position and the melody ends on 1.
  • A ritornello without basso. The basso is silent through the whole first ritornello and enters, muted, only in m. 11, against Quantz’s usual tutti texture. We read it as part of the mesto color.
  • The closing tutti uses the dotted figure of the pizzicato interruption over the lament bass, then the flute’s cadence of mm. 47–48. Quantz asks for parts of the ritornello inside the solos (rule 6); here the borrowing also runs the other way.
