Quantz on Articulation, Phrasing and Breath

What Johann Joachim Quantz tells a flute player about tonguing, slurs, strokes and dots, breathing, and shaping a phrase, gathered from his Versuch (1752) with a page reference for every rule. A last section carries the same rules to the keyboard.

Every rule is paraphrased from Edward R. Reilly’s translation, On Playing the Flute (1966), re-read page by page for this guide. References give chapter, paragraph and Reilly’s printed page. Quantz marks a rule of his; C. P. E. Bach a rule from the Essay (trans. Mitchell, 1949); Ours an inference or practical suggestion of ours. Note values use US names: Reilly’s quaver is an eighth, semiquaver a sixteenth, crotchet a quarter.

The Three Tonguings

Quantz teaches the tongue through syllables spoken while blowing. There are three kinds: the single tongue ti or di, the compound tiri, and the double tongue did’ll VI §2, p. 71. In the diagrams below each box is one note; a blue outline marks a “good” (stressed) note; a dashed box is a note slurred to the one before, where the tongue rests.

The notated examples are ours: short figures in G major written to show each rule, with Quantz’s syllables under the notes. His own figures (Tables III–V) are in the Reilly volume beside the paragraphs cited. Ours

1. Ti and Di, the Single Tongue

  • Ti is for short, equal, lively, quick notes. Di is for slow melody, and also for cheerful melody as long as it stays pleasing and sustained. In an Adagio it is always di, except on dotted notes, which take ti. VI i §1, pp. 71–72
  • How they differ in the mouth. In ti the tip of the tongue springs straight back to the palate and stops the wind, which makes the note short. In di the tongue stays free in the middle of the mouth so the wind keeps feeding the tone. The sound comes from releasing the stopped wind, not from a blow of the tongue. VI i §2, §5–6, pp. 72–73
  • Leaps and steps. Eighths that leap in an Allegro take ti. Notes that then move by step, whether eighths, quarters or halves, take di. Quarters with strokes over them keep ti. VI i §7, p. 73
  • Many shades between. There is more than one degree between a firm and a gentle stroke, just as there are shades between black and white; the tongue must be supple enough to give each note what its nature asks. VI i §12, p. 75
  • The room matters. In a large, resonant place with listeners far off, tongue with more force and sharpness, above all on repeated notes. VI i §13, p. 75
  • Register. Place the stroke further back on the palate for low notes and move it forward as the line rises; from the highest C the tongue is straight, between the teeth. VI i §4, p. 72
  • Its limit. In quick passage-work the single tongue makes every note alike, and taste wants them a little unequal. That is the job of tiri and did’ll. VI i §9, p. 74

2. Tiri, for Dotted Notes and Moderate Passage-Work

  • The accent of the word falls on ri: ti is short, ri long. So ri goes on the good notes (1 and 3 of four sixteenths) and ti on the passing notes (2 and 4). VI ii §4, p. 76
  • You cannot begin with ri, so the first two notes both take ti; then ti ri runs on until the figure changes or a rest comes. VI ii §5, p. 76
  • It is indispensable for dotted notes, which it makes sharper and livelier than any other tonguing can. VI ii §3, p. 76
  • For even notes without dots use the softer di ri, keeping ti only on the first note; ti throughout would be too hard and too unequal at speed. VI ii §7, p. 77

  • Threes. Where a longer note is followed by two quicker ones, and in three equal eighths or triplets, the first two may take di and the third ri. In gigue rhythm (first of three dotted) it is ti ti ri. Always adjust to the leaps. VI ii §6, §9, pp. 77, 79
  • Eighths after sixteenths. Leaping eighths take ti, stepwise ones di, as with the single tongue. VI ii §7, p. 77
Correction to Our Earlier PagesThe concerto pages label the recommended pattern “ti‿ti ri ti”. Quantz’s syllables for it are ti (slur) ri ti: the second note has no syllable because the tongue rests. The engraved slurs on those pages are right; only the label is loose.

3. Did’ll, the Double Tongue

  • Only for the very fastest passage-work. The second syllable has no vowel and is made with the middle of the tongue, not the tip. VI iii §1–2, p. 79
  • It is the opposite of tiri: the accent falls on the first syllable, di, which always sits on the good note. VI iii §3, p. 80
  • Hold the di note a little and make the d’ll note slightly shorter, so the tongue does not run ahead of the fingers. The first note of each figure of three, four or six is always held a little. VI iii §5, §15, pp. 80–81, 84
  • When to break the pattern. After a rest in place of the first note, the next two notes take ti. Repeated pitches take ti on each. A last note that leaps up can take ti. Wide leaps, rests and repeated notes are the usual reasons to change. VI iii §8–10, §13, pp. 81–82
  • After a tie or a dot. If the first quick note is tied to a long note or replaced by a dot, start the group with a breath from the chest, hi, in place of di. VI iii §11, p. 82
  • Triplets. did’ll di on each three; if the second note makes a very large leap down, di did’ll. VI iii §14, p. 84
  • The test. Repeated quick notes show whether the second syllable is as sharp as the first; if it is not, runs will never sparkle. VI iii §16, p. 85

Slurs, Strokes and Dots

  • Follow the composer’s marks. Play slurred notes as marked, since a particular expression is often meant by them, and do not slur notes that need the tongue. Slurring what should be articulated, or articulating what should be slurred, is a basic fault; notes must never sound stuck together. XI §10, p. 122; XII §21, p. 133
  • Slur. Only the note the slur starts on is tongued, normally with di; the tongue does nothing for the rest. If the note before the slur carries a stroke, both it and the slur’s first note take ti. VI i §10, p. 74
  • A slur from a weak note to a strong one takes di, or ti in a quick tempo. VI i §10, p. 74
  • Slur over repeated notes. Pulse them with the breath, by chest action, without the tongue. If dots also stand over them, make the pulses much sharper, as if articulated from the chest. VI i §11, p. 75
  • A marked pattern continues. When only the first of several like figures is marked with a slur or with strokes, the rest are played the same way until the kind of note changes. XVII ii §5, p. 217
  • Stroke versus dot. Written for the bow; Reilly’s note at VI i §7 sends flute players here. Notes with strokes are fully detached and sound about half their value; a single stroke before quicker notes also means an accent. Notes with dots are short but sustained, not lifted. Under a slur, dots mean one bow with a pressure on each note; strokes under a slur mean each note attacked sharply. XVII ii §12, p. 223; §27, p. 232
  • Tempo changes the length. Strokes are not shortened as much in an Adagio as in an Allegro, or they sound dry. Running eighths in an Allegro and sixteenths in an Allegretto are very short but not lifted. XVII ii §27, p. 232
  • Strokes belong mostly to the Allegro. They appear more often there than in the Adagio. XVII ii §12, p. 223
Side Note: What This Means for Modern Signs OursQuantz’s stroke is close to today’s wedge or staccatissimo, his dot to a light staccato, and dots under a slur to portato. A modern editor’s wedge in a slow movement therefore asks for more bite than Quantz’s Adagio allows (XIV §13).

Good and Bad Notes

Behind every tonguing pattern lies one idea: principal (“good”) notes are brought out more than passing (“bad”) ones XI §12, p. 123.

  • Slight inequality. The fastest notes of a piece in moderate tempo, and in an Adagio too, are played a little unequally: notes 1, 3, 5, 7 slightly longer and stronger than 2, 4, 6, 8, but never as much as if dotted. XI §12, p. 123
  • Which notes count as fastest. The quarter in 3/2, the eighth in 3/4 and alla breve, the sixteenth in 3/8, the sixteenth or thirty-second in 2/4 and common time, unless still quicker notes are mixed in. XI §12, p. 123
  • Exceptions, played equally. Very fast passage-work, where only the first of each four gets length and weight; notes with strokes or dots; repeated notes on one pitch; notes under a slur of more than two (four, six, eight); eighths in gigues. XI §12, pp. 123–124
  • Hold the first note of a quick figure. Stressing and slightly holding it keeps the fingers from rushing and keeps tongue and fingers together. XII §5, p. 130; X §9, p. 112
  • After a short rest on the beat, wait half as long again as the rest seems to last, because the note that follows is a passing note and must be shorter. XII §12, p. 131
  • Triplets stay round and equal; the first may be held slightly, the first two never hurried. XII §10, p. 131

The Allegro

“Allegro” here means every lively piece, from Allegretto to Prestissimo. Its character is gaiety and liveliness XII §1, §3, p. 129.

  • Passage-work must be round, correct, distinct, lively and articulated; the tongue tips firmly at one time and gently at another, as the kind of note requires. XII §4, p. 129
  • Short eighths, sung quarters. In an Allegro assai whose fastest notes are sixteenths, tip the eighths briefly and play the quarters singing and sustained. Short notes mean the hard ti, singing notes the soft di, “once and for all”. XII §22, p. 133
  • Rising principal notes with falling passing notes: hold and stress the principal notes; the passing ones may be slurred gently to them. XII §16, p. 132
  • Deep leaps. The deeper the leap, the stronger the low note, because it is a principal note of the harmony and the flute’s low register is less penetrating. XII §17, p. 132
  • Long notes are swelled and diminished; the quick notes after them are set off by a lively delivery. A long note that suddenly interrupts quick ones gets special emphasis. XII §18–19, p. 132
  • Singing notes amid passage-work must not be hurried; when slow singing notes follow quick ones, cool the fire at once. XII §7, §20, pp. 130, 132
  • The main subject must sound different from the side ideas each time it returns, through the liveliness of tongue, chest and lips and through piano and forte. In repetitions, alternate piano and forte. XII §23, p. 133
  • Tempo follows the hardest passage, and expression, not speed, is the goal: a machine could play faster and would move nobody. XII §8, §11, pp. 130–131
  • Small graces. Half-shakes on the first or third of descending stepwise notes and battements on rising ones add sparkle, if not overused. XII §14, p. 132

The Adagio

An Adagio should resemble a flattering petition; what does not come from the heart does not easily reach it XIV §5, p. 163.

  • No harsh tonguing. Every note is caressed; never tip harshly, unless the composer wants a few short notes to wake the listener. XIV §13, p. 166
  • Messa di voce. On a note of a half or whole bar, tip gently, begin pianissimo, swell to the middle and diminish to the end, with a finger vibrato (flattement) on the nearest open hole. XIV §10, pp. 165–166
  • Every note has its own piano and forte as far as time allows; where it does not, shape groups of notes so that some are louder and some softer, by the breath from the chest. XIV §11, p. 166
  • Do not clip the note before a rest. Hold it a little over its value and let it fade, then begin again with new strength, unless the bass has singing notes that fill the gap. XIV §12, p. 166
  • Very melancholy Adagios take slurred notes more than leaps or shakes, though not without any shakes at all. XIV §8, p. 165
  • Piano and forte are the light and shade of the Adagio; swell and diminish smoothly instead of jumping between them. XIV §9, p. 165
  • Kinds of slow piece differ. Cantabile, Arioso, Affettuoso, Andante, Largo and Larghetto are not played like a pathetic Adagio. An Adagio spiritoso has more notes articulated than slurred. A Siciliana is played very simply, with some slurred sixteenths and appoggiaturas and almost no shakes. XIV §7, §18, §22, pp. 164–168
  • Slurs in slow sixteenths. Of each two, the first is heavier in length and volume than the second. Slurred notes must not sound detached unless dots stand under the slur. XVII ii §12, p. 223

Dotted Notes

  • The short note is very short. After a dotted eighth, sixteenth or thirty-second, play the short note as short as possible in any tempo, so the dot is held longer than written. V §21, p. 67
  • Against triplets the short note comes after the third triplet note, not with it. V §22, p. 68
  • Reversed dotting (short–long). The shorter the first note, the livelier and bolder the effect. V §23, pp. 68–69
  • Tonguing: ti ri, the sharpest way to speak them. VI ii §3, p. 76
  • In the Allegro dotted notes express majesty: attack them sharply, hold the dots long, make the following notes very short. XII §24, p. 133
  • In a Grave swell the dotted note up to the dot and, if the interval is small, slur softly and briefly to the next note; across very large leaps tongue each note. XIV §17, p. 167
  • A slur over a dotted pair in the Adagio: the short note is not attacked but slurred on with a diminuendo. XVII ii §13, p. 224

Appoggiaturas and Shakes

  • Appoggiatura. Tip it gently, let it swell if time allows, and slur the main note to it a little more softly (the Abzug). VIII §4, p. 93
  • Its syllable matches the note before it, firm or gentle. VI i §7, p. 73
  • Separate it from the note before, above all when both are on the same pitch: the tongue springs back at once so the earlier note is shortened and the appoggiatura is heard clearly. VI i §8, pp. 73–74
  • Accented and passing kinds. Accented appoggiaturas fall on the beat. Passing ones, between notes descending in thirds, are taken before the beat in the French manner: the little note is tongued and the main note slurred to it, with a flattering expression. VIII §5–6, pp. 93–94
  • Length. Half of a plain note, two thirds of a dotted one. VIII, p. 95
  • Shake. Every shake begins with its appoggiatura and ends with a two-note termination, even where only a plain note is written. The appoggiatura is always tongued, long or short; the shake and its termination are slurred to it. IX §7–8, pp. 103–104

Breath and Phrasing

Breathing is punctuation. To split notes that belong together is like taking a breath in the middle of a word VII §1, p. 87.

  • Between a long note and a short one. Never after a short note, still less after the last note of a bar. Very fast stepwise triplets are the exception; where there is a leap of a third or more, breathe inside the leap. VII §3, p. 87
  • Before a new idea. After a cadence, before a restatement of the main subject, and before an upbeat that opens a phrase, so that the end of one thought is separated from the start of the next. VII §4, p. 88
  • Before a long held note, even if a short note precedes it. A tied note may be split into two notes on the same pitch to breathe between them. VII §5, p. 88
  • In running sixteenths: best in the first quarter of the bar, after its first note, unless the first four move by step and leaps follow. Wide intervals are the best places. VII §8, p. 89
  • Hide the breath. Make the note before it very short, breathe quickly and shallowly, and slightly rush the next two or three notes so the beat is not delayed and no note is dropped. Breathe early and calmly instead of waiting until the last moment. VII §6, pp. 88–89
  • Only when needed. The marked places are options, not obligations. In front of an audience expect about half the breath you have alone. VII §7, §10, pp. 89–90
  • Phrase sense comes first. Learn what makes a musical thought and what must hang together: do not separate what belongs together, and do not join passages that hold more than one phrase. VII §10, p. 90
  • Separate ideas even without a rest, especially when the last note of one phrase and the first of the next share a pitch. XI §10, p. 122
  • Cadenzas for a wind instrument must fit in one breath. XV §17, p. 185

Passion and Touch

Articulation is how the passion of each idea is shown, and the passion can change from bar to bar XI §15–16, pp. 124–126.

  • Sustained and flattering notes are slurred to one another; gay and leaping notes are detached. XI §11, p. 123
  • Flattering ideas in the Adagio must not be attacked too rudely, and joyful ideas in the Allegro must not be dragged, slurred or attacked too gently. XI §15, p. 125

At the Keyboard

The keyboard rules of the period run parallel to the flute rules, so they sit here as a section and not on a page of their own. Quantz says little about solo keyboard touch; C. P. E. Bach’s Essay (1753), written at the same court a year later, fills the gap and agrees with him on the main points. What differs is the means: the keyboard cannot swell a note or breathe, so length of touch has to do the work that wind does on the flute.

  • Quantz on keyboard touch. Each finger strikes with equal force and weight and gives the string time to sound. In running passages do not lift the fingers straight up: draw the fingertips back along the key until they glide off, which Quantz’s note gives as J. S. Bach’s manner. XVII vi §18, pp. 259–260
  • Which notes to bring out. Long notes among quick ones, the notes that begin a main subject, and above all dissonances. XVII vi §10–12, pp. 253–254
  • Neither glued nor burning. Do not hold notes too long or leave them too soon; the middle way is best, though every touch has its use. C. P. E. Bach III §6, p. 149
  • Where the keyboard differs most. It cannot sustain, swell or diminish a tone. Broken chords, trills and mordents keep a long note alive in place of the flute’s swell. C. P. E. Bach III §7, pp. 149–150
  • Breath at the keyboard. Ours Neither writer gives a keyboard breathing rule. Quantz’s breath places still mark the phrase: at each one, shorten the note before it and re-start the next idea clearly, as he asks even where no rest is written (XI §10, p. 122).
  • Tonguing as a model for touch. Ours Read ti as detached, di as the ordinary half-length touch, a slur as full-length legato with a light first-note pressure, and the 1–2 slur of four sixteenths as slur two, detach two. Quantz’s slight inequality of good and bad notes has no keyboard rule in C. P. E. Bach; treat it as optional color, not as a rule.
Side Note: The Bow as a Third ModelQuantz himself equates tongue and bow VI §1, p. 71; XVII ii §3, p. 216. His bowing chapter gives the stroke for each tempo: Allegro and Presto a lively, light, short, detached stroke; Allegretto a rather heavier but still lively one; Arioso and Cantabile a light, quiet stroke; Maestoso and Adagio spiritoso a heavier, sharper one; Adagio assai and Lento the longest, calmest stroke XVII ii §26, pp. 230–231. A flute or keyboard player can read the same list as a guide to weight of tongue or touch Ours.

A Working Method

Ours A practical order for marking up a flute part with Quantz’s rules. Each step points back to the rule it uses.

  1. Find the character. Read the tempo word, key, intervals and dissonances, idea by idea (Passion and Touch). Decide which ideas are gay, majestic, bold or flattering.
  2. Keep every printed slur, stroke and dot, and extend a marked pattern through like figures (Slurs, Strokes and Dots).
  3. Find the fastest note value and set the levels around it: in an Allegro assai with sixteenths, eighths short and quarters sung (The Allegro).
  4. Choose the tonguing for the passage-work by speed: ti ri or di ri, then the 1–2 slur, then did’ll. Break the pattern at rests, repeated notes and wide leaps (The Three Tonguings).
  5. Mark leaps and steps in the eighths: ti on leaps, di on steps.
  6. Mark the breaths at cadences and before new ideas first, then inside long runs after the first note of a group or at a leap (Breath and Phrasing).
  7. Shape the long notes and dissonances: swell and diminish, stress the low note of a deep leap, play dissonances stronger.
  8. In a slow movement, soften everything: di throughout, slurs first heavy then light, the note before a rest held and faded (The Adagio).
