Fugue in E Minor, BWV 533 Literal

The organ fugue that Scott Roberts (2004, chapter 5) uses to show a palindrome as a notable property. The eight entries span 2, 3½, 3½, 5 | 5, 3½, 3½, 2 bars. 5 never gets past 6, the subject climbs 1–2–3 and never comes back down, and nothing is resolved by the end, so the fugue is literal. Roberts offers two readings of the same facts: frustration, or confidence and stability.

  • 36 bars, 4/4
  • 4 voices
  • 8 entries plus a fifth entry in the exposition
  • 2 digressions
  • 6 notable properties, none reconciled

The Six Steps

Roberts ran the steps in order but writes this chapter as a narrative. The cards sort his findings back into the six steps; which finding belongs to which step is our reading ours.

  1. STEP 1

    Identify the Traditional Fugue Elements

    Mark every entry, countersubject, codetta, episode and coda, with voice, key and devices.

    • Exposition: tenor (E minor), bass (B minor), soprano, alto, then a fifth entry in the pedal under a cadential extension (m. 12).
    • The first authentic cadence, m. 15.1, ends the exposition.
    • Four more entries, tenor, soprano, alto and bass, with two digressions between the groups.
  2. STEP 2

    Find the Notable Properties

    Look in the subject and exposition for linear lines, patterns, suspended degrees, contrasts and structural units.

    • Static, then active: the subject’s halves, the reverse of BWV 578.
    • Repetition: each half repeats one short figure.
    • Suspended 5, no descent: 5 is left hanging and 1–2–3 rises without a 3–2–1 to answer it.
  3. STEP 3

    Look for Patterns in the Sections

    Measure the sections: do they grow, shrink, mirror or form simple ratios?

    The spans between entries form a palindrome: 2, 3½, 3½, 5 | 5, 3½, 3½, 2. They grow by 1½ bars twice and shrink by the same amount. Minor codettas such as mm. 5–6 are left out, as melodic links were in BWV 578.

  4. STEP 4

    Test Other Segmentations

    Find phenomena that justify other cuts: cadences, texture, rhythm, new material.

    m. 15: a perfect authentic cadence, fewer voices and a new rhythm. m. 19: more voices, another rhythm and a new entry. m. 31: new rhythm and new melodic ideas. The ½-bar cadential extension in m. 35 lies outside the palindrome, so Roberts joins it to Digression 2.

  5. STEP 5

    Repeat the Pattern Search

    Look for patterns again in the new segmentation.

    The 1½-bar steps give 3:2, the perfect fifth. The digressions, 4 bars and 2½ + ½ = 3 bars, give 4:3, the perfect fourth. Every entry is in the tonic or the dominant, and the subject opens with a fifth, 5 to 1.

  6. STEP 6

    Decide How the Fugue Responds

    A property is reconciled when it returns with the trait that made it notable, now completed. None reconciled: literal.

    Literal. 5 never gets past 6, even in the last attempt in m. 35, and the 1–2–3 ascent is never answered. Only two keys are used and the spans backtrack to where they began. The fugue states a condition; it does not act on it.

Palindrome and Ratios

Table 8 to scale, in bars. Blue bars are entry spans, orange bars are digressions.

The Palindrome Roberts

Read the eight entry spans in order and they mirror: 2, 3½, 3½, 5 | 5, 3½, 3½, 2. The music sets out from a point and then backtracks to it. In the first reading this is frustration: the goal of the journey is never reached.

3:2 and 4:3 Roberts

The spans change by 1½ bars, which is 3:2, the ratio of the perfect fifth. Digression 1 lasts 4 bars; Digression 2 lasts 2½, or 3 with the ½-bar extension of m. 35, giving 4:3, the perfect fourth. Roberts calls this correlation speculative, but notes that Bach belonged to the Society of Musical Sciences and knew the interval ratios.

Six Notable Properties

The first three come from the subject and serve both readings. Properties 4 to 6 are added for the second reading. None is reconciled. The buttons scroll the score to the place and play it.

1 · Contrast

A static half, then an active half

Why notable
The first half repeats B in echoes; the second half moves in steady eighths. BWV 578 had the reverse order.
Response
Not reconciled; every entry keeps the same order.
2 · Pattern

Both halves repeat a short figure

Why notable
Repeating without getting anywhere suggests the non-progress that the first reading hears as frustration.
Response
Not reconciled.
3 · Linear lines

Suspended 5 and an ascent with no descent

Why notable
No line runs 5–1 or even 3–1. Again and again 6 blocks 5 from rising: mm. 8, 14, 16–17, 23, 29–33 and the last attempt in m. 35.
Response
Not reconciled; in m. 35 the line reaches 5, touches 6 and falls back to 2–1.
4 · Ratio

3:2 and 4:3 in the structure

Why notable
Entry spans step by 1½ bars (3:2); the digressions are 4 and 3 bars (4:3), the fifth and its inversion.
Response
A structural fact that supports the second reading; there is nothing to resolve.
5 · Keys

Only tonic and dominant

Why notable
Every entry is in E minor or B minor, and the fugue never leaves those keys; hints of G major (mm. 21–22) and A minor (mm. 24–25, 32) stay inside E minor. This may explain why the fugue is so short.
Response
Not reconciled.
6 · Structural unit

A fifth entry in a four-voice exposition

Why notable
After all four voices have entered, the pedal brings the subject once more, in B minor, under the cadential extension of the fourth entry (mm. 12–14).
Response
Not reconciled.

Two Readings, One Verdict

Reading One: Frustration Roberts

The palindrome leaves and then retraces its steps, never reaching a goal. 5 is boxed in by 6 and cannot rise; the ascent 1–2–3 is never matched by a descent. The fugue is easy to play but hard to make sense of because of its stop-and-start manner. Its statement might be “some problems cannot be solved.”

Mm. 33–35 read like a parenthesis: repeated motions from 7 to 1 hold the goal just out of reach of 5, as if mocking it.

Reading Two: Confidence and Stability Roberts

The same boundaries can be heard as a safe enclosure rather than a cage. The perfect fifth and fourth shape the structure, only the two most closely related keys appear, and the subject begins with a fifth. Seen this way, the parenthesis in mm. 33–35 is a future hope: 5 will rise in time, and meanwhile one is content.

Why Both Are Literal Roberts

Neither reading needs the fugue to act. Both describe a state of affairs, frustration or confidence, that calls for no change, so no property is reconciled and the fugue is literal.

Playing the Subject Roberts

The downbeat of the subject is the eighth rest; the two Bs after it are echoes, like a bouncing ball, not an upbeat and a downbeat. Roberts suggests shortening the quarter notes and letting the building carry the sound. Two more reasons: the accent of a mordent falls on its first note, not on the return, and in every accompanied entry the harmony changes halfway through the quarter note (his Table 7).

Ornaments ours

Roberts quotes the Neue Bach-Ausgabe, which has mordents on the subject’s quarter notes and a turn on 5 in m. 35. The Bach-Gesellschaft text used here does not show them, so playback leaves them out rather than guess their shape.

About This Score

Notes and Layout

The notes follow the Bach-Gesellschaft edition (BG 15, ed. Wilhelm Rust, 1867) as typeset by Pierre Pouillon for the Mutopia Project. They were engraved from Peter van Kranenburg’s Humdrum encoding after correcting it in 14 places where the two disagree (below); after that, all 671 notes agree in pitch and position. The corrections also restore the shape of the subject at mm. 11, 14 and 25, which supports them.

Each staff here is one line of the encoding, so the score reads as open score; Bach’s layout has two manual staves and pedal. Line breaks are the engraver’s. Bar numbers agree with Roberts, who quotes the Neue Bach-Ausgabe.

Playback uses recorded samples: the Steinway Grand by default, an Italian harpsichord at A = 415, or a baroque pipe organ at Chorton A = 465 with pedal pipes for the pedal staff.
