Roberts's Fugue Analysis Method

Scott Roberts, Toward a Methodology for the Analysis of Fugue: An Examination of Selected Bach Organ Works (PhD diss., Florida State University, 2004). A six-step method that asks what problem a fugue sets itself at the start, and whether it solves it by the end.

  • 6 steps
  • 2 outcomes: rhetorical or literal
  • Demonstrated on WTC I, Fugue in C Minor
  • Applied to BWV 578, 533, 545, 547
Thesis, paraphrased

The opening material of a fugue holds one or more notable properties in its rhythm, harmony and melody. The fugue may treat them as conflicts and work out solutions from that same material. The formal structure gives evidence for both the conflicts and the solutions. A fugue that resolves its properties is rhetorical; one that does not treat them as challenges is literal.

Roberts, chapter 1. He builds on Daniel Harrison (fugue as rhetoric, conflicts called statūs) and Joel Lester (the opening "core" material and parallel sections).

Two Compositional Approaches

The method ends in one of two labels. Roberts treats them as two ways a fugue can relate to its own opening, not as a ranking.

Rhetorical

The opening sets up musical "challenges." The fugue then argues them out, and each notable property comes back with the very attribute that made it notable, now completed. Roberts calls this being reconciled.

In every rhetorical fugue he studied, the resolutions arrive together, and the fugue ends soon after. Any coda is brief and only rounds off the piece.

Examples: WTC I C minor, BWV 578, BWV 545, BWV 547.

Literal

The features that catch the analyst's eye are presented but never resolved. They persist to the end, so the fugue illustrates a state of affairs instead of arguing toward a goal. "Literal" here means a presentation not meant to persuade.

A mix of reconciled and unreconciled properties (a hybrid) is possible in theory, but Roberts found none.

Example: BWV 533.

The Six Steps

The process is recursive: the significance of a property is only clear once the whole fugue has been considered. Not every analysis needs all six steps. Select a step in the diagram to jump to its card.

Steps 1 to 4 collect evidence. Step 5 sends the analysis back to step 3 when step 4 has produced a better segmentation. Step 6 gives the verdict.

    Worked Example: WTC I, Fugue in C Minor (BWV 847)

    Roberts demonstrates the method on this 31-bar fugue because it is short, has few notable properties, shares them with the organ fugues, and is one of Lester's own examples. Steps 3, 4 and 5 find nothing here, which shows that a step can come up empty.

    Step 1: Traditional Structure (Roberts, Table 3)

    S/AS/AE1S/AE2S/AE3S/AE4S/AE5S/ACoda

    Order only, not to scale. E1 (mm. 5–6) is called a codetta in some texts; Roberts follows Lester's label. E4 inverts E1.

    Step 2: Three Notable Properties, and Step 6: How Each Is Reconciled

    Blocked linear descent

    ^6–^5–^4–^3 in the subject

    Why notable
    Repeated and prominent in every entry, yet it stops on ^3 and never reaches tonic.
    Reconciled by
    A complete descent to ^1 in soprano and bass, mm. 27–29.
    Linear ascent

    Chromatic ^5 → ^1 in Episode 1

    Why notable
    It climbs through every half step of the minor-mode upper hexachord (mm. 5–6, again mm. 17–18) instead of taking the melodic minor scale.
    Reconciled by
    An unhindered ascent up the melodic minor scale from dominant to tonic, mm. 27–29.
    Rhythmic and melodic pattern

    Cells of 4 + 4 + 3 + 3 + 2 eighths

    Why notable
    The subject splits into motives that shrink from 4 to 3 to 2 eighths, which sets up a missing final cell of 1.
    Reconciled by
    4 + 4 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 1 in all three voices, mm. 27–29: the capstone cell of one eighth.

    Drawn to scale in eighth notes from Roberts's cell counts (Figures 8 and 12). Cells are grouped by melodic contour, so a cell of four can be three eighths and a rest.

    Verdict. All three properties resolve together in mm. 27–29, and only a short coda follows. The approach is rhetorical. Roberts takes two lessons from this that hold for every rhetorical organ fugue in the study: the resolutions are concurrent, and once they are done the fugue ends.

    Verdict Tool (Step 6)

    Pick a fugue to load the notable properties Roberts found, with his answer for each. Switch any property to see how the verdict depends on it. Properties marked as support strengthen the reading but are not conflicts in themselves, so they do not count.

      Terms

      Notable property
      A salient feature of the opening, either an anomaly or a trait that is not required of a fugue (for example a head-and-tail subject). Step 2 only lists candidates; later steps decide which matter.
      Core material
      Lester's term for the opening section up to the first parallelism. Roberts widens it beyond subject, answer and countersubject to include structure and Exposition Blocks.
      Integrant
      The element the structure begins and ends with, and whose return is expected. Named after "integral." In a traditional reading it is the same as an entry.
      Digression
      Any secondary element between integrants. In a traditional reading it is the same as an episode. Roberts uses new names because step 4 can segment by other events.
      Blocked ascent or descent
      An incomplete scale line. Descents are complete at ^1; ascents from below ^5 are complete at ^5; ascents from ^5 are complete at ^1.
      Suspended scale degree
      A non-tonic degree that is not linearly connected to other degrees, such as ^5 boxed in by ^6 in BWV 533.
      Exposition Block (EB)
      Several subject and answer entries that act as one integrant. Coined for BWV 547, whose one-bar subject cannot stand alone.
      Reconciled
      A property returns together with the attribute that made it notable, completed: an incomplete descent returns as a complete one.
      Obscured entry
      An entry hidden by voices above and below it, or placed in the middle of a continuing idea. It weakens a structure built on entries and points the analyst elsewhere.

      Checklist for Your Own Analysis

      Roberts's outline, turned into questions. Work through it in order, then go back when a later step changes an earlier answer.

      1. Mark the traditional elements. Where are the subject and answer entries, the end of the exposition, the countersubject, codettas, episodes and coda?
        • Note voice, key and devices (augmentation, diminution, retrograde, inversion, stretto). Middle entries need not be called subject or answer.
      2. List what is unusual in the exposition. Check the six kinds: linear lines, blocked lines, rhythmic or melodic patterns, suspended degrees, contrasting material, structural units.
        • Only list candidates. A subject whose descent stops short is not automatically notable, since the Urlinie descent is expected.
      3. Measure the sections. Label integrants and digressions and write their lengths in bars.
        • Do they grow or shrink steadily? Do they mirror? Do lengths form simple ratios (1:2, 1:3, 1:4) or tuning ratios (1:1, 2:1, 3:2, 4:3, 5:4)?
      4. Ask whether the entry-based structure is the useful one. Is it incoherent because entries overlap or are obscured? Are there rare rhythm changes, rare texture changes, or groups of entries acting as one block?
      5. If step 4 gave a better segmentation, return to step 3 and look for patterns in it.
      6. Follow each property to the end. Does it come back completed? Do the completions arrive together, with the fugue ending soon after?
        • All completed: rhetorical. None: literal. A mix would be a hybrid, which Roberts has not found.

      Rules vs Readings

      Roberts separates his procedure from his interpretations, and flags several readings as speculative himself. This table keeps the two apart.

      TopicMethod (Roberts's rule)Interpretation or caution
      Notable propertiesSix kinds in step 2; accept only properties supported both by the opening and by the whole fugue (chapter 8).Which features count is the analyst's judgment. Step 2 lists candidates only.
      Section ratiosNote simple and tuning ratios between section lengths (step 3).Speculative Roberts calls the BWV 533 ratio reading "admittedly speculative," and for BWV 545 leaves numerology to the reader. Some sections need adjustment (codettas or links ignored) to fit.
      Expressive narrativeNot part of the method.Speculative The BWV 578 story and the two BWV 533 readings (frustration, or confidence and stability) are offered as reasonable extrapolations, not proof.
      VerdictReconciled means rhetorical; not reconciled means literal (step 6).Hybrids are possible in theory but untested.
      Analytic toolsTraditional harmonic and formal analysis plus foreground Schenkerian graphs.Roberts says the method is not limited to these tools or to foreground level.
