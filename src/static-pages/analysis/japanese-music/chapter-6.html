1Sōkyoku-jiuta and the Tōdō

1.1What Sōkyoku-jiuta Is

Two Halves of One Repertoire
Sōkyoku-jiuta箏曲地歌 is a group of genres for koto箏 and shamisen三味線 (sangen三弦) handed down by one body of musicians from the early Edo江戸 period to today. Sōkyoku箏曲 are the genres composed mainly for the koto; jiuta地歌 those composed mainly for the shamisen. Most pieces can be played by both together, but each belongs to one genre, and the second instrument is usually optional.
(1.1)

Tokita 169.

The option is not equal for every genre. Koto kumiuta組歌 and shamisen kumiuta are never played with another instrument, while for tegoto-mono手事物 both instruments are the norm Tokita 169. G Flavin’s own summary: the koto and shamisen are inseparable, and one cannot be discussed without the other Tokita 169.

The works range from serious large-scale chamber music to openly erotic songs of the licensed quarters, and they absorbed popular narrative genres along the way. They are sorted by form, by text and by how they were licensed, and some have moved between categories as new genres arose Tokita 169.

1.2The Tōdō

The Guild
The early sōkyoku-jiuta musicians were blind men of the Tōdō当道, a self-governing guild founded in the fourteenth century by biwa hōshi琵琶法師. Its four main ranks, highest first, were kengyō検校, bettō別当, kōtō勾当 and zatō座頭; the title follows the family name, as in Mitsuzaki Kengyō光崎検校.
(1.2)

Tokita 170–171. On the Tōdō’s origins in heike平家 recitation, see Chapter 3.

  • Money. The shogunate funded the guild, which paid members by rank. The guild also held a monopoly on teaching heike and, in the Edo period, sōkyoku-jiuta Tokita 171.
  • Licensing. A student paid to be accepted, paid for each piece, learned a set number of pieces in fixed order, and bought certificates that cost more at each level Tokita 169 n. 1, 171.
  • Status. Tōdō musicians moved in higher circles than blind monks still tied to Buddhist work, and took up the koto and noble forms to set themselves apart; the guild later admitted acupuncturists, moxa specialists and masseurs Tokita 171 and n. 5.
  • End. The Meiji明治 government abolished the Tōdō in 1871 Tokita 171.

G Malm adds a social picture: the koto came from the court and became an accomplishment for daughters of merchant and noble families, and its gentle tone suited Victorian taste, unlike the shamisen with its theater and pleasure-quarter past Malm 193.

2History

2.1Through the Periods

Step through the history with the buttons or the arrow keys.

Court and Temple · 7th–16th centuries

  • The koto箏 came with gagaku雅楽 in the seventh and early eighth centuries and was a favorite of Heian平安 aristocrats Tokita 171.
  • Players memorized koto parts by singing the hichiriki篳篥 vocables; in the thirteenth century these were replaced by waka和歌-style or religious texts, especially sung to Etenraku越天楽: the Etenraku utaimono越天楽謡物 Tokita 172.
  • Malm notes that the Tale of Genji源氏物語 features the koto or wagon和琴 on at least fifteen occasions, and that court women fleeing to Kyushu九州 from the twelfth century kept solo koto music alive there Malm 195–196.

Tsukushi-goto · 16th century

  • The priest Kenjun賢順 of Zendōji善導寺 in northern Kyushu systematized the Etenraku utaimono, possibly adding arrangements of Chinese qin琴 pieces. The result, tsukushi-goto筑紫箏, is vocal pieces with koto plus one instrumental piece, Rinzetsu輪舌 Tokita 172.
  • It was restricted to clerics and men; blind men and women were barred. Ironically, its last twentieth-century performers were all women Tokita 172.

Yatsuhashi八橋 · 17th century

  • Kenjun’s pupil Hōsui法水 failed at court in Kyoto, moved to Edo江戸, broke the rules and taught the repertoire to a blind shamisen三味線 player; he was expelled Tokita 172.
  • That player, Yatsuhashi Kengyō八橋検校 (1614–85), reworked tsukushi-goto with modal ideas from shamisen music and became the founder of sōkyoku-jiuta箏曲地歌 Tokita 172.
  • The koto–shamisen pairing was in place by the Shichiku shoshinshū糸竹初心集 (1664), which shows koto with hitoyogiri一節切 and shamisen Tokita 173.

Jiuta and Tegoto-mono · 18th–early 19th centuries

  • Tōdō当道 composers wrote mostly jiuta地歌, above all tegoto-mono手事物 and hauta端歌 Tokita 179.
  • Minezaki Kōtō峰崎勾当 (fl. 1780s) made the long instrumental interlude a form of its own; Kikuoka Kengyō菊岡検校 and his koto partner Yaezaki Kengyō八重崎検校 made the two instruments equal Tokita 192.
  • Yamada Kengyō山田検校 (1751–1817) in Edo created a voice-led koto genre from Edo jōruri浄瑠璃 Tokita 174.

Bakumatsu and Meiji明治 · 1830s–1910s

  • In the last decades of the shogunate, Mitsuzaki光崎 and Yoshizawa吉沢 returned to the koto with neo-classical kumiuta組歌 and new scales: bakumatsu shinkyoku幕末新曲 Tokita 174, 179–182.
  • After 1871, composers wrote mostly for two koto, chose classical texts, avoided the shamisen and explored brighter scales: Meiji shinkyoku明治新曲 Tokita 182.

Miyagi and After · 1920s–

  • Miyagi Michio宮城道雄 (1894–1956) brought Western techniques and new kotos; composer and performer began to separate Tokita 194.
  • Postwar works came from composers trained in Western music, and new ensembles such as Pro Musica Nipponia Tokita 194.

2.2Yatsuhashi and the Two Lines

The Turning Point
Contemporary sōkyoku箏曲 begins when tsukushi-goto passed from Buddhist clerics to the blind Tōdō musicians and was adapted to popular taste. Yatsuhashi Kengyō’s reworking popularized the koto and tied it to the Tōdō; a Yatsuhashi-ryū八橋流 barely survives today.
(2.1)

Tokita 172.

Malm gives one concrete change: Fuki菜蕗, traditionally the first tsukushi-goto piece, is in an old gagaku mode in the Tsukushi version but in the folk in陰 scale in the Yatsuhashi school. An early name for blind koto players, insei, meant “singing in the in scale”, though hostile writers chose characters reading “a lewd voice” Malm 198.

Miki names the koto by stage: gakusō楽箏 (court), chikusō筑箏 (the sixteenth-century Tsukushi genre), zokusō俗箏 (popular, after Yatsuhashi) Miki 125–126.

beyond the main source

Malm reports that the pieces credited to the Yatsuhashi school in the 1664 Shichiku shoshinshū include Rokudan六段, Hachidan八段 and Midare乱, and that a Ryukyu琉球 version of Rokudan in a Chinese tuning has led some to argue for a Chinese origin Malm 198–199.

3The Instrument

3.1Body, Strings and Bridges

The Koto
The koto箏 is a 13-string zither of paulownia with movable bridges (ji地). Plectra (tsume爪) on the right thumb, index and middle fingers pluck the strings; the left hand presses or pulls the strings left of the bridges to change pitch and color. Strings are silk or, more and more, nylon or tetron.
(3.1)

Tokita 171; Miki 127–129; Malm 204.

MeasureValueSource
Length, averageabout 185 cm, varying by region and styleFlavin (Tokita 171)
Length, standard6 shaku尺, 181.8 cmMiki 127
Length, Yamada山田 koto180.5 cm; the Ikuta生田 koto is longer and narrowerMalm 204
Bridge height6 cm, with 4.7 cm bridges for tight spotsMiki 129

Two pieces of paulownia make the body: one hollowed as the sound box, one as the bottom plank with two sound holes. Good instruments show sharp grain patterns and are carved inside (ayasugi綾杉) to improve the tone Malm 204. The parts carry dragon names: head, tail, horns, the fixed bridge ryūkaku竜角 Miki 127. A koto is thought to sound best ten to twenty years after it is made Miki 127.

3.2Tunings

Standard Tunings
Because the bridges move, the koto can take any 13-note tuning; in practice a set of standard pentatonic tunings is used. The two most common are hirajōshi平調子 and kumoijōshi雲井調子. Strings are numbered 1–10, then to斗, i為, kin巾; string 1 is a middle pitch, and strings 2–4 lie below it.
(3.2)

Malm 204–205; Miki 131–133. Pitches on D after the Koto Sound Dictionary Sapp, because the figures in Malm and Miki do not survive in our text copies.

G Tuning names differ between the Ikuta and Yamada schools, which causes confusion, and every tuning can be transposed to suit the singer Miki 131. Malm notes that these tunings belong to the yō-in system, while the court koto and early tsukushi-goto筑紫箏 kept the ryo-ritsu呂律 tradition of gagaku雅楽 Malm 205.

The Myth of Two Schools
Flavin warns that “Ikuta-ryū生田流” and “Yamada-ryū山田流” are often presented as the only two schools, which is a mistake. There are 12 ryūha流派 from the Edo period, plus later ones, sharing one body of kumiuta and tsukemono. Yamada-ryū stands apart only by its own repertoire; the others, sharing the rest, have been lumped together as “Ikuta-ryū”.
(3.3)

Tokita 174. For jiuta there are only two ryūha: Yanagawa-ryū柳川流 (Kyoto) and Nogawa-ryū野川流 (Osaka) Tokita 185.

Malm and Miki both frame the koto world as Ikuta versus Yamada, instrumental versus vocal Malm 199 Miki 126: see Where the Sources Differ.

The thumb plucks a few centimeters from the ryūkaku竜角, about one eighth of the string length, and stops on the next string; index and middle fingers pluck the other way Miki 141.

Kakezume (Flavin)
Kakezume is the quintessential kumiuta組歌 pattern: the index finger strikes two adjacent strings upward, the middle finger repeats the move one string lower, and the thumb ends on the octave above the third note.
(4.1)

Tokita 175 n. 9.

4.2Left Hand

Changing Pitch with the Left Hand
Before the twentieth century the left hand worked only on the string to the left of the bridges. Oshide押手 presses the string before plucking to raise it a half or whole step; oshibiki presses after plucking for a rising slide; hiki-iro引き色 pulls the string to lower it slightly; yuriユリ is vibrato.
(4.2)

Miki 136–138. Malm calls pressing typical of popular koto箏 style, and mentions twisting the string for subtle pitch and tone changes Malm 205.

Miki’s other left-hand effects: ato-oshi後押し (an indeterminate press after the pluck, then a lower string), oshi-hanashi (press, pluck, release), tsuki-iro (a quick press and release), keshizume消し爪 (a buzzing mute with the back of the nail), pizzicato with the bare fingers, and harmonics, called fuene笛音 (“flute sounds”) in classical music Miki 137–140. G Miki compares hiki-iro to the shakuhachi’s nayashi: an ornament, not a way to get a new pitch Miki 138.

4.3Rhythm and Shōga

Koto players learn with shōga唱歌, mnemonic syllables that name strokes, much as in other genres (Chapter 1). Teachers sing them while playing when a piece has no vocal part Malm 201 Miki 141.

G Miki contrasts two rhythmic feels: the shamisen’s kokema, where weak beats feel rushed, and the koto’s kinuta砧 rhythm, where weak beats drag, used also in jiuta地歌 shamisen三味線 and in the Awa dance Miki 148. Compare the kinuta-mono砧物 of 6.2.

5Koto Kumiuta

5.1A Fixed Frame

The Kumiuta Frame
Kumiuta組歌 (kumi組 “weaving” + uta歌 “song”) string together short, metrically rigid poems. Each poem has four lines, each line split into 7 + 5 syllables; each of these eight subsections gets eight beats, so each poem fills 64 beats. A typical kumiuta has about six poems.
(5.1)

Tokita 175.

  • Each subsection is marked by a stock pattern made from a basic technique, such as kakezume (4.1). Starting the pattern on different notes gives an effect of theme and variations Tokita 175.
  • Each poem ends with a standard cadential formula, also slightly varied Tokita 175.
  • A kumiuta “beat” is a strong omote表 plus a weak ura裏; so the two opening notes of almost every kumiuta make one beat, however slow Tokita 175 n. 8.
Sacred Repertoire
Until Meiji明治, koto箏 kumiuta were required of every professional and amateur, studied in a fixed order as part of Tōdō当道 licensing. Coming from aristocratic and Buddhist forms, they were revered and are never played on or with other instruments.
(5.2)

Tokita 175.

5.2Fuki and Etenraku

Fuki菜蕗 is the first kumiuta every student learns. Every four measures of its first section repeat a kakezume gesture. Some traditions call it Etenraku越天楽, and setting its first section beside the koto part of the gagaku雅楽 piece shows the derivation clearly Tokita 175.

Malm agrees that Fuki rests on Etenraku imayō and reports research suggesting the whole shirabe-mono調べ物 repertoire is variation on the Etenraku tune Malm 197, 208. Late in the Edo江戸 period Mitsuzaki光崎 and Yoshizawa吉沢 wrote new kumiuta that loosely kept the classical form (9.1) Tokita 175.

6Instrumental Sōkyoku

6.1Danmono

Danmono
Danmono段物 (also shirabe-mono調べ物), with kinuta-mono砧物, are the only instrumental genres of sōkyoku箏曲. A danmono is built from sections (dan段) of a fixed 54 beats. The tempo starts leisurely and accelerates to about twice as fast by the end. The title counts the dan: Rokudan六段 has six.
(6.1)

Tokita 176–178. The exception is Midare乱 (Midare rinzetsu乱輪舌), with dan of irregular length, hence “Disorder” Tokita 177.

Dan-awase
Because every dan has the same length, different dan can be played at the same time: dan-awase段合せ. The first and second dan of Rokudan may be paired; the first dan of Rokudan is often combined with the first of Hachidan八段; and whole pieces, such as Kudan九段 and Kumoi kudan雲井九段, can be played together.
(6.2)

Tokita 177.

Unlike kumiuta組歌, danmono are flexible: duets with second koto箏 parts, sankyoku三曲 with shamisen三味線 and shakuhachi尺八 or kokyū胡弓, even versions without koto. This flexibility made them the representative sōkyoku genre Tokita 176–177. A two- or three-beat motive often opens the first dan and returns, varied, as a half-cadence at the end of each dan but the last Tokita 177.

Malm calls dan-awase partly “paper music”, since you cannot hear a variation as a variation before you have heard the theme, and describes uchiawase打ち合わせ, where a second koto (kaede) plays an unrelated melody in the same tuning against the honte本手 Malm 209–210.

6.2Kinuta-mono

Kinuta-mono
Kinuta-mono imitate the kinuta砧, the wooden block for beating silk, whose thump Chinese and Japanese poets tied to autumn and to a wife’s resentment of an absent husband. Their mark is an ostinato of the block’s rhythm, around which the themes play in rhythmic counterplay. Most have four dan of varying length and share material.
(6.3)

Tokita 178–179.

The first Kinuta is credited to Sayama Kengyō佐山検校 (d. 1694) for shamisen; Ikuta Kengyō生田検校 (1656–1715) reset it for solo koto; the line culminated in Mitsuzaki光崎 Kengyō’s Godan-ginuta五段砧 for two koto, which influenced Meiji明治 music Tokita 179.

6.3Rōsai-mono

Rōsai-bushi弄斎節 was an urban shamisen song of the early seventeenth century. Rōsai-mono弄斎物 turn it into poem suites like kumiuta, but in the 7-7-7-5 meter of folk and popular verse, with folk-like vocables Tokita 179. G Flavin treats them as an example of a lasting habit: the musicians reworked and classicized popular forms Tokita 179.

7Jiuta

7.1The Shamisen Arrives

Jiuta and Its Instrument
Jiuta地歌 are the vocal genres with shamisen三味線 handed down by the Tōdō当道. Ji地 (“earth”) here means the Kansai region, and the character for uta歌 marks it as the blind musicians’ song. The shamisen came from the Ryukyu琉球 sanshin三線 via Sakai堺, reaching Kansai by the late sixteenth century, probably first in the hands of biwa hōshi琵琶法師.
(7.1)

Tokita 184.

The biwa hōshi kept their large plectrum, so snakeskin gave way to dog or cat skin (mainland snakes are also too small), and they added the biwa’s buzzing sawariさわり Tokita 184. Within a century the shamisen had some ten types; jiuta uses the middle-necked chūzao中棹 Tokita 184–185 and n. 12. Malm adds that the koto箏 ensemble’s shamisen (sangen三弦) uses a thin, often tortoiseshell plectrum and a heavier bridge for a softer tone Malm 207.

7.2Shamisen Kumiuta

Linked Only in Name
Shamisen kumiuta組歌, from the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, are the oldest shamisen music. They were the core of Tōdō certification, but shamisen and koto kumiuta are “linked only in name”: shamisen texts come from popular and folk song with no fixed form, use vocables such as so, iyo, ei, have no fixed length per text, and open with short preludes.
(7.2)

Tokita 185–186.

The Nogawa-ryū野川流 sorts them into five groups, oldest first: omotegumi表組 (hontegumi本手組), hadegumi端手組, uragumi裏組, nakagumi中組, okugumi奥組. The omotegumi share a prelude so distinctive that they are called terenton-mono, after the kuchi-jamisen口三味線 for moro-bachi: down- and up-stroke on string 3, then a down-stroke, usually on the open string 2 Tokita 186. After 1871 they left the working repertoire; Yanagawa-ryū柳川流 keeps only six Tokita 185.

8Tegoto-mono

8.1From Ai no Te to Tegoto

A Synthesis of Sōkyoku and Jiuta
Tegoto-mono手事物 are vocal pieces whose instrumental interludes (tegoto手事) often outlast the singing. They grew from the short interludes (ai no te合の手) of nagauta長歌 and hauta端歌. Minezaki Kōtō峰崎勾当 (fl. 1780s) made them a form (Azuma-jishi吾妻獅子, Echigo-jishi越後獅子, Zangetsu残月), with the koto箏 optional; in the nineteenth century, above all with Kikuoka Kengyō菊岡検校 and his koto partner Yaezaki Kengyō八重崎検校, koto and shamisen三味線 became joint conceptions.
(8.1)

Tokita 191–192. For the first time the composer of the koto part is known; early koto parts were improvised, so one piece may have several koto settings Tokita 173–174, 192.

G Flavin calls tegoto-mono “the final synthesis of sōkyoku箏曲 and jiuta地歌”: late works count as jiuta, but they cannot be performed without both instruments Tokita 174, 192.

8.2Form

Tegoto-mono Form
The basic tegoto-mono is mae-uta前歌 (foresong) – tegoto (interlude) – ato-uta後歌 (aftersong). It can grow to two interludes and three songs, and add a prelude (maebiki), an introduction before the tegoto (makura枕), sections marked by shifts of rhythm and tempo (chirashi散らし) and a postlude (ato-chirashi). Retuning both instruments mid-piece to modulate is common.
(8.2)

Tokita 192. An average piece lasts about 18 minutes; the longest, 30 or more Tokita 192.

Malm gives the same three-part core and a practical ceiling of six parts (introduction–song–tegoto–song–tegoto–song), with no thematic link between interludes Malm 209.

8.3The Sankyoku Ensemble

The trio of koto, shamisen and a third instrument is sankyoku三曲. For much of the Edo江戸 period the third was the bowed kokyū胡弓, Japan’s only bowed instrument; since Meiji明治 the shakuhachi尺八 has almost entirely replaced it Tokita 173.

Who Does What (Malm)
In Malm’s description of a jiuta trio, the shamisen plays the main melody, the shakuhachi follows its contour most melismatically, and the koto adds ornament; koto and shamisen players usually sing. A second koto may embellish the interludes.
(8.3)

Malm 209. The bones–flesh–skin image is in Chapter 5 (6.2); Malm finds it “picturesque but not quite appropriate”, since the parts are closer than that Malm 209.

Malm describes the kokyū: about 69 cm, two to four strings, tuned in two fourths (sansagari三下がり), bowed with a loose bow tightened by the fingers and turned on a peg to change strings Malm 206–207.

9New Music from Bakumatsu to Today

9.1Bakumatsu Shinkyoku

Neo-classical Koto Music
In the last Edo江戸 decades (bakumatsu幕末) Mitsuzaki Kengyō光崎検校 and Yoshizawa Kengyō吉沢検校 shifted the focus from shamisen三味線 back to koto箏. Their bakumatsu shinkyoku幕末新曲 loosely follow kumiuta組歌 form, set classical poems (Yoshizawa’s Kokingumi古今組 use the Kokin wakashū古今和歌集) and explore new tunings, such as a scale drawn from the banshiki-chō盤渉調 of gagaku雅楽.
(9.1)

Tokita 179, 182. Mitsuzaki’s Akikaze no kyoku秋風の曲 sets six poems to 64 beats each, like Yatsuhashi八橋, with a prelude that matches Rokudan六段 beat for beat Tokita 179.

9.2Meiji Shinkyoku

Meiji明治 Shinkyoku
After 1871, composers chose classical texts over pleasure-quarter ones, avoided the shamisen and wrote mostly for two koto tuned a fifth apart, with denser textures and new techniques such as left-hand pizzicato. Their new scales lack the half steps of miyako-bushi都節, probably under the influence of Chinese minshingaku明清楽, and were heard as “brighter”.
(9.2)

Tokita 179 n. 10, 182.

Malm adds two named nineteenth-century tunings: orandajōshiオランダ調子 (the Western major scale, after the Dutch) and kankanjōshi (a pentatonic tuning named for a minshingaku piece) Malm 210–211.

9.3Miyagi and the New Kotos

Miyagi Michio宮城道雄 (1894–1956) began several trends: Western techniques, a growing split between composer and performer, and new instruments Tokita 194. Postwar koto music came largely from composers trained in Western music, such as Miki Minoru三木稔 and Takemitsu Tōru武満徹, and ensembles like Pro Musica Nipponia (from the 1960s) Tokita 194.

9.4Lessons and Research

Malm’s picture of lessons: first come, first served at the teacher’s home; the teacher plays along, singing syllables; the order of pieces is fixed and pupils may not study another school’s pieces; a professional name (natori名取) is bought, pyramid-style, up to the iemoto家元; and students pay to perform in long recitals Malm 200–202. G He warns that rote teaching risks producing “musical automatons” Malm 203.

Flavin notes that Japanese research, led by Hirano Kenji平野健次, has been historical and literary, with little musical analysis; Hirano’s Sōkyoku-jiuta taikei (220 pieces on 60 LPs) is a landmark Tokita 195.

Where the Sources Differ

Errors and disagreements found while cross-reading, grouped by source, with the check that exposed each one. The guide text uses the form named in each check.

Malm 2000

Length of a kumiuta dan. Malm: tsukushigoto dan are 64 beats, but Yatsuhashi八橋 kumiuta dan are 120 beats (p. 208).
Check: Flavin: each poem is set to 64 beats, the classic form “established by Yatsuhashi” that Mitsuzaki光崎 still followed (Tokita 175, 179). The guide follows Flavin.
Did Ikuta生田 invent the koto箏–shamisen三味線 ensemble?. Malm: Ikuta Kengyō’s big innovation was combining koto with shamisen, and he coined jiuta for it (p. 199).
Check: Flavin: the pairing was established by the Shichiku shoshinshū of 1664, before Ikuta was born (1656) or active; “Ikuta-ryū生田流” is a collective label for the non-Yamada ryūha (Tokita 173–174).
Two schools. Malm: the main distinction is between the Ikuta (instrumental) and Yamada山田 (vocal) schools (p. 199).
Check: Flavin: there are 12 Edo-period ryūha; Yamada differs by its own repertoire, and the two-school picture is a mistaken polarization (Tokita 174).
Jiuta and tegoto-mono. Malm: jiuta is “sometimes called tegotomono” (p. 208).
Check: Flavin: tegoto-mono is one of seven jiuta genres (Tokita 185). Malm notes himself that jiuta is now more a manner of performance (p. 210).
Dates. Malm: Kenjun賢順 154?–1636, Yatsuhashi 1614–84, Yamada Kengyō山田検校 1757–1817 (pp. 197–199).
Check: Flavin: Kenjun ?1534–1623, Yatsuhashi 1614–85, Yamada 1751–1817 (Tokita 172, 174); Miki also gives 1614–85 (p. 126).
Year of the 17-string koto. Malm: invented by Miyagi in 1922 (p. 204).
Check: Miki: 1921 (p. 126); Tsukitani also gives 1921 (Tokita 163).

Miki 2008

Two traditions. Miki: the zokusō developed into two traditions, Ikuta and Yamada, which remain the two major ones (p. 126).
Check: Flavin: a widespread simplification (Tokita 174). Both views agree that Ikuta and Yamada are the best-known labels.

Tokita and Hughes 2017

Kikuoka’s birth year. Flavin gives Kikuoka Kengyō菊岡検校 as 1792–1847 (p. 173).
Check: The same chapter gives 1791–1847 (p. 192). Not resolved here.
