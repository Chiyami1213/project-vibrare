1The Shakuhachi and Its Kinds

1.1Name and Blowing Edge

The shakuhachi尺八 is an end-blown “notched flute” of bamboo, born in China and developed in Japan. It has a sharp blowing edge, the utaguchi歌口, and five finger holes: four on the front and a thumb hole behind Tokita 145.

What “Shakuhachi” Means
The name is a length: one shaku尺 and eight (hachi鈸) sun寸, with ten sun to the shaku. A Tang-period shaku was shorter, so the Tang instrument was 43.7 cm; the standard Japanese instrument today is 54.5 cm.
(1.1)

Tokita 145; Miki gives the same 54.5 cm Miki 35. Malm’s figure for the length differs: see Where the Sources Differ.

The shakuhachi’s blowing edge is cut diagonally outwards and, seen from the front, is a shallow crescent. Later Chinese notched flutes (dongxiao洞簫, nan chiba) slant the cut inwards, make a deeper “u” notch and have six holes. Flutes with the shakuhachi kind of edge survive only in Japan.
(1.2)

Tokita 145. Malm also calls the outward cut “the exact opposite of the Chinese manner” and believes it a Japanese innovation Malm 166.

1.2Five Kinds in Japan

Tsukitani counts the gagaku雅楽 instrument and at least four later kinds Tokita 145–146:

Tokita 145–148, 153–154. Malm counts four end-blown flutes: the tempuku天吹, the gagaku shakuhachi, the hitoyogiri and the modern shakuhachi Malm 165.

The gagaku shakuhachi has six holes and gives a heptatonic scale, as in China. The other pre-twentieth-century Japanese kinds have five holes, giving intervals close to the anhemitonic pentatonic yō陽 (min’yō民謡) scale.
(1.3)

Tokita 146. Every kind of music now played on the shakuhachi, from Zen pieces to jazz, uses the fuke type Tokita 146.

1.3Where It Came From

Legend says a foreigner called Roan brought the hitoyogiri in the late fifteenth century, and the Zen monk Kakushin覚心 brought the fuke shakuhachi from China in 1254. Evidence is lacking for both Tokita 146.

TheoryClaim
Three importsgagaku shakuhachi, hitoyogiri and fuke shakuhachi each came separately
Two importsthe six-hole gagaku instrument died out; a five-hole instrument came separately
One importall five-hole shakuhachi are Japanese developments of the gagaku instrument

Tokita 146.

Tsukitani’s View
G Tsukitani supports the one-import theory. All three instruments share the same blowing edge; moving from a Chinese heptatonic to a Japanese pentatonic scale would make one hole unnecessary; and by the thirteenth century China had no flutes with this edge, so any later import should have had the dongxiao mouthpiece.
(1.4)

Tokita 147. Malm and Miki tell older stories: Malm derives the shakuhachi from single pipes of a Chinese pitch-pipe panpipe (paixiao排簫) Malm 165–166; Miki repeats the Kakushin account with “it is said” Miki 35.

2History

2.1Through the Periods

Step through the history with the buttons or the arrow keys. Each period has its own instrument or its own players.

Court Instrument · 8th–12th centuries

  • The shakuhachi尺八 came to Japan with the tōgaku唐楽 ensemble of gagaku雅楽 in the late seventh or early eighth century Tokita 145.
  • Eight survive in the Shōsōin正倉院 of Tōdaiji東大寺, Nara奈良: five of bamboo, one of white jade, one of carved stone and one of ivory, all with three nodes (real or carved) and six holes. Five belonged to Emperor Shōmu聖武; four were gifts from King Uija of Paekche to a Japanese minister. A ninth, at Hōryūji法隆寺 (44.2 cm), is now in the Tokyo National Museum Tokita 145, 147.
  • The six-hole instrument vanished from gagaku in the late ninth century. The Imakagami今鏡 (1170) notes a performance in 1158 of an instrument “not heard for a century”, the last mention Tokita 147.

Hitoyogiri · 13th–18th centuries

  • The Kyōkunshō教訓抄 (1233) calls the short flute shakuhachi and says blind monks and sarugaku猿楽 players use it Tokita 147.
  • The Taigenshō體源鈔 (1512) gives the first picture of a one-segment, five-hole shakuhachi. The Zen monk Ikkyū一休 (1394–1481) is said to have loved it Tokita 147.
  • The name means “one segment cut”: a single node, with the root end as the blowing end, standardized at 33.6 cm in the seventeenth century Tokita 145–147.
  • Ōmori Sōkun大森宗勲 (1570–1625) revived and refined it. Books with notation followed: Shichiku shoshinshū糸竹初心集 (1664), Tōshōkyoku (1669), Ikanobori (1687) Tokita 147.
  • At its late-seventeenth-century peak it played solos, accompanied popular songs and joined koto箏 and shamisen三味線, an early form of sankyoku三曲. It was nearly gone by the early nineteenth century, too quiet and simple to compete with the fuke普化 shakuhachi Tokita 147–148.

Tenpuku · 16th century to 1959

  • A thin flute (7–8 cm around, about 30 cm long) of Kagoshima鹿児島, encouraged by Shimazu Tadayoshi島津忠良 (1492–1568) of Satsuma薩摩 together with the satsuma-biwa薩摩琵琶 Tokita 148.
  • Its mouthpiece is basically of the Chinese dongxiao洞簫 type, so it may have been imported separately Tokita 148.
  • It was part of Satsuma education until about 1920. By 1950 Ōta Ryōichi (1888–1959) was the only living player; Shirao Kunitoshi recorded him in 1958 Tokita 148–149.
  • Seven short solo pieces survive (30 seconds to four minutes), in free rhythm, taught without notation or shōga唱歌. Makers measured it by finger and fist widths, so pitches varied Tokita 149.

Komusō and the Fuke Sect · mid-17th century to 1871

  • The fuke shakuhachi is a modern scholars’ name; Edo江戸 sources say komusō虚無僧 shakuhachi, after the “priests of nothingness” of the Fuke sect of Rinzai臨済 Zen Tokita 150.
  • The sect had no doctrine or scripture; playing, called suizen吹禅 (“blowing Zen”), took the place of meditation and sutras Tokita 150.
  • Shakuhachi were used only for takuhatsu托鉢 (playing door to door for alms) and temple services; the pieces born there are today’s koten honkyoku古典本曲 Tokita 151.
  • In 1847 the shogunate declared the sect’s founding decree a forgery; in 1871 the sect was abolished and the shakuhachi opened to all Tokita 151–152.

Open to All · 1871–

  • The Kinko-ryū琴古流 in Kantō and the Tozan-ryū都山流 in Kansai (from 1896) became the shakuhachi “majority” Tokita 152.
  • Two paths opened: a secular one centered on sankyoku ensemble and artistry, and a smaller religious one that kept the spiritual tradition Tokita 152.
  • Twentieth-century movements gave it new music, new instruments and, from the 1960s, an international following Tokita 162–164, 168.

2.2The Fuke Sect: Legend and Record

The Legend
The Kyotaku denki虚鐸伝記 (1795) tells the founding legend. The Tang monk Puhua普化 (Fuke) walked the streets ringing a bell; his would-be disciple Zhang Bai張伯 made a bamboo pipe to imitate it, the kyotaku虚鐸 or “empty bell”. Sixteen generations later Kakushin覚心 learned it and brought it to Japan in 1254. His disciple Kichiku寄竹 (Kyochiku虚竹) heard music in a dream and made Mukaiji霧海篪 and Kokūji; with Kyorei虚鈴 these became the three fundamental pieces.
(2.1)

Tokita 151. Tsukitani calls the account semi-legendary Tokita 151.

The Record
The record, as Tsukitani gives it: beggar-ascetics called boro暮露 (mentioned in Tsurezuregusa徒然草) were absorbed in the late fifteenth century into the komosō薦僧, “straw-mat monks”, who played the hitoyogiri一節切 or a straight three-node flute, the miyogiri三節切. The komosō became the komusō; the Fuke sect was established in the mid-seventeenth century, its members often masterless samurai, and by the late eighteenth century it had more than 70 temples.
(2.2)

Tokita 151. Main temples: Myōanji明暗寺 in Kyoto (founded by Kyochiku’s disciple Tengai Myōfu; revived 1890 as the Myōan Kyōkai明暗教会, incorporated 1950), and Ichigetsuji一月寺 and Reihōji鈴法寺, which controlled the Kantō temples and supported Kurosawa Kinko黒沢琴古 (1710–71) Tokita 151.

The sect’s privileges, including a monopoly on the shakuhachi, rested on the Okitegaki jūikkajō, a decree supposedly from Tokugawa Ieyasu徳川家康. In practice the public played too, and with other instruments. In 1847, after years of high-handed behavior by komusō, the shogunate declared the decree a forgery by the sect itself; in 1871 the sect was abolished.
(2.3)

Tokita 151–152.

Malm tells a darker story, from the unfinished research of Nakatsuka Chikuzen筑前: rōnin浪人 who feared persecution after Shimabara島原 (1638) formed the Fukeshu in Kyoto, forged papers to win recognition, and were licensed on condition that they spy on other rōnin; their headquarters moved to Edo, with a “business” center in Asakusa浅草 Malm 168–169. He adds that they rebuilt the flute as a club for self-defense Malm 169. G Tsukitani does not mention spying or clubs; treat both as Malm’s colorful account rather than settled history.

3The Instrument

3.1Bamboo, Joint and Inlay

Material and Shape
The fuke普化 shakuhachi尺八 is cut from madake真竹 bamboo with the wide root end as the bell. It has seven nodes as standard: three in the body, one at the utaguchi歌口 and three at the bottom. The utaguchi is reinforced with an inlay of deer antler or water buffalo horn: crescent-shaped for Tozan都山, trapezoidal for Kinko琴古.
(3.1)

Tokita 146, 152–153. Miki adds ivory and, since these are hard to get, ebonite for the inlay Miki 35–36. Malm notes the inlay is called tsuno角 or hasamiguchi, and that the Myōan明暗 instrument has a differently shaped one Malm 166, 171.

Before the twentieth century the instrument was one piece of bamboo. Today it is usually cut in the middle and rejoined with a joint like a recorder’s, which makes it easier to carry and to make, and lets the maker adjust the length Tokita 152–153. Malm adds that makers often bend the bamboo to get the accepted curved shape Malm 171.

3.2Ji and Jinashi

Old Pipes and New
The komusō虚無僧 often made his own flute, by experience and intuition. These kokan古管 (“old pipes”) vary in length, width and holes; the node rims are left inside and the bore is only lacquered against moisture. Modern instruments coat the bore with ji地, a paste of lacquer, polishing powder and gypsum, for stable pitch and timbre and more volume. The uncoated kind is now called jinashi地無し, “without ji”.
(3.2)

Tokita 153.

G Almost all professionals now use ji-coated instruments even for honkyoku本曲, though Tsukitani feels that repertoire suits the uncoated flute better Tokita 153. The coating lets the shakuhachi hold its own beside koto箏, shamisen三味線 and Western instruments in large halls, and allows mass production to one standard Tokita 153.

3.3Lengths and Keys

Naming by Length (Miki)
A shakuhachi is named by the pitch it gives with all holes closed. The standard 1.8 (one shaku尺 eight sun寸) gives d′ and is called the d-kan管. Each sun longer lowers the pitch by about a semitone; each sun shorter raises it by about a semitone.
(3.3)

Miki 36–37. Tsukitani gives the same standard: all holes closed on a 1.8 gives d′ Tokita 156.

G The rule is only approximate at the extremes. The a-kan, a fourth below the d-kan, is 2.4, not 2.3 as the rule predicts; Miki’s chart lists sizes from 1.1 (a-kan) to the G-kan, labeled 2.5 but nearly 2.7 in practice, so long that it is extraordinarily hard to play and rarely seen Miki 37, 40–42.

3.4More Holes and Keys

Western influence changed the instrument in two steps Tokita 153–154:

  • Hole placement. Many modern five-hole flutes move and angle the holes slightly to give Western major seconds and minor thirds; older flutes often had evenly spaced holes and needed changes of blowing angle for those intervals Tokita 153.
  • More holes. Half-holing and changing the blowing angle give the twelve tones unevenly in volume and timbre. That unevenness is what makes the shakuhachi special, but new music wanted uniformity Tokita 153.

Nine-holeKoga Yūzen, 1932; Shibata Seizan, 1949all twelve tones easily, but hardly used now
ŌkurauroオークラウロŌkura Kishichirō大倉喜七郎, 1923; relaunched 1936 in four sizesmetal, Boehm keys; never caught on, partly for its price

Tokita 153–154. Miki sees the seven-hole flute more positively: it keeps the five-hole fingerings, suits contemporary music, and many professionals use one Miki 35. Malm adds a horizontal “bamboo bass flute” experiment Malm 172.

4Fingering, Meri and Kari

4.1The Open-Hole Row

Five Holes, Five Fuji
On a standard 1.8, opening the holes one by one from the bottom gives d′–f′–g′–a′–c″, the min’yō民謡 scale of folk song. Each fingering has a syllable, a fuji譜字: ro, tsu, re, chi, then ha (Tozan都山, Myōan明暗) or ri (Kinko琴古).
(4.1)

Tokita 156, 165; Tozan and Kinko names from Miki’s chart Miki 39. Malm gives the five tones as D, F, G, A, D: see Where the Sources Differ.

G The fuji name fingerings, not pitches, and they differ between schools. In Kinko notation a number can be added to a basic sign for a special fingering, such as go-no-hi (“five-hi”), which is still sung as hi although it differs in pitch from the normal hi Tokita 164, 166.

4.2Meri and Kari

Bending Pitch with the Head
Pitches between the open-hole notes come from part-holing and from meriメリ and kariカリ: lowering or raising the pitch by moving the head against the mouthpiece. Meri also weakens and diffuses the tone, which gives shakuhachi尺八 music its special flavor.
(4.2)

Tokita 165, 167–168.

The three books size the bends differently:

SourceSize of the bend
Tsukitanif and a lowered “by up to a tone” in honkyoku本曲 scales (Tokita 156)
Mikimeri = a minor second down, ō-meri大メリ = a major second down, kari = a minor second up (Miki 43)
Malmin theory three levels each of meri and kari, often a quarter tone or less (Malm 172)

G The views fit together if Miki gives the standard steps used in notation, and Malm the finer shadings players use within them. Miki adds practical points: kari is easier in tune in the low register than in the high, meri-kariメリカリ is easier on shorter flutes, and half-holed and bent notes are softer than open ones Miki 43.

4.3Three Registers

Registers (Miki)
Miki names three registers. Otsu乙 (low): rich, with great control of color; a good player can make the lowest note strong. Kan甲 (the second overtone series): lips tighter, sound bright and piercing, color hardly changes with volume. Daikan大甲 (above the third): piercing up to g‴ on a 1.8, “unmusical” above, and playable only at forte.
(4.3)

Miki 42–43. Tsukitani gives the honkyoku range of a 1.8 as just over two octaves, c′ to e‴ Tokita 156.

G Miki writes for composers: in the otsu and kan registers basic notes go from pp to ff with full crescendos, and the extended meri a tone below the fundamental is “rarely used outside” honkyoku Miki 42–43.

5Classical Honkyoku

5.1Two Living Traditions

What Classical Honkyoku Are
Classical honkyoku本曲 are the pieces the komusō虚無僧 played as religious practice in the Edo江戸 period. After 1871 they followed two lines: the Kinko-ryū琴古流 in Tokyo turned its “thirty-six honkyoku” into concert pieces, while Myōanji明暗寺 in Kyoto (also read Meianji明暗寺) and other former temples kept the religious practice.
(5.1)

Tokita 154–155. At Myōanji devotees still play every November before an image of Kyochiku虚竹, play in unison after sutras on death anniversaries, and occasionally go out in komusō dress for takuhatsu托鉢 Tokita 154.

Honkyoku means “original pieces”, played only on the shakuhachi尺八. All are solo pieces (unison playing included) except Shika no tōne鹿の遠音, with two overlapping parts. Still, by the late eighteenth century Kinko琴古 players were adding second melodies to some pieces, like the honte本手 and kaete替手 of koto箏 music Tokita 155. Malm hears Shika no tōne as a buck and doe calling, their two melodies drawing together until they are one Malm 176.

5.2Transmission and Regional Styles

More than 150 classical honkyoku survive; who composed them, and when, is unknown. Komusō carried them from temple to temple and region to region Tokita 155.

Same Title, Different Tune
It was said that each temple had its own melodies, ichiji ichiritsu一寺一律, “one temple, one melody”. So many pieces share a title but not a tune: there were many Reibo鈴慕 (“yearning for the bell” of Puhua普化), told apart by region, such as Kyō reibo京鈴慕, Kyūshū reibo九州鈴慕, Echigo reibo越後鈴慕; Tsuru no sugomori鶴の巣籠 survives in more than ten versions. After 1871 players named versions by the person they learned from, not by the temple.
(5.2)

Tokita 155.

5.3Scale

The Honkyoku Scale
Classical honkyoku do not use the open-hole min’yō民謡 row. The f and a are lowered by meri and part-holing, giving scales of two conjunct tetrachords with nuclear tones a fourth apart (d–g, g–c). Most pieces are in miyako-bushi都節 d–e♭–g–a♭–c; ritsu律 d–e–g–a–c appears in Myōan Taizan-ryū and some Itchōken pieces.
(5.3)

Tokita 156; compare the tetrachord types of Chapter 1.

Two further resources Tokita 156:

  • Kinko akebono-jōshi曙調子 pieces such as San’ya sugagaki三谷菅垣 and Banshiki-chō盤渉調 use miyako-bushi on g: g–a♭–c–d♭–f.
  • The miyako-bushi tetrachord a–b♭–d makes brief, partial modulations.

G The semitones of miyako-bushi shrink toward a quarter tone in the Tōhoku style, giving its plaintive feel; the middle tones of a tetrachord vary with the player in all shakuhachi music Tokita 156. Malm calls the old pieces’ scale the in陰 scale, the usual name in his book for this family Malm 174.

5.4Rhythm and Breath

One Breath, One Phrase
Nearly all classical honkyoku are in free rhythm. A piece is a chain of short melodic units, each one breath long. A player’s breath is about constant, so a unit with few notes holds each note long, and a unit with many notes is busier. Pieces usually begin slowly and speed up; tempo changes little in Kansai and Kantō styles, while Tōhoku pieces grow much denser.
(5.4)

Tokita 156, 158.

A few pieces are hyōshi-buki拍子吹き, “blowing with a beat”: Sagariha下り葉 repeats notes on a regular, unmetered pulse, and the festive Azuma-jishi吾妻獅子 and Kumoi-jishi, linked to lion dances, have a rubato duple meter. They are seen as lighter and less religious Tokita 156.

Malm stresses how each breath phrase ends: a final grace note, often a brief flick below the final tone, chosen to suit the melodic situation and the school, together with constant dynamic swells from a reedy piano to a ringing forte Malm 172–173. He notes this describes the Kinko style; Myōan明暗 phrases are shorter and breathier Malm 173 n. 7.

5.5Form

Four Form Patterns
Tsukitani names four common form patterns after representative pieces. Binary (Ajikan阿字観): I A–B, II C–B–coda, with C at the top of the range. Arch (Tōhoku Reibo): low shirabe調べ, a climb to a high climax, the modulating hachigaeshi鉢返し, a descent and a coda, like the shape of a mountain; the classic form. Four-part (Kokū reibo虚空鈴慕): A–B–C–B′, an expanded binary. Dan段 structure (Tsuru no sugomori): parallel sections of equal length, likely influenced by koto danmono段物.
(5.5)

Tokita 158. The Kinko Sōkaku reibo巣鶴鈴慕, a variant of Tsuru no sugomori, has 12 dan, each opening with new material and closing with one of three or four formulas Tokita 158.

G Tsukitani’s point is that honkyoku are not free improvisation: even pieces that fit none of the patterns are more than strings of breath units Tokita 158. Malm agrees that form is real but sees a rondo-like A–B–A–C–A, with varied returns of a main idea and climaxes in the new sections, while warning that his view rests on only a fraction of the repertoire Malm 173–174.

6Sankyoku and Gaikyoku

6.1Ensemble Before and After 1871

In shakuhachi尺八 music, gaikyoku外曲 (“outside pieces”) means jiuta地歌 and sōkyoku箏曲 melodies arranged for the shakuhachi and played in the sankyoku三曲 trio with koto箏 and shamisen三味線 Tokita 158.

Evidence of Early Ensemble
Ensemble was older than the ban. The hitoyogiri一節切 played pieces such as Sugagaki菅垣 and Rinzetsu輪舌 with koto and shamisen, common in the late seventeenth century. The Fuke普化 sect forbade ensemble playing, but the jiuta book Utakeizu歌系図 (1782) shows a fuke普化 shakuhachi in a trio, and late-eighteenth-century Kinko琴古 books contain a part for the koto piece Rokudan六段.
(6.1)

Tokita 158–159.

Since Meiji明治, gaikyoku have been the mainstream Tokita 159:

  • Kantō: Kinko arrangements began right after 1871, led by Araki Kodō荒木古童 II (1823–1908).
  • Kansai: the Sōetsu-ryū宗悦流 of Kondō Sōetsu近藤宗悦 (1821–67) was already playing them; from it came the Tozan-ryū都山流 (Osaka, 1896) of Nakao Tozan中尾都山 (1876–1956).
  • Osaka, 1917: the Ueda-ryū上田流 (Ueda Hōdō上田芳憧) and Chikuho-ryū竹保流 (Sakai Chikuho酒井竹保) also began by building a gaikyoku repertoire.

G The 1871 abolition of both the Fuke sect and the Tōdō当道, the guild of blind musicians, opened Zen music and blind musicians’ chamber music to everyone and let them merge Tokita 159–160.

6.2Bones, Flesh and Skin

The Shakuhachi as Skin
In sankyoku the parts are likened to bones (shamisen), flesh (koto) and skin (shakuhachi); the shakuhachi follows the shamisen most closely. The trio asks the shakuhachi for moderate volume, exact pitch, soft tone and rhythmic coordination, a very different world from honkyoku本曲.
(6.2)

Tokita 160. Playing in time with strings and voice also pushed notation to show meter clearly (8.3) Tokita 160.

Malm agrees: in jiuta the shakuhachi plays a melismatic version of the shamisen line, having replaced the kokyū胡弓 fiddle as the usual third instrument by the late nineteenth century; he does not count such pieces as an independent shakuhachi form Malm 175. G The individual techniques of honkyoku (9.2, 9.3) are absent here; the shakuhachi must suppress its individuality Tokita 168.

7New Schools and New Music

7.1Tozan, Ueda and Chikuho

The founders of the three new schools also composed honkyoku本曲 for them, sometimes called kindai近代 (modern) honkyoku Tokita 160.

Tozan’s Modernization
The Tozan-ryū都山流 has 87 honkyoku, 28 by Nakao Tozan中尾都山, and most are ensemble pieces: of Tozan’s 28, only three are entirely solo. Some show Western influence (3/8 rhythms in Kojō no tsuki湖上の月 and Shinsei), and his parallel, organum-like homophony stays mostly within Japanese modes, as if seeking a Japanese harmony.
(7.1)

Tokita 160.

  • Chikuho-ryū竹保流: Sakai Chikuho酒井竹保 I (1892–1984) wrote in many styles from 1917 to 1966, from Kochō no tawamure (1919, with a waltz movement) to Karyō no mai (1952, with mixed choir); the school also keeps classical honkyoku Tokita 162.

7.2Shin Nihon Ongaku and Shin-hōgaku

Two Movements
Shin nihon ongaku新日本音楽 (“new Japanese music”) began with a 1920 concert of works by Miyagi Michio宮城道雄 and Motoori Nagayo本居長世; the Kinko琴古 player Yoshida Seifū吉田晴風 gave it its name. Shin-hōgaku新邦楽 names a 1930s trend. Both enlarged the ensemble, raised the shakuhachi’s role, took in Western classical influence and remodeled instruments.
(7.2)

Tokita 162.

Miyagi moved the shakuhachi尺八 from skin to equal partner: in Aki no shirabe秋の調 (1919), a canon, it is still an obbligato, but in Haru no umi春の海 (1929, A–B–A′) it carries the main melody, like a flute sonata Tokita 162. New instruments followed: the 17-string koto箏 (1921), the bass shamisen三味線 (1924), and the seven- and nine-hole shakuhachi and ōkurauroオークラウロ Tokita 163. Malm notes Haru no umi has been heard in every arrangement, but sounds best in the original Malm 170–171.

7.3Gendai Hōgaku

Back to Honkyoku Technique
From the late 1950s composers trained in Western methods began writing for Japanese instruments: gendai hōgaku現代邦楽. The shakuhachi joined in the 1960s through cross-school groups, led by the Shakuhachi Sanbonkai尺八三本会 (1964: Aoki Reibo青木鈴慕 II, Yamamoto Hōzan山本邦山, Yokoyama Katsuya横山勝也). Works such as Moroi Makoto’s Chikurai goshō竹籟五章 (1964) and Takemitsu武満徹 Tōru’s November Steps (1967) built on classical honkyoku technique, a “renaissance” for the instrument.
(7.3)

Tokita 163. Irino Yoshirō’s Music for Two Koto (1957) was the first twelve-tone work for Japanese instruments Tokita 163.

G Tsukitani’s judgment: the shin-hōgaku邦楽 period, with its German Romantic influence, saw the shakuhachi’s individuality as a drawback; in the end “it was the classical shakuhachi that caught international ears” Tokita 163, 168. Malm, from a listener’s side, criticizes massed shakuhachi bands as the belief that “ten shakuhachi sound ten times better than one” Malm 171, 175.

7.4Folk, Popular and Jazz

  • Folk song. Shakuhachi accompaniment is surprisingly recent: Kōji Toyotarō of Hokkaido北海道 moved Esashi oiwake江差追分 from shamisen to shakuhachi in the 1890s, and the practice spread with the song. It suits free-rhythm songs, which are close to honkyoku Tokita 164.
  • Popular music. It accompanies shigin詩吟 (recited Chinese poetry) and expresses heartbreak in enka演歌 Tokita 164.
  • Jazz. Yamamoto Hōzan of the Tozan都山 school played at the 1967 Newport Jazz Festival Tokita 164.

G Few Japanese instruments cover so many genres Tokita 164. Miki adds that kobushi小節, the ornament of folk song and enka, also suits honkyoku but is hard to notate Miki 51.

8Notation

8.1From Hitoyogiri to Fu-ho-u

Fingerings, Not Pitches
The oldest shakuhachi尺八 notation is the hitoyogiri一節切 score Tanteki hidenfu短笛秘伝譜 (1608), which writes fingerings, not pitches, with katakana syllables such as ya, ta, ho, ro. Modern notation still writes fingerings as fuji譜字 in columns, but the fuji differ between hitoyogiri and shakuhachi and between schools.
(8.1)

Tokita 164. Nothing is known of notation for the gagaku雅楽 shakuhachi Tokita 164.

The hitoyogiri books of 1664 and 1687 use 13 syllables and no rhythm signs beyond phrase marks. The first fuke普化 shakuhachi notation took eight of them; called fu-ho-u after its first three signs, it was used at Myōanji明暗寺 and nearby Tokita 164.

8.2Myōan Notation

Size Shows Duration
Myōan Taizan-ryū明暗対山流 now uses Kobayashi Shizan’s Myōan明暗 Dōshūkai notation: basic fuji (ro, tsu, re, chi, ha, i), signs for uchi打ち, meriメリ and kariカリ, and character size for duration: large = one “beat”, medium = a half, small = a quarter, with a vertical line below for each extra beat. In Tozan都山 notation, by contrast, size shows high and low.
(8.2)

Tokita 165. Earlier, teachers wrote each pupil’s score by hand, which led to discrepancies Tokita 165.

G These “beats” are only a rough guide in unmetered honkyoku本曲, and some special fingerings are learned by watching, not from the page; the syllables can be sung in teaching Tokita 165.

9Technique

9.1Breath and Learning

Learning Order
The saying kubifuri sannen, koro hachinen首振り三年コロ八年, “three years for shaking the head, eight for korokoroコロコロ”, sums up how long mastery takes. Basic to everything is tanden kokyū丹田呼吸, breathing centered on the tanden丹田, a point below the navel. Beginners play long notes with stable pitch first, then learn fingering with part-holing and embouchure changes.
(9.1)

Tokita 166. Miki translates the saying as three years to produce vibrato by shaking the head Miki 43.

G Traditionally the shakuhachi尺八 does not tongue. Miki notes that contemporary pieces often need tonguing, double and triple tonguing are possible, and many players use circular breathing, which no longer impresses audiences Miki 43, 45.

9.2Finger Articulation

Articulating with the Fingers
Repeated notes are articulated by the fingers, not the breath or tongue: oshi押し (or yuriユリ) briefly opens a closed hole and closes it again; uchi打ち briefly closes an open hole. Either works as a run of upper or lower mordents; the difference is in sound quality more than pitch. The ultimate form is korokoro, alternating oshi on holes 1 and 2 with a fixed fingering.
(9.2)

Tokita 166. Miki calls the same pair of moves yuru (ren-on) and utsu (uru) Miki 51–52.

In classical honkyoku本曲, korokoro is a slow “whirlpool of sound”; in newer pieces it can be a quick tremolo. Karakaraカラカラ and harahara, much used since the Tozan-ryū都山流, are a true tremolo of repeated uchi on hole 1 Tokita 166–167. Miki adds suri摺り (a slide by slowly covering or uncovering a hole) and nayashi (a sustained note bent down and back) Miki 50.

9.4The Shakuhachi Today

Conclusion (Tsukitani)
After 1,300 years in Japan, much of the shakuhachi’s history is still unclear. Its popularity has eased since the boom of the 1960s and 1970s, but it keeps steady devotees and a slowly growing following abroad. Its Fuke-sect past made it a “male instrument”; women players, once rare, are becoming common, especially outside Japan.
(9.3)

Tokita 168.

G Tsukitani regrets that the research community in Japan is small and that the most visible work comes from scholars abroad Tokita 168. Malm likewise notes the growth of non-Japanese players and makers, and sees the instrument’s center in private, intimate performance Malm 171, 176.

Where the Sources Differ

Errors and disagreements found while cross-reading, grouped by source, with the check that exposed each one. The guide text uses the form named in each check.

Malm 2000

Length of the standard flute. Malm: one shaku eight sun is “approximately fifty-six centimeters” (p. 166).
Check: Tsukitani and Miki: 54.5 cm (Tokita 145; Miki 35). Malm himself gives 54.5 cm later (p. 171). The guide uses 54.5 cm.
Holes of the Chinese flute. Malm: each pipe of the panpipe was given five holes, making the dongxiao (p. 166).
Check: Tsukitani: the dongxiao and the gagaku雅楽 shakuhachi尺八 have six holes (Tokita 145, 147).
Scale of the gagaku shakuhachi. Malm: it “produced the Chinese pentatonic scale” (p. 167).
Check: Tsukitani: six holes yielding a heptatonic scale, as in China (Tokita 146). The guide follows Tsukitani.
The open-hole notes. Malm: the five holes give D, F, G, A, D (p. 172).
Check: Tsukitani: d–f–g–a–c (Tokita 156); Miki’s chart has c″ for ha/ri and d″ only as an alternative fingering (Miki 39). Malm’s top D is probably the octave, not a fifth hole.
When the Tozan都山 school began. Malm: Tozan appeared “in the early Meiji明治 period” (p. 170).
Check: Tsukitani: Osaka, 1896 (Tokita 152, 159), the 29th year of Meiji. Not early.
The three fundamental pieces. Malm: “Kyorei”, “Mukaiji” and “Kokureibo” (p. 174).
Check: Tsukitani: Mukaiji, Kokūji and Kyorei (Tokita 151); Kokū reibo is a separate Kinko琴古 piece (Tokita 158). The guide follows Tsukitani.
Spies and clubs. Malm: the Fukeshu were rōnin licensed as government spies, and redesigned the flute as a club for self-defense (pp. 168–169).
Check: Tsukitani mentions neither (Tokita 151–152). Malm’s account rests on Nakatsuka’s research, which he calls incomplete (p. 168 n. 3). Treat as unconfirmed.
Form of honkyoku. Malm: a rondo-like A–B–A–C–A (p. 174).
Check: Tsukitani: four patterns, binary, arch, four-part and dan (Tokita 158). Malm warns his view rests on a fraction of the repertoire (p. 173). Not an error, a narrower sample.

Miki 2008

Kakushin覚心 as origin. Miki: Kakushin is said to have brought the modern shakuhachi and its repertoire, marking the start of the Fuke普化 style (p. 35).
Check: Tsukitani: evidence for the 1254 story is lacking; the Fuke sect was established only in the mid-seventeenth century (Tokita 146, 151). Miki hedges with “it is said”.
Komi or komibuki?. Miki: komi is a staccato made by stopping the airflow, a general technique (p. 44).
Check: Tsukitani: komibuki is diaphragm bursts, peculiar to Nezasa-ha Kinpū-ryū根笹派錦風流 (Tokita 168). These may be different techniques with similar names; not resolved here.
