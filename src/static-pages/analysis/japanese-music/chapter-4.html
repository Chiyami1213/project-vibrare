1Nō and Kyōgen as Music

1.1Utai and Hayashi

Scholars call nō能 and kyōgen狂言 together nōgaku能楽, and musicologists speak of “the music of nō”. Performers rarely say ongaku音楽 (music). The arts center on a literary text, with music as a background, and they are not felt as entertainment the way popular music is Tokita 127.

Utai and Hayashi (Fujita)
Performers use two words. utai謡 is the performed text: once only the metrical, song-like parts, now every part including speech, so the libretto is the utaibon謡本, “book of utai”. hayashi囃子 is the instrumental ensemble, from hayasu囃す, “to encourage”: it makes a central part stand out, including the cries of players at the edge of the action.
(1.1)

Tokita 127–128. There is no single word covering both Tokita 127.

Malm describes nō as a Gesamtkunstwerk of equal arts, none more important than the others; taken out of context, its music can seem “dull or a trifle undernourishing”, like one dish from a fine dinner Malm 119.

1.2From Sarugaku to Nō

Malm traces nō to popular, not courtly, entertainments Malm 120–122:

AncestorOrigin (Malm)
Sarugaku猿楽“monkey music”: a shrine or temple pantomime that became comic popular theater
Sangaku散楽Chinese acrobatics; later replaced by comic plays, kyōgen (“mad words”)
Dengaku田楽“field music”: rice-planting dances taken into the court

Malm 120, 122. Other medieval forms include jushi, ennen延年 and furyū風流; Mibu壬生 kyōgen pantomimes in Kyoto keep something of that early spirit Malm 120–121.

The word nō means “accomplishment” or “ability” and was a suffix to dengaku and sarugaku. Nō and kyōgen became a unified art about six hundred years ago, when Zeami世阿弥 and his troupe won the patronage of the shogunate.
(1.2)

Tokita 128. Malm: Kan’ami観阿弥 (1333–84) and Zeami brought a more serious manner to sarugaku-no-nō under the shogun Yoshimitsu足利義満 and borrowed heavily from Buddhist chant Malm 122–123. Zeami’s dates differ between the books: see Where the Sources Differ.

1.3From Theater to Hobby to Ritual

  • Early sixteenth century. Nō began to be enjoyed as private amateur music and dance. Professionals who sang at banquets taught utai and dance (shimai仕舞) to warrior patrons; Fujita compares it to golf today Tokita 128.
  • Seventeenth century. Amateur utai spread to merchants; printed librettos sold all over the country and served as a sung encyclopedia and a school text. Nō became the shogunate’s ceremonial art, actors became its employees, and its status rose over shamisen三味線 music and kabuki Tokita 129.
  • Today performances feel almost like religious practice, and practitioners number less than a tenth of a century ago Tokita 129.

G Fujita traces several musical habits to this history: drummers who follow the singer (8.4), the growing chorus (6.1), and the value placed on unchanged transmission (9.3) Tokita 140, 136, 142.

2The Play and Its Program

2.1Five Kinds of Play

By the seventeenth century a program included one play of each of five kinds Malm 124:

KindMain Character
kami nō神能 (or waki nō脇能)a god or goddess; congratulatory
shura nō修羅能a warrior of old
kazura nō鬘能 (“wig” play)a woman
zatsu nō雑能miscellany: madness, obsession, “present-day things”
kiri nō切能a supernatural figure; closing play

Malm 124–125. A standard day was five nō能 and three kyōgen狂言; today the number varies Malm 124–125. Fujita’s modern recital program mixes nō, shimai仕舞 and kyōgen Tokita 141.

The shiteシテ is the principal actor around whose actions and emotions the play is built; the wakiワキ is the second actor and a foil; companions are tsureツレ Malm 125.

2.2Jo-Ha-Kyū in Five Dan

Five Dan in Two Acts (Malm)
Malm’s outline of a two-act play: Act I: jo序 = 1st dan段, the waki enters; ha破 = the shite enters, the two talk; kyū急 = the high point, the shite may dance and exits. A kyōgen interlude follows. Act II repeats the shape: the waki waits, the shite returns, often as a new character, and the act ends in a dance and a closing chorus.
(2.1)

Malm 125–126, following Yokomichi and Omote. Malm warns that plays vary as much as Bach fugues do from the textbook form Malm 126.

G Fujita uses dan for something much smaller, a recurring frame of a few units (4.3). Keep the two meanings apart; see Where the Sources Differ.

2.3A Play, Step by Step

Malm follows the music of the usual stage actions Malm 123–128:

  1. Warm-up. Behind the curtain the flute plays Shirabe調べ. The musicians enter in fixed order: flute, shoulder drum, hip drum, stick drum. The chorus (ji地) enters from a small door at the right.
  2. Waki entrance along the bridgeway (hashigakari橋掛り) to flute music and perhaps a shidai次第 song; a self-introduction (nanori名ノリ); a traveling song (michiyuki道行).
  3. Shite entrance, often with an issei一声, a recitative sashiサシ, and songs: sageuta下ゲ歌 (low) and ageuta上ゲ歌 (high).
  4. Dialogue: mondō問答 (speech) or rongi論義 (more melodic), and laments (kudoki口説).
  5. High point leading to the kuseクセ; the shite may exit. A kyōgen or an instrumental interlude follows.
  6. Act II: speech, perhaps a waiting song (machiutai待謡), the shite’s return, a kuriクリ and kuse.
  7. Dance (mai舞), then a short poem and a choral finale (kiri切).

G Malm compares nō to the tea ceremony, which grew up at the same time: the point is the atmosphere and objects presented, not plot. He notes that historical research suggests the slow pace was not part of the original productions Malm 128.

3Utai: Voice and Scale

3.1The Utai Voice

The utai謡 voice is extremely tense with a wide vibrato and a characteristic rising glissando. Later genres such as nagauta長唄 and gidayū-bushi義太夫節 “quote” it.
(3.1)

Tokita 129. Malm: the voice comes from the abdomen, with graces and vibrato added; the pronunciation is an abstraction of old styles, like reading Chaucer in the original Malm 129. Singing comes from Buddhist chant and keeps its solemnity Malm 129.

3.2Two Scales

Yowagin and Tsuyogin
R yowagin弱吟 (soft style) uses defined pitches. Its nuclear tones ge下 (low), chū中 (middle) and jō上 (high) lie a fourth apart; passing tones are chū-uki (a tone above chū), jō-uki (a tone above jō) and kuriクリ (a minor third above jō, the highest). tsuyogin強吟 (strong style) concentrates on one nuclear tone within a minor third, blurred by wide vibrato and a slight rise in pitch.
(3.2)

Tokita 129–130. Pitch is entirely relative Tokita 129.

Malm adds that raised (uki浮) and lowered (osae抑) forms of the centers tend to return to them, like upper and lower leading tones, and that yowagin seems the older style Malm 130. Fujita dates the split to about three hundred years ago: tsuyogin once had an octave range like yowagin Tokita 129.

3.3Choosing a Style

Which Style When
G Yowagin suits romance, beauty and melancholy, often female characters and the poet-warriors of the defeated Heike平家; tsuyogin suits auspicious, congratulatory texts and battle, often male characters and the victorious Genji源氏.
(3.3)

Tokita 130. Malm: yowagin for lyric scenes, tsuyogin for masculine and warlike ones Malm 130.

G Fujita’s hypothesis: when nō能 became Edo江戸 ritual music, a stronger, more austere style was needed to mark celebration, and that became tsuyogin Tokita 130.

4Utai: Units and Patterns

4.1Units of Utai

The musical analysis of nō能 is the most advanced of any Japanese genre, built by Yamazaki Gakudō (1915), Miyake Kōichi (1939) and Yokomichi Mario横道萬里雄 (1986), whose system became a model for other genres Tokita 130. Units of utai謡 (Yokomichi’s shōdan小段) divide as follows:

DeliveryRhythmExample Units
kotoba詞 (spoken)no meter; no hayashi囃子katari語, nanori名ノリ, mondō問答
fushi節 (sung)hyōshi-ai拍子合: congruent, in the 8-beat barnorijiノリ地, ageuta上ゲ歌, sageuta下ゲ歌, kuseクセ, rongi論義, shidai次第
fushi (sung), melismatichyōshi-awazu拍子不合: non-congruentissei一声, waka和歌, kuriクリ
fushi (sung), syllabichyōshi-awazu: non-congruentsashiサシ, kudoki口説

Tokita Table 6.1 Tokita 131. One eight-beat bar is a kusari鎖. The kuse came from the popular kusemai曲舞 at the end of the fourteenth century Tokita 131.

Congruent and Non-Congruent
R In congruent rhythm (hyōshi-ai) each sound has a fixed pitch and duration within the eight-beat bar. In non-congruent rhythm (hyōshi-awazu) the voice is not tied to the drums’ bar, but pitch and approximate duration are still fixed. Kotoba speech is stylized and austere, not realistic as in kabuki or bunraku文楽.
(4.1)

Tokita 128 n. 3, 131. “Congruent rhythm” is Hoff and Flindt’s translation of Yokomichi’s hyōshi-ai, “matching rhythm”. Malm uses the same pair (hyōshi au拍子合, hyōshi awazu拍子不合) for whether drums and voice match, and splits the unmatched kind into a steady noribyōshi乗拍子 and a deliberately uneven sashibyōshi指拍子 Malm 142.

4.2Reading an Utaibon

Every sung mora in the libretto has a neume-like gomaten胡麻点 to its right: a horizontal one for the same pitch, a slanted one for direction, more complex ones for ornaments such as mawashi. Spoken text has none. The book also marks congruence, some drum cues, occasional pitch names and the unit’s name.
(4.2)

Tokita 131. The drum patterns are not in the libretto, which matters for 8.4 Tokita 140. Malm stresses how much must be learned before the notation can be sung correctly Malm 131–132.

4.3Frames of Units

The Frame (Yokomichi)
R Most plays repeat a fixed frame, which Yokomichi calls a dan段: (introductory song) → speech or sung narrative → song. In an entrance frame: an introductory song such as shidai (congruent) → sashi (non-congruent, syllabic, like recitative) → sageuta → ageuta (both congruent). The last unit of a frame is always congruent.
(4.3)

Tokita 131, 133–134. Fujita notes that nō shares this alternation of narrative and song with other narrative arts Tokita 133; compare the heike平家 dan in Chapter 3.

4.4Melodic Patterns

A unit is made from a small set of melodic patterns with a shared syntax, so after one play the others sound familiar. A pattern goes on for a while, then gives way to the next Tokita 133. The kuse of Hagoromo runs:

The Kuse of Hagoromo羽衣
ge-chū → jō-chū → ge-chū → jō上 → kuri → jō-chū → ge-chū: low–middle, high–middle, back down, up to the top, and home again.
(4.4)

Tokita 133. The singer’s sense of the order also lets the dancer’s movements be anticipated, because the patterns are linked to the syntax of the dance Tokita 133.

5Rhythm: The Eight-Beat Bar

5.1Three Nori

Nori: Text per Bar
R Congruent utai謡 places text on the eight-beat bar of the hayashi囃子 by one of three noriノリ: hiranori平ノリ spreads 12 moras (a 7 + 5 line) over eight beats in syncopation; ōnori大ノリ puts 8 moras one per beat; chūnori中ノリ puts 16 moras two per beat.
(5.1)
NoriMoras per BarMoras per BeatTypical Use
hiranori (plain)12 (a 7 + 5 line)1.5every play: ageuta上ゲ歌, kuseクセ; syncopated
ōnori (large)81slow heavenly dances, but also fast battle scenes
chūnori (medium)162battle scenes; sounds fast even when slow

Tokita 134 Malm 131. Malm calls the eight-beat system yatsubyōshi八つ拍子 Malm 131. Playwrights must know these conventions, because nori are tied to the meter of the text Tokita 134.

G The scene link is loose: ōnori serves both slow heavenly dances and fast battle scenes Tokita 134.

5.2Theory and Practice

Malm separates jibyōshi地拍子, the theoretical “basic rhythm” of a textbook, from practice. His Figure 15 sets one line of Matsukaze in jibyōshi with the drum pattern mitsuji三ツ地, again with tsuzuke続ケ, and then transcribes a performance: the syllables sound evenly spaced with some slowing, but the singer’s counts are uneven, and a passage may have more than eight beats Malm 131–132.

G Like a chord progression in a harmony textbook, jibyōshi explains the system but rarely sounds like an actual piece Malm 131.

5.3What Changes Mean

Meaning Lies in the Switch (Fujita)
G In nō能 no melodic pattern stands for anything: the same pattern serves joy and sorrow. Meaning lies in the change from one style to another, of nori, of yowagin弱吟 and tsuyogin強吟, or of melodic pattern. For listeners who cannot follow every line, these changes mark the drama.
(5.2)

Tokita 135–136.

  • Funa Benkei: as the boat is about to leave and the lovers part, ōnori gives way to hiranori Tokita 135.
  • Ama: the diver’s descent is in yowagin, slower for her feelings; at the dragon king’s palace the voice turns tsuyogin and the rhythm chūnori as she seizes the jewel; on her return the yowagin and hiranori come back Tokita 135.
  • Hagoromo: as the angel flies over Ashitakayama愛鷹山, the kuriクリ pattern, the highest pitch, stresses taka, “high” Tokita 136.
  • Kyōgen狂言: breaking into song can erase a quarrel. In Kagyū (Snail) cheat and victim end up singing and dancing together Tokita 136.

Malm describes kyōgen as mostly heightened speech, with some nō-style songs, parodies, short songs (kouta小唄) and dances; any flute and drum parts are simplified nō music Malm 124.

The kuse of Hagoromo opens with the six lines at right, sung to the melodic pattern ge-chū. Decide whether the kuse is congruent or not, which nori fits a 7 + 5 line, how many moras per beat that gives, roughly how many eight-beat bars the six lines fill, and where in the whole kuse the melody peaks.

LineMoras
Harugasumi5

Opening of the kuse of Hagoromo (Tokita 133).

  1. Assumptions. Assumptions: 8-beat bar; nori = moras per bar.
  2. Unit Type. The kuse is a congruent (hyōshi-ai) unit.
  3. Nori. Hiranori: one 7 + 5 line = one bar; 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5 moras per beat.
  4. Bars. 59 moras ÷ 12 ≈ 4.9 bars ≈ 40 beats (an estimate; short lines are stretched or combined in practice).
  5. Contour. Pattern path ge-chū → jō-chū → ge-chū → jō → kuri (peak) → jō-chū → ge-chū.

Answer
The kuse is a congruent unit in hiranori: each 7 + 5 line fills one eight-beat bar at 1.5 moras a beat, so the six opening lines (59 moras) fill about 5 bars. Its melody climbs through the patterns to kuri, the top of yowagin, and returns to ge-chū. Fujita’s point: none of these choices carries meaning alone; the changes between them do.
(E5.7)

6The Chorus

6.1The Jiutai

The units at the start of a frame are sung by individual characters; the song at the end is often sung by the jiutai地謡, the chorus seated at stage left. While it sings, the masked actor moves or dances in silence, like a puppet, and the chorus may switch from first to third person: “I am the ghost of …, he said, and disappeared” Tokita 136.

How the Chorus Grew (Fujita)
G In Zeami’s time the actors sang these lines with a peripheral chorus. In the seventeenth century, with utai謡 popular, a chorus always on stage became the custom and the word jiutai appeared; in the eighteenth it grew to as many as 25 singers; today it is normally eight. The chorus’s share of the text also grew, freeing the actor to dance.
(6.1)

Tokita 136.

G Fujita rejects two easy readings: the chorus is neither a mere stand-in for the main character nor a way to make the text louder. By moving the character’s lines into an impersonal voice, it transcends the character’s viewpoint Tokita 136. Malm adds that the chorus leader (jigashira地頭) controls the tempo by stretching sounds and may change the melody slightly, with the chorus following Malm 129.

7The Hayashi: Nōkan

7.1The Instrument

The nōkan能管 (or fue笛) is the only melodic instrument besides the voice. It looks like other Japanese flutes but is built differently Tokita 137:

The Nōkan’s Throat
A tube called the nodo喉 (“throat”), set between the mouth hole and the first finger hole, narrows the bore, so the upper register lies less than an octave above the lower and matches no Japanese scale. The flute has no standard length and no absolute pitch, and its melody has no pitch relation to the utai謡.
(7.1)

Tokita 137. Malm: overblowing gives intervals from an octave down to a minor seventh; he wonders whether this links to the seventh between the high and low tones of the vocal system Malm 134. Miki says “some” nōkan have the nodo Miki 25.

  • Construction. Bamboo split into strips and turned inside out, then bound with bark and lacquered; red lacquer inside today makes the turning less necessary Malm 133 Miki 25.
  • Size. About 34 cm long, but the sounding tube is only 29 cm: the flute is solid from the mouth hole to the closed end Malm 133.
  • Fingering. Seven holes, covered with the middle joints of the fingers, which helps the half-holed sound Malm 133; the hole names run opposite to the ryūteki龍笛’s Miki 25.

Miki tells composers that pitches vary from flute to flute and that Western notation shows only approximations; if a melody matters most, choose another flute such as the shinobue篠笛 Miki 26. Its special high motive hishigiヒシギ, made with very forceful breath, accompanies the entrance of ghosts and spirits Miki 27.

7.2Contour, Not Pitch

Heard by Contour (Fujita)
R Only the central tones of the nōkan, named banshiki and ōshiki after gagaku雅楽, are heard as scale degrees; they also end each pattern. For the rest, what counts is direction, higher or lower than the last note: a flute pattern is recognized by contour. Its shōga唱歌 encodes that contour in its vowels, from high to low i, a, o, u.
(7.2)

Tokita 137. So hyo-i rises, and hi-u-i-ya-ro goes down, up, down, down Tokita 137.

A wide mouth hole allows more than two tones per finger position, with slides made by angle and gradual uncovering Tokita 137. In lessons the teacher sings the shōga, passing on breath strength and phrasing as well as contour; an upward slide is more a change to stronger breath than a rise in pitch Tokita 138. G Because each flute differs, teachers enforce fingerings strictly but say little about the resulting pitch Tokita 137.

7.3Two Ways to Play

Ashiraiアシライ: the flute plays with the utai but in its own rhythm and melody, with no pitch relation; what matters is timbre, which can suggest space or emotion. It goes with non-congruent rhythm. Awase-buki合せ吹: the flute plays with the drums, without utai, in the eight-beat meter; the standard for dances (maigoto舞事).
(7.3)

Tokita 138. Malm lists three functions: signaling structural moments (often with the piercing top note), adding atmosphere, and giving melody to entrances and dances Malm 134–135.

The basic dance is chū no mai中之舞; jo no mai序之舞, otoko-mai男舞 and kami-mai神舞 are variations of it, while gaku楽, kakko羯鼓 and kagura神楽 point to other arts Tokita 138. Malm finds most dance music built on four eight-beat patterns, ryo呂, ryo no chū, kan甲 and kan no chū, framed by special patterns that open and close each section Malm 135–136.

8.2Kakegoe, Komi and Mitsuji

Calls and the Silent Komi
R Drummers’ shouts, kakegoe掛け声 (yo, ho, iya), cue the position in the eight-beat bar. Before a shout comes the silent komiコミ: the player silently says tsu or hm, tenses the abdomen and grips the cords. The komi is what keeps the drums together with the utai謡.
(8.1)

Tokita 138. A pattern is wrong unless strokes and calls come in the order of the player’s own school; mitsuji三ツ地 may be called “yo, ho, ho” or “ho, yo, ho” Malm 143.

The commonest pattern, mitsuji (“three points”): the ōtsuzumi plays don on the off-beat of 8, calls yō for 1.5 beats, takes the komi at 2, calls hō and plays a loud chon on 3; the kotsuzumi then takes over with a komi on 4, yō, po, and so on Tokita 138.

Mitsuji appears dozens of times in one play and is one of over a hundred patterns, played in a fixed order but repeated or dropped to fit the tempo Tokita 139. Malm counts about two hundred per instrument and notes that professional scores show only pattern names beside the text Malm 144.

8.3Cycles in Chū no Mai

Chū no Mai
In chū no mai中之舞 each instrument cycles its own phrases: flute four, ōtsuzumi one, kotsuzumi two. Their cycles start at different points: ōtsuzumi on beat 8, kotsuzumi on beat 1, taiko and flute on beat 2, giving a chain of easy cues. The dancer signals the end (fan or stamp); taiko, ōtsuzumi, kotsuzumi and flute pick it up in that order.
(8.2)

Tokita 139–140. If the dancer’s cue is late, the music repeats until it comes Tokita 140.

8.4Utai Is King

Utai Is the King (Fujita)
G “Utai is the king, hayashi囃子 the subject.” The libretto shows no drum patterns, and many amateur singers do not know there is an eight-beat bar at all, singing even congruent units as if there were no beat. Drummers therefore memorize which mora falls on the komi and play mitsuji elastically, dropping the “empty” half-beats before beats 4 and 6.
(8.3)

Tokita 140–141 and Table 6.3. In Table 6.3 the syllables o-mo-shi-ro-ya-a-me-na-ra-de from Hagoromo are matched to the calls and strokes.

G Fujita links this to history: since the mid-fifteenth century, nobles and warriors sang as soloists with hired professionals, who had to cover the amateurs’ rhythmic slips Tokita 140. Malm describes a constant process of adjustment among chorus, hayashi and actors, “barely visible beneath the placid surface” Malm 129.

9.2Amateurs and Children

Amateurs are patrons and sometimes more gifted than professionals, though the iemoto家元 hierarchy assumes otherwise. At an amateur recital more than ten professionals may support one amateur Tokita 141–142. Children play special roles (kokata子方), such as Yoshitsune義経 in Funa Benkei, and sometimes lead roles with adults covering their mistakes Tokita 142.

G Fujita calls this mix of unequal skills on one stage “authentic tradition”, and the likely source of the drummers’ elastic practice (8.4) Tokita 142.

9.3Iemoto and Guilds

How Nō Is Handed On (Fujita)
G Nō is passed on in the iemoto system: separate schools (ryū流) for the shite role and for each instrument, one teacher for life, certificates for fees. Its conventions: do not theorize (“If you explain things theoretically, your art will not progress”); do not interpret the notation in detail; perform from memory; and keep secret teachings (hiden秘伝), which also fund the system.
(9.1)

Tokita 142–143. Old librettos are therefore of little use in lessons, though they record past performance Tokita 142. The basic aim is to pass the art on unchanged Tokita 142.

9.4Flourishing and Declining

  • New work: experimental plays, guest performers from other genres, plays in other languages, and revivals of long-unperformed plays; “musically there is little new” Tokita 143.
  • Status: the National Nō Theatre (1983), Japan’s first UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage listing (2001), and broadcasts on national holidays Tokita 143.
  • Image: archaic, elitist and boring to many, especially the young; kyōgen fares better. Fujita asks for socio-musicological research on why many ordinary people have disliked nō for centuries Tokita 143–144.

G Fujita frames research as poiesis (how Zeami世阿弥 and others made musical form, as in Yokomichi’s unit analysis) and praxis (how the music is kept, handed on and received) Tokita 144.

Where the Sources Differ

Errors and disagreements found while cross-reading, grouped by source, with the check that exposed each one. The guide text uses the form named in each check.

Malm 2000

Zeami’s dates. Malm: Zeami世阿弥 Motokiyo 1363–1444 (p. 122).
Check: Fujita: 1364–1443 (Tokita 128), the dates Chapter 1 also follows. Both pairs appear in the literature.
Two meanings of dan. Malm: a play has five dan in a jo-ha-kyū frame across two acts (pp. 125–126).
Check: Fujita, after Yokomichi: a dan is a small recurring frame of introductory song, narrative and song (Tokita 131–133). Not an error, but the same word names units of very different size; the guide keeps them apart (2.2, 4.3).
Is the kuse a dance?. Malm describes the high point as leading “to dance (kuse)” (p. 127).
Check: Fujita classes the kuse as a congruent sung unit taken from kusemai (Tokita 131), central in hiranori (Tokita 134). The kuse may be danced or sung seated; the guide treats it as a sung unit.
Where the paper patch goes. Malm: the chōshigami papers go on the outside of the kotsuzumi’s rear skin (p. 138).
Check: Miki: on the front membrane (p. 158). Not resolved here.
Kotsuzumi ancestry. Malm: the kotsuzumi is related to the court san no tsuzumi (p. 137).
Check: Miki: it grew from the ikko, the smallest gigaku yōko; the san-no-tsuzumi is a separate survivor in komagaku (pp. 157, 167). Malm’s “related” may mean only family resemblance.

Miki 2008

Is the nodo standard?. Miki: “Some nōkan have” the nodo (p. 25).
Check: Fujita and Malm present the nodo as the defining feature of the nōkan (Tokita 137; Malm 134). The guide follows them.
How long an ōtsuzumi head lasts. Miki: heated heads are usable only “a few times” (p. 165).
Check: Malm: professionals retire a set after ten performances (p. 139). Practice probably varies; both agree heating shortens skin life.
